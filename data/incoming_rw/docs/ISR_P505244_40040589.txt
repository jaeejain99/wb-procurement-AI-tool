3. KEY ISSUES & STATUS


 3.1 Implementation Status and Key Decisions

   Significant Progress in Key Reform Areas

   The team noted significant progress across most areas of the reform program. Reforms under Pillar A, designed to create
   a level playing field for private sector participation, trade, and contestability, are advancing.

        •       SOE Reform (Prior Action #1): The government continues to make progress in addressing the dominance of
                State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs). The Ministry of Finance and Economic Planning (MINECOFIN) submitted a list of
                SOEs for privatization to the Cabinet, which approved a new resolution on streamlining the public investment

               and design of a digital dashboard to monitor SOE performance —the second indicator for this reform— will be

       •       Competition and Consumer Protection (PA#2): The Rwanda Inspectorate, Competition and Consumer Protection
               Authority (RICA) has decided on 10 competition investigations or merger reviews, progressing toward a target of
               18 by December 2025.
       •       Insolvency Law (PA#3): The number of initiated insolvency procedures for Micro and Small Enterprises has
               increased to 12 from a baseline of 5, with a target of 15 by December 2025. The MSE Insolvency Law is expected
               to be gazetted by the end of August 2025.
       •       Financial Inclusion and Risk Management (PA#4): The share of banks with guidelines aligned to BNR’s financial
               consumer protection regulations has surpassed the original 85 percent target, reaching 100 percent as of August
               2025. While all commercial banks are compliant, 98 percent of regulations stated in the law have been included.
               The second results indicator for this reform was initially designed to track both the share of women and of
               female-owned businesses with access to credit; however, the 18 percent baseline reflects only the share of
               Rwandan women with access to formal credit. This figure has since risen to 20 percent, according to the latest
               Finscope Gender Thematic Report (compared to 29 percent for men). The December target of 27.5 percent is
               unlikely to be met, primarily due to limited collateral and non-fulfillment of FSP lending requirements, which
               remain the key binding constraints.
       •       Trade Liberalization (PA#5): The main focus of this PA was the removal of 3 barriers: lifting of the reciprocity
               requirement linked to the licensing of architects and engineers under Law governing the professions of engineers
               and architects (2 restrictions) and lifting a subsidiary requirement for foreign commercial banks seeking to
               provide services in Rwanda (3rd restriction). This has come into effect, and the number of African Continental
               Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) service sectors with major trade restrictions has achieved the target of 9 for 2025.

  Reforms under Pillar B, which are designed to mobilize investment, particularly green investment, also show substantial
  progress.
      • RDB Law (PA#6): The development of new guidelines for the Rwanda Development Board’s dispute mitigation
         division are aimed to enhance the handling of investor grievances, with a particular focus on green investments.
         The nonfunctional CRM has hindered the monitoring of actual investment retained amounts. The World Bank
         team and RDB are currently exploring alternative solutions.
      • Nature Reserves (PA#7): As of August 2025, six proposals to establish privately owned nature reserves have been
         received by RDB and are under evaluation. Reviews are proceeding in line with the new Ministerial Orders that
         set out the modalities, requirements, and stages for licensing.
      • Climate Finance (PA#8): Rwanda’s pioneering role in mobilizing climate investment is reflected in the successful
         implementation of this reform. The government’s launch of the Climate and Nature Finance Strategy (CNFS) in
         2024 and commitment to its implementation has unlocked US$576 million in climate finance, well above the
         $400 million target. Most of these resources support climate adaptation investments. This achievement reflects
         Rwanda’s strategic focus on resilience and its growing ability to leverage public and private finance to deliver its
         climate agenda.
      • Sustainable Procurement (PA#9): The percentage value of contracts signed online stands at 58.46% as of June
         30, 2025. The target of 95% by December 2025 seems unrealistic. Regarding the pilot products to be
         implemented through the SPP framework, the target is on track. By end of this year at least 3 products are on

  Beyond the Numbers: Broader Impact and Collaboration

  These tangible results underscore the strong momentum of the DPF's development objective, but the impact of this
  reform series extends far beyond simple metrics. For example, the policy dialogue and implementation support for SOE
  reform (PA#1) have laid crucial groundwork for enhanced private sector participation. This work has fostered a strong,
  collaborative relationship with government counterparts, who have now requested continued support for implementing
  these legal frameworks.

  This collaboration has created a foundation for further engagement with the International Finance Corporation (IFC). The
  IFC is now providing technical assistance to the Ministry of Finance to enhance corporate governance and facilitate the
  privatization of SOEs, directly helping to crowd in private investment. This integrated approach, which leverages both
  World Bank and IFC expertise, is also informing the development of a comprehensive SOE oversight system.

  Similarly, the DPF’s policy dialogue and analytical work, including a workshop on spatial-based policies, have set the stage
  for continued engagement and the development of future reforms aimed at promoting green industrialization,
  particularly in Special Economic Zones (SEZs). These additional reforms are now being integrated into the government's
  new Industrial Policy (2024–2034), and the government has formally requested assistance with their implementation.
  This programmatic DPF approach has thus proven effective in providing high-level policy support that leads to concrete,
  lasting legal frameworks and sets the direction for future reforms beyond the DPF series itself.
