   I.    PROGRAM CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES

  A. Context at Appraisal

 Overall Context
 1.       The Rwanda Energy Sector Development Policy Financing was designed in 2017 as a programmatic
 series of three consecutive Development Policy Operations (DPOs) to support the Government’s efforts
 at containing the fiscal impact of the electricity sector while simultaneously improving operational
 efficiency, reliability, affordability, and accountability of services. On May 1, 2020, the World Bank
 approved a supplemental DPO to the Third Rwanda Energy Sector Development Policy Operation
 (P169040). The purpose of the supplemental financing was to support the Government of Rwanda (GoR)
 in preparing a timely fiscal response to the economic shock from the COVID-19 pandemic, to put in place
 platforms for policy development and coordination of prevention and preparedness and safeguard the
 reform agenda supported by the DPO series.

 2.      This Implementation Completion and Results Report (ICR) provides an evaluation of all the DPOs
 in the series—the First Rwanda Programmatic Energy Sector Development Policy Operation (P162671),
 the Second Rwanda Energy Sector Development Policy Operation (P166458), the Third Rwanda Energy
 Sector Development Policy Operation (P169040), and the Rwanda Supplemental Energy Sector
 Development Policy Financing (P173882).

 Macroeconomic Context

 3.       To support its vision of transforming Rwanda into a middle-income country by 2020, the GoR set
 ambitious targets for electricity generation capacity expansion and access to electricity as part of its
 Economic Development and Poverty Reduction Strategy II (EDPRS II) for FY2013/14–FY2017/18. EDPRS II,
 part of a series of such strategic plans, was expected to achieve annual gross domestic product (GDP)
 growth rates of 11.5 percent and reduce poverty from 39 percent in 2014 to less than 30 percent in 2018.
 The follow-up strategic plan, the National Strategy for Transformation (NST1) (2017/18–2023/24), revised
 the target dates for lower- and upper-middle-income-country status to 2035 and 2050, respectively. Like
 EDPRS II, NST1 assigned energy access as central to Rwanda’s pursuit of more inclusive growth and
 development with universal access to electricity by 2024 as one of the core targets.

 4.      Before the DPO series, Rwanda’s economy had grown at an average rate of 7.8 percent per year
 during 2006–2015 and slowed down to 6.0 percent and 4.0 percent in 2016 and 2017, respectively (table
 1), due to declining investment levels and other temporary factors, such as drought and low commodity
 prices for Rwanda’s exports. GDP growth accelerated in 2018–2019 and reached 9.4 percent in 2019,
 mostly driven by large public investments for implementation of NST1. Public debt grew rapidly as well—
 more than tripling relative to GDP since 2010—raising concerns about its long-term sustainability with
 limited space to absorb shocks. To achieve its objective of medium-term fiscal sustainability, Rwanda
 committed to further strengthening its debt management capacity and identifying credible revenue and
 spending measures for a growth-friendly fiscal consolidation plan with a view to reaching the debt anchor
 of 65 percent of GDP. The main factors driving the fiscal consolidation plan include (a) the phasing out of
 COVID-19 and SDR-financed spending (3.3 percentage points of GDP), (b) domestic resource mobilization
 (1.5 percentage points of GDP), and (c) expenditure controls and efficiency improvement measures to be

 identified with the support of the World Bank Public Expenditure Review and the International Monetary

 5.        The ongoing IMF Policy Coordination Instrument program was approved by the IMF Executive
 Board in June 2019. The program supports implementation of NST1 through four main pillars: (a) creating

 transparency, including the identification and management of potential government liabilities (‘fiscal
 risks’); (c) regaining momentum on domestic revenue mobilization; and (d) supporting implementation of
 the forward-looking monetary policy framework of the National Bank of Rwanda that uses interest rates
 to control policy impulses. On January 11, 2022, the IMF’s Executive Board concluded the fifth review of
 the program, noting that program performance remains broadly satisfactory.

 6.        Faced with a slowdown in GDP growth and increasing external imbalances due to decreasing
 foreign grants and weak export performance, the GoR exercised fiscal restraint, resulting in a reduction
 in the fiscal deficit from 5.0 percent in 2014/15 to 3.4 percent in 2015/16. Fiscal transfers to the electricity
 sector decreased from 2.5 percent of GDP in 2013/14 to an estimated 1.4 percent in 2016/17.

 7.       However, to achieve its electricity generation and access targets under EDPRS II and NST1, budget
 transfers to the sector (under a business-as-usual scenario) were projected to increase to 4.5 percent by
 2020. The DPO series was conceived to support the GoR in implementing a series of reforms aimed at
 containing the fiscal impact of budget transfers to the sector while simultaneously ensuring adequate
 electricity supply to meet the GoR’s targets for economic growth and access to electricity.

 Sector/Structural Context
 8.      In just over a decade before the start of the DPO series, in 2017, Rwanda achieved considerable
 progress in developing its energy sector. Key sector indicators on access to electricity; installed generation
 capacity; private sector participation; and quality, reliability, and efficiency of supply had all improved
 substantially. Figure 1 shows the rapid growth in electricity supply and access rates during 2005 – 2021.
 The country had also undertaken several significant energy sector reforms, especially the structural
 reforms since 2013.

 9.      Electrification expansion. The country’s electrification rate was one of the fastest in the world, 1
 with access to electricity increasing from 6.0 percent in 2008 to 40.7 percent in 2017. The share of grid-
 connected households increased from 6.0 percent to 29.7 percent while that of off-grid households
 increased from 0.0 percent to 11.0 percent during the same period. Virtually all hospitals and more than
 90 percent of health centers and administrative offices were connected to electricity by 2017, while the
 connection rate for schools was about 70 percent. Going forward, Rwanda placed electricity access

 expansion at the center of its development strategy—NST1 for 2017–24 which was under preparation at
 the inception of the DPO series—aimed at achieving upper-middle-income and high-income status by
 2035 and 2050, respectively. Innovative features of the country’s approach to electricity access expansion
 included unprecedented high off-grid connection targets (48 percent under NST1) in recognition of the
 potential role of off-grid affordable solutions for electrification and the application of cutting-edge
 planning models and modern geospatial tools to find cost-effective ways of electrification. 2 Rwanda was
 one of the first countries to prepare and implement a nationwide electrification program combining grid
 and off-grid means, based on a systematic and least-cost plan aided by geographic information system
 (GIS) mapping techniques that combine technical, economic, demographic, and demand and supply data.

 10.    Increases in power supply. Electricity generation capacity increased from 55 MW in 2009 to 213
 MW in 2017. The total installed generation capacity in 2017 comprised hydro (45 percent), heavy fuel oil
 and diesel (27 percent), peat (7 percent), solar (4 percent), lake methane (14 percent), and imports (3
 percent). The increase in generation capacity was largely enabled by the participation of independent
 power producers (IPPs) who owned about 52 percent of the total capacity by 2017.

 11.      Improvements in efficiency and quality of supply. Rwanda took some significant steps to address
 operational performance issues, including poor quality and reliability of electricity services, high levels of
 system losses, and management of corporate resources. Following the sector restructuring in 2014, a
 business plan was prepared for the Energy Utility Corporation Limited (EUCL) comprising state-of-the-art
 tools (information systems and advanced metering technologies) to support efficient, transparent, and
 accountable execution of operations in all areas. The business plan provided a basis for the World Bank
 to support the introduction of a set of management information systems comprising enterprise resource
 planning, commercial management system, incident recording and management system, and a GIS for
 corporate databases under the Rwanda Electricity Sector Strengthening Project (RESSP, P150634). A
 revenue protection program was prepared and implemented to support enhancement of revenue
 collection and reduction of commercial losses. The use of modern tools for data collection facilitated the
 reduction in system losses from above 25 percent to less than 20 percent by FY2017/18.

 12.     Energy sector reforms. Rwanda implemented energy sector reforms, particularly since 2013
 when deeper restructuring of the power sector commenced. The objectives of the restructuring reforms
 were to streamline operations, increase governance and accountability, and create an off-taker entity for
 private sector produced power. The reforms entailed separation of electricity functions from the former
 combined water and electricity utility (Electricity and Water Sanitation Authority). The electricity functions
 were moved to the Rwanda Energy Group (REG) which was assigned the responsibility for sector
 operations from planning 3 and infrastructure development to operation of the system. Within REG, non-
 revenue- and revenue-generating functions were placed in two separate subsidiaries—the Energy
 Development Corporation Limited (EDCL) and the EUCL, respectively. The responsibility for planning and
 development of new generation capacity, expansion of the transmission system, and distribution rollout
 to unelectrified areas was assigned to EDCL. EUCL was mandated to operate the existing public
 generation, transmission, distribution and retail services, and the expansion of the distribution network
 within the electrified areas. EUCL also became the offtaker for privately generated power. Regulation of

 13.      While substantial progress in the development of the sector was achieved, several key challenges
 remained at the inception of the DPO series, including high electricity costs, limited affordability,
 efficiency and reliability of electricity services, and a significant gap between the rate of access to
 electricity and the EDPRS II target of 70 percent by 2018 (revised to 100 percent by 2024 under NST1
 which was then under preparation). These issues, if left unaddressed, would adversely affect the
 achievement of EDPRS II and/or cause huge unsustainable fiscal costs.

 14.     High cost of electricity. The average cost of electricity in 2016/17 was US$0.32 per kWh compared
 to an average tariff of US$0.20 per kWh, resulting in an average revenue-average cost (ARCOS) gap of
 US$0.12 per kWh. Several reasons accounted for the large ARCOS gap, including the country’s lack of low-
 cost energy resources, the limited international power trade which could have enabled Rwanda to source
 cheaper power from neighboring countries, the sizable proportion of diesel in the energy generation mix
 (about 18 percent), absence of efficient least-cost expansion planning tools, and the use of non-
 competitive methods of procurement for some of the electricity capacity investments.

 18.       The DPO was designed to build on these earlier and ongoing projects to consolidate and scale up
 power supply and access expansion using budget financing and with a stronger focus on improving
 governance, accountability, results, institutional capacity strengthening, and long-term sector
 sustainability. A financially strong power sector capable of attracting private capital for development of
 power infrastructure required to support the country’s development and poverty reduction program
 (NST1) was essential. The approach was to promote sector financial sustainability by proactively managing
 fiscal risks to avoid large fiscal imbalances that were expected to arise under a business-as-usual scenario
 for sector growth.

 Rationale for Bank Involvement

 19.      EDPRS II (2013/14–2017/18) and the subsequent NST1 (2017–2024) provide the overall context
 for the development of the power sector and the anchor for the DPO series. EDPRS II placed the power
 sector on top of the GoR’s development and poverty reduction program and assigned it specific capacity
 and access targets. To support the economy’s rapid transformation by meeting the demand for electricity,
 the generation capacity target was set at 563 MW by 2018. This target was revised to 556 MW under NST1
 and was to be met with the participation of private sector investments. To support EDPRS II’s development
 goal, the national electrification target was set at 70 percent by 2018 6 and revised to 100 percent by 2024.

 20.      As stated above, the World Bank had supported Rwanda in developing its energy sector for many
 years and was impressed by the country’s track record in implementing reforms. A DPO was considered
 the most appropriate instrument to support Rwanda in shifting from a largely investment-oriented
 strategy to one that embraced both investment and policy reforms. It was important for Rwanda to
 consolidate the gains thus far and increase the efficiency and effectiveness of the World Bank’s ongoing
 support on improving access to electricity and quality and reliability of supply. Development policy lending
 would provide the most suitable instrument by introducing a more results-oriented approach and
 accountability and governance framework for management of the sector. Further, a sector specific DPO
 was necessary to ensure focus to address the range and diversity of policy reforms that could not be
 realistically addressed through a multisector DPO.

 Original Program Development Objective(s) (PDO) (as approved)

 23.     The PDO of the parent DPO series (P162671, P166458, and P169040) was to enable fiscally
 sustainable expansion of electricity services in Rwanda. The DPO series was built around two pillars: (a)
 containing the fiscal impact of the electricity sector and (b) improving the operational efficiency,
 affordability, and accountability of electricity service. There were no changes in the PDO throughout
 program implementation but there were changes in outcome indicators which are described in Section B.

 Original Policy Areas/Pillars Supported by the Program (as approved)

 affordability, and accountability of electricity services. The program was designed around these two pillars
 with detailed policies under each pillar all contributing to the overall objective of enabling fiscally
 sustainable expansion of the electricity services in Rwanda.

 Pillar A: Contain the fiscal impact of the electricity sector

 25.      The purpose of this pillar was to support several steps leading to the approval by the Cabinet’s
 Economic Cluster of medium-term projections of fiscal transfers to the sector based on efficient revenue
 requirements (ERRs). The supported steps included (a) determination of revenue requirements based on
 cost-efficient investment and operations, (b) rationalization and adjustment of tariff categories to boost
 revenues while protecting affordability for the poorest households, and (c) joint adoption by MININFRA
 and Ministry of Economic Planning and Finance (MINECOFIN) and submission to the Economic Cluster of
 options for achieving fiscal sustainability and containing fiscal transfers to the sector. The pillar covered
 the determination of revenue requirements for use in preparing trajectories of medium-term fiscal
 transfers, but the reductions in electricity costs were to result from least-cost generation and access
 expansion and from improved operational efficiency actions supported by Pillar B. Thus, the actual
 containment of fiscal transfers was to result from actions under both pillars A and B.

 Pillar B: Improve the operational efficiency, affordability, and accountability of electricity service
         •     B1: Transition to least cost and low carbon energy mix
         •     B2: Increase access to affordable and reliable electricity
         •     B3: Improve accountability and transparency of REG
         •     B4: Improve operational efficiency and quality of electricity services

 26.      The purpose of this pillar was to support electricity cost reductions through adoption of least-cost
 investment planning; competitive procedures for new capacity additions; diversification of energy
 sources; enhancement of sector governance; and increase in the efficiency, affordability, reliability, and
 quality of services. Specifically, the pillar supported a wide range of actions aimed at facilitating (a) the
 institutionalization of least-cost power planning and competitive procurement procedures for generation
 and transmission investments; (b) technical, financing, regulatory, and investment approaches for the
 national electrification program, including in the off-grid space; (c) improved financial reporting based on
 International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) with financial statements audited by external auditors
 and published—improving transparency and the potential for REG to access commercial financing; and
 (d) improved operational performance and quality of service, including reductions in system losses and in
 power outages.

 27.      The policy actions under Pillar B would support both cost reductions and revenue enhancements
 that would optimize the sector’s revenue requirements, thereby helping contain the fiscal impacts of the
 sector in the medium term in line with the PDO.

 Macroeconomic Developments

 30.      Rwanda’s macroeconomic performance (table 1) during the period covered by the DPO program
 was broadly positive but later was hit by the COVID-19 pandemic. Real GDP annual growth remained high
 over 2017–2019, averaging about 7.3 percent, bolstered by trade and transport services as well as food
 production. Inflation was quite stable, reflecting prudent macroeconomic management and lower food
 and fuel prices. Fiscal revenue rose steadily due to improved tax administration and increased economic
 activity, while the Government kept expenditures in line with available resources. An increase in the
 overall fiscal deficit was recorded due to a decline in grants. In 2017–2019, the overall external position
 had improved, with the current account deficit averaging 10.5 percent of GDP down from 15.2 percent in
 2016. Foreign direct investment and capital inflows were important in financing it, and reserve coverage
 remained above 4.5 months of imports. The growth momentum was, however, interrupted in 2020 by the
 pandemic as the authorities put in place a nationwide lockdown to contain the spread of COVID-19 while
 international flow of goods and services was disrupted with significant spillovers to Rwanda’s broader
 economy. GDP fell by 3.4 percent in 2020, compared to an expansion of 8 percent anticipated before the
 COVID-19 outbreak. Domestic and global restrictions to contain the pandemic severely interrupted
 economic activities and depressed exports, private investment, and consumption. The current account
 deficit deteriorated in 2020, but by only 0.3 percentage points of GDP. The fiscal deficit widened to about
 9.5 percent of GDP in FY2019/20. Rwanda experienced revenue shortfall and higher spending needs for
 containing the infection and mitigating the lockdown’s social impact. Foreign borrowing, much of it
 concessional, more than financed the deficit. Public and publicly guaranteed debt is estimated to have
 increased from 57 percent of GDP in 2019 to 71 percent in 2020. Rwanda’s risk of external debt distress
 was downgraded from low to moderate in the 2020 IMF/World Bank debt sustainability analysis.

 31.     The economy surged in 2021, growing by about 11 percent. Gradually easing mobility restrictions
 have supported a broad-based rebound, stimulating private consumption, by increasing incomes amid the
 reopening of economic activities, and falling inflation. Household consumption made significant
 contribution to growth, thanks to government transfers rolled out to households affected by the
 pandemic. Government investment spending contributed significantly, accounting for one-third of GDP
 growth. However, unemployment continued to be higher relative to the pre-crisis levels as firms were not
 yet confident about the recovery sustainability and did not hire permanent employees. In August 2021,
 the unemployment rate was more than 3 percentage points higher than in August 2019.

 Pillar A: Contain the fiscal impact of the electricity sector

 expansion program was to be financed by the private sector. However, because they had not been
 competitively bid, some of the private sector projects were to supply electricity at high costs. The high
 cost of power from these projects together with the costs of the GoR’s ambitious access expansion

 PILLAR B: Improve the operational efficiency, affordability, and accountability of electricity service

 B1: Transition to a least cost and low carbon energy mix

 B1.1: Prior Actions related to least-cost planning

 40.     Before the DPO program, power system expansion investments were selected on an ad hoc basis
 against unsolicited proposals, without systematic planning to optimize supply options, including regional
 power trade. In FY2016/17, the energy generation mix contained a sizable proportion of expensive and
 high-carbon emitting technology (18 percent of thermal diesels). The proportion of thermal power in the
 energy mix had a significant impact on total supply costs when international oil prices increased and
 because of high transport costs due to the country’s landlocked location and long distance from the
 nearest ports. High costs of power supply were also caused by the practice of securing private sector
 investments, in many cases, through bilateral negotiations, thus depriving the country of the cost and
 transparency benefits of competitive bidding.

 41.     A key measure in addressing the issue of high costs of electricity was the move toward the use of
 systematic least-cost planning for all segments of the power sector. Pending completion of a revised least-
 cost expansion plan, initial measures were taken to rationalize the existing power development plan at
 the beginning of the DPO series by delaying less urgent capacity additions. These measures contributed
 to a reduction in the proportion of thermal generation from 18 percent in FY2016/17 to 10 percent in
 FY2020/21 while the proportion of energy generated from hydro increased from 39 percent to 52 percent.

 B1.2: Prior Actions related to regulatory framework

 44.      DPO3: PA 3.4: Completion by RURA of a review of its regulatory framework for cross-border
 electricity trade which concluded that its grid code and licensing regulations are compatible with
 electricity trade in the East Africa Power Pool. Imported and shared power comprised about 11 percent
 of the generation mix in FY2016/17 and remains at about the same level in FY2020/21.

 45.     This prior action is relevant to the objective of diversifying sources of supply and incorporating
 lower cost power from neighboring countries. The prior action is, therefore, rated Highly Satisfactory.

 B2: Improve access to affordable and reliable electricity

 46.     Under EDPRS II, the GoR had set an electrification target of 70 percent by 2018 comprising 48
 percent on-grid and 22 percent off-grid connections. Experience showed that grid connections were slow
 due to the high cost of connections and the affordability constraints faced by many rural households even
 with Government subsidies. The Government revised its electrification strategy to place emphasis on off-
 grid solutions which appeared more suitable to households with low incomes and low-volume power
 needs. The revised targets under NST1 showed that 100 percent electrification could be achieved
 comprising 52 percent on-grid and 48 percent off-grid connections by 2024. The on-grid and off-grid
 targets appear to have been policy constraints introduced into the modelling based on the GoR’s
 assessment of the best combination of on-grid and off-grid to achieve its electrification targets given the
 constraints it faced.

 Pillar A: Contain the fiscal impact of the electricity sector

 RI A1: Subsidies as a percentage of GDP and RI A2: Implement quarterly automatic tariff adjustments

 63.       Relevance. Subsidies are defined as budget transfers to the sector, including grants for public
 investments. RI A1 accurately captures the impact of PA 3.1, which in turn, is relevant to the PDO of
 containing the fiscal impact to the electricity sector. It does so by fully incorporating the public sector
 financing needs including for the NEP financing. In September 2019, RURA approved implementation of
 automatic quarterly tariff adjustments (RI A2) to protect the tariff’s loss of value due to cost increases
 arising from inflation, foreign exchange changes, and fuel costs. Both results indicators were measurable,
 appropriately captured the impacts of PA 3.1 and its associated prior actions (PA 1.1 and PA 2.1).
 Therefore, the relevance of these results indicators is rated Highly Satisfactory and so is the rating for
 Pillar A.

 64.      Efficacy of results. Since quarterly increases were introduced to reinforce RI A1, the two results
 indicators are rated together for efficacy. Actual subsidies to the energy sector (RI A1) remained at or
 below the target level of 1.5 percent of GDP throughout the four-year implementation period of the DPO
 series (FY2017/18–FY2019/20) (figure 6). Despite annual variations, the subsidy averaged 1.5 percentage
 for the period indicating the GoR’s commitment to the policy objective of containing subsidies to the
 sector. Thus, even as the overall fiscal deficit widened in 2019/20 and FY2020/21, the target was achieved.
 However, in FY2018/19 and FY2020/21, subsidies amounted to less than 1 percent of GDP partly due to
 actual expenditures falling below budgeted levels (figure 7). 15 The current three-year trajectory covering
 FY2021/22–FY2023/24 has been prepared, confirming Rwanda’s adoption of this mechanism for
 managing subsidies to the energy sector.

 65.      The objective was to contain subsidies in the short and medium term and to increase cost
 recovery gradually with the intention of eliminating subsidies in the long term. A review of the financial
 results of REG’s revenue earning subsidiary (EUCL) over the four-year implementation period for the DPO
 series indicates improvements in cost recovery (table 7). The margin between revenues and operating
 costs increased because of tariff increases implemented in 2017, 2018, and 2020; prudent management
 of expenditures; and growth in sales. Thus, the gross profit margin doubled from 13 percent in FY2017/18
 to 26 percent in FY2020/21 with most of the improvement taking place in FY2020/21 due to the tariff
 increase implemented in that fiscal year. The operating ratio 16 which measures the recovery of operating
 costs including depreciation improved from 120 percent in FY2017/18 to 98 percent in FY2020/21 while
 the working ratio which includes depreciation improved from 110 percent to 84 percent over the same
 period. It is notable that (a) the annual operating subsidies to EUCL over the four-year period remained
 broadly at the same level as shown in the table and (b) EUCL incurred net operating losses before financing
 charges and excluding subsidies every year until FY2020/21 when a small profit of RWF 3.66 billion was
 realized.

 66.     EUCL’s modest financial performance improvement over the program implementation period
 indicates that progress has been achieved in the implementation of the GoR’s policy of containing fiscal
 transfers to the sector while gradually improving cost recovery.

 Pillar B: Improve operational efficiency, affordability, and accountability for electricity services

 RI B1: Ensure all generation and transmission projects initiated or accepted by the Government over the
 past 24 months are consistent with the LCPDP and comply with the PPP Law and competitive procurement
 procedures.

 68.      Relevance of results indicator. This results indicator is linked to PA 1.2, PA 1.3, PA 2.4, and PA 3.2
 which cover the requirement for REG to update the LCPDP annually based on an approved methodology
 for incorporating GHG emission analysis and is consistent with the NEP. The results indicator which
 measures compliance with system planning practices was to ensure that only least-cost investments were
 implemented, thus helping reduce costs, and minimize fiscal transfers to the electricity sector. The
 second aspect of the results indicator was to measure compliance with the PPP Law’s (2016) provisions
 for competitive procurement of power infrastructure investments. The results indicator, therefore,
 accurately measures the impact of PA 1.2, PA 1.3, PA 2.4, and PA 3.2 which are appropriately linked to the
 PDO of containing fiscal impact through both revenue and cost management measures. The relevance of
 this results indicator is rated Highly Satisfactory.

 69.      Efficacy of results. Although no new generation projects were accepted and procured by the
 Government for implementation during 2018–2020, this results indicator was achieved. The generation
 expansion plan for 2019–2025 contained several power plants, the commissioning dates of which were
 delayed, which helped address the problem of overcapacity that was anticipated by 2025. Some small
 hydropower projects experienced delays in reaching financial closure, and with their PPAs expiring, the
 GoR renegotiated their commissioning schedules to align with the timing of new capacity additions
 indicated by the LCPDP and renegotiate their feed in tariffs. Other factors which suggest the
 institutionalization of the least-cost planning approach to power sector expansion are that (a) the GoR did
 not accept and procure new projects on an ad hoc basis as was the practice before 2018 and (b) the
 planning for the 2026–2040 includes new technology options (hydro-pumped storage, biomass, and
 natural gas-fired power plants) as suggested by the LCPDP planning tools. This results indicator is rated
 High for efficacy because the target of zero new capacity additions was not based on the LCPDP approach,
 and no non-competitive bidding was met and the transition to least-cost planning was implemented.

 EUCL made substantive improvements in the quality of service in Rwanda. The two targets for RI B6 were
 both exceeded with a SAIDI of 17.2 hours compared to a target of 20 hours and a SAIFI of 37 compared to
 a target of 283. Hence, this results indicator is rated High for efficacy.

 RI #B7: Implement and publish annual customer satisfaction survey

 82.     Relevance of results indicator. This results indicator was intended to measure the impact of
 actions to improve the quality of service (PA 2.8 and PA 3.9). The surveys would reflect the effectiveness
 of actions to improve quality, which would in turn increase customers' willingness to pay for services. The
 relevance of the results indicator is, therefore, rated Highly Satisfactory.

 83.     Efficacy of results. REG indicates that it has carried out regular customer surveys. REG provided
 data on customer surveys comprising a questionnaire and a tabulation of customer responses for
 FY2020/21, a report by the Rwanda Governance Board (RGB), including, among other things, data on
 annual customer surveys and an EUCL action plan for addressing RGB’s findings. The FY2020/21 customer
 survey was appropriately focused on customers’ knowledge about REG and whether the services that it
 provided were satisfactory. The questionnaire could have been more specific to solicit data on the
 customers’ experiences with the quality and pricing of electricity services (for example, outages and
 voltage fluctuations). Overall, about 90 percent of the responses rated the quality of electricity services
 in the good to excellent range. Given that there are no trend data on annual customer survey results and
 on specific actions taken by REG, the efficacy rating for this results indicator is Modest.

  C.      Overall Outcome Rating and Justification

 85.      Given an overall rating of Highly Satisfactory for the relevance of prior actions and a Satisfactory
 rating for efficacy, the outcome rating is Satisfactory. The efficacy assessment was positively influenced
 by the GoR’s success in containing subsidies to the sector even when its fiscal deficits widened due to the
 impact of the pandemic in FY2019/20 and FY2020/21; the GoR’s adherence to the policy of not initiating
 new projects that are not part of the least-cost expansion plan and not entertaining unsolicited bids—a
 clear break with the past which will have a strong impact on the costs of power supply in future (RI B1)
 and REG’s remarkable achievement in improving the quality of power supply as demonstrated by the
 reductions in the frequency and duration of supply interruptions (RI B6). A few shortcomings lowered the
 program’s efficacy ratings. There were the shortcomings in the design of the Results Framework with
 some misalignment between the dates for achievement of targets and the program closure date and
 inconsistencies between baseline and target values for system losses. In addition to the system losses
 results, the other indicator which weighed down the efficacy rating was the one on the annual customer
 satisfaction survey, where there was lack of trend data of annual surveys of customer satisfaction (RI B7).
 The client provided only data for FY2020/21 to the ICR team.

 III.    OTHER OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS

 A. Poverty, Gender, and Social Impacts

 86.      The program had the potential to have poverty, gender, and social impacts through various
 policies, including the prior actions on tariff adjustments and rationalization of categories, least-cost
 expansion planning, including a new emphasis on renewable instead of fossil-fueled new generation
 capacity.


                                                                                                         Page 41 of 65
The World Bank
Third Rwanda Energy Sector Development Policy Operation (P169040)



 87.      Rwanda had increased tariffs in 2015 and 2016 and was to increase them again in 2017, 2018, and
 2020. The simulation of the impact of the tariffs showed that the net impact on the poor was very small
 in most cases due to the low volume of electricity consumption by most households. The minimal impact
 of the increases since 2017 was due to the introduction of a new tariff schedule under the DPO (PA 1.6),
 which included, among other measures, a lifeline tariff for low-volume consumers consuming less than 15
 kWh. This measure reduced tariffs for low-income households by about half without significantly affecting
 the utility’s revenues.

 88.     A second measure (PA 1.7) introduced a new connection policy which allowed consumers to pay
 connection costs in instalments with energy purchases, thus eliminating the need to accumulate savings
 or seek financing for connections.

 89.      The adoption of least-cost expansion planning should lead to cheaper power with prioritization of
 domestic renewable energy over more expensive fossil-fueled technologies. International experience,
 including from Rwanda, has shown that access to electricity is a viable pathway to reducing poverty and
 managing prosperity as people get opportunities to move into other types of productive activities, to build
 social capital through education and access to health facilities.

 90.      The national access rate to electricity increased from 41 percent in 2017 to about 54 percent in
 2020. Grid connections increased from 29.7 percent to 39 percent during the same period whereas off-
 grid connections increased at a slower rate from 11 percent to 15 percent, falling short of the target of 23
 percent. The slower off-grid connection rate was despite the Government’s wide range of policies
 targeted at universal access by 2024 suggesting the persistence of constraints. Access to debt financing
 was identified by the GoR as a major constraint to off-grid electrification, a finding which led to the World
 Bank financing of the Rwanda Renewable Energy Fund Project (P160699) in an amount of US$48.94
 million, approved on June 20, 2017. The uptake of the debt facility was slow primarily because the high-
 income end of the household sector was already saturated, and the lower-income households faced
 affordability constraints. Opening of the window for lending to solar companies was delayed because of
 the GoR’s placing of higher priority on households; micro, small, and medium enterprises; and mini-grid
 developers. The REF was restructured in 2020 to address the problem of limited affordability by providing
 partial grants through an additional window and removing size restriction on eligible borrowers for other
 windows. Since access to financing has been identified as a major constraint to off-grid access expansion
 several countries have provided lines of credit facilities. Lessons of experience from these efforts will help
 inform the best approaches for financing off-grid access expansion.

 91.      While a sub-indicator of the NER was introduced during the program to track the electrification
 rate among female-headed households, this indicator has not been tracked and there is no readily
 available way to assess progress. The indicator is normally captured during the HLCS exercise, which was
 interrupted due to the restrictions introduced by the COVID-19 pandemic. This should be an important
 priority given that studies have shown that, in general, women tend to benefit more from access to
 electricity because of the reduction in hard chores such as gathering firewood and are able to use the
 saved time on other more productive activities.

 B. Environmental, Forests, and Natural Resource Aspects
 92.     The program was not expected to have significant adverse impacts on the environment. On the
 contrary, the adoption of least-cost power planning had several positive benefits for the environment,


                                                                                                          Page 42 of 65
The World Bank
Third Rwanda Energy Sector Development Policy Operation (P169040)



 including the comparison of fossil fuel technologies with renewables with full accounting for GHG
 emissions which will likely lead to selection of more renewable projects for development. The wide range
 of policies adopted by the GoR for the off-grid space, including a simplified regulatory framework,
 investment guidelines, and financing incentives are favorable to the development of more
 environmentally friendly renewable energy technologies, but the uptake has been slower than anticipated
 due to affordability constraints. The negative environmental effect of handling solar panels and batteries
 in the off-grid solar development is being handled by a separate project—the REF.

 IV.     BANK PERFORMANCE

 A. Other Unintended Outcomes and Impacts

 95.      The World Bank drew on its deep knowledge of the Rwanda energy sector derived from many
 years of working on the sector and firm international experience to design a comprehensive and ambitious
 program. The program design built on the lessons from other ongoing and closed World Bank operations
 to bring together policies that could not have been effectively supported by separate operations. The
 consolidation helped to add momentum to the GoR’s own efforts which were incorporated in the strategic
 development plans like EDPRS II and the NST1.

 96.      The program design was further supported by a wealth of analytical work such that most prior
 actions had identifiable links to relevant studies. The World Bank team also took two approaches that
 differed from usual World Bank practice in that (a) the program sought to address the potential fiscal risks
 of the electricity sector based on projections before the risks materialized and (b) some prior actions were
 identified as important and were included in the design on the basis that they would be refined, and data
 collected for the results targets before the subsequent DPO. Both approaches worked out successfully, in
 practice.

 97.       On May 1, 2020, the World Bank approved a US$100 million supplemental financing DPO to
 support the GoR in preparing a timely fiscal response to the economic shock from the COVID-19 pandemic,
 put in place platforms for policy development and coordination of prevention and preparedness, and
 safeguard the reform agenda supported by the DPO series. The DPO program was vulnerable to disruption
 through multiple channels as a result of the pandemic, including the possibilities of inadequate fiscal
 transfers to the sector given the demand on public funds for fighting the pandemic, reduced energy
 demand due to the lockdown and reduced economic activity, reduced revenues to the utility, and
 difficulties in paying IPPs. The mitigation of impacts on the energy sector was through general budget
 support to the GoR and to the preparation of a response to the pandemic at the macro level.

 98.      The World Bank team used the program Results Framework to supervise the operations. The DPOs
 were implemented quickly one after another which enabled the team to remain deeply focused as is
 apparent from the successive Program Documents. However, the design shortcomings, related to national
 electrification and system losses baseline and targets and to arrangements for data collection for results
 verification, were not identified and corrective actions taken.

 99.     On the Government side, the Program Appraisal Documents reported that MININFRA had the
 responsibility to monitor the program for that purpose and had formed a working group that included
 MINECOFIN and REG and its subsidiaries group for that purpose. The World Bank team reported that it
 interfaced with the working group as part of its program monitoring activities. Available minutes of
 meetings by a technical working group attended by MININFRA, REG, and the World Bank team indicate
 that the program results indicators and targets were reviewed and required actions identified. MININFRA
 indicated that there were also other meetings within the GoR during which program implementation was
 monitored and evaluated.




                                                                                                        Page 45 of 65
The World Bank
Third Rwanda Energy Sector Development Policy Operation (P169040)



 100. The Government and partners convened regular meetings of the Energy Sector Working Group
 which discussed energy sector issues more broadly but were not specifically aimed at monitoring
 implementation of the DPO series.

 V.      RISK TO SUSTAINABILITY OF DEVELOPMENT OUTCOMES

 101. The DPO series has been successful in supporting the GoR objective of containing subsidies while
 simultaneously ensuring adequate electricity supply to meet its economic growth and electricity
 expansion objectives. There are, however, a few key risks. First, a macroeconomic risk of slower-than-
 average economic growth or an economic shock could prevent the payment of fiscal transfers to REG and
 would make sector operations untenable as the sector’s dependency on transfers is still large. A severe
 economic contraction could also affect the utilities revenue generation with customers facing affordability
 problems and tariffs not being increased under such circumstances. The issues were already encountered
 in 2020 with the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic. Although tariff adjustments, including
 implementation of the newly introduced automatic quarterly adjustment, were suspended due to the
 pandemic, the three-year medium-term trajectory of subsidies continued to be prepared and used as tool
 for managing subsidies. Due to its high level of commitment to the policy of containing subsidies and with
 external support (including the World Bank Supplemental DPO) and controls of energy sector expenditure,
 the GoR was able to contain subsidies within the agreed range of not more than 1.5 percent of GDP.

 102. Another risk is that operational improvement reforms may not yield significant cost reductions,
 non-adjustment of tariffs, or a combination of both. Lack of progress in closing the ARCOS gap would
 compromise the reform program. The World Bank’s long-term relationship with Rwanda and support to
 its energy sector is expected to continue addressing these issues. The World Bank is currently providing
 technical support to the energy sector through a programmatic series of advisory services and analytics
 (ASA) which include, among other things, pillars on financial sustainability and operational efficiency of
 the electricity sector (Analyses to Improve Energy Access and Operational and Financial Performance of
 Energy Services Delivery in Rwanda - P177429). The ASA will support the development of a tariff setting
 methodology consistent with the best international practices and designed to ensure full cost recovery.
 While REG has implemented state-of-the-art information systems to support operations in all business
 areas, additional work is required to bring about further improvements through IBMSs. The World Bank’s
 work on these two pillars will contribute to managing the risks of inadequate tariffs and improvements in
 REG’s operations. In addition, the ASA program provides support for improving least-cost planning and
 aligning it with Rwanda’s NDC commitments and energy efficiency and renewable energy, thus helping
 reduce costs of supply and identifying productive uses of electricity which will help improve affordability.

 103. Finally, connections in the off-grid sector totaled 15 percent missing the DPO target of 23 percent
 as of June 2021 suggesting that constraints remain, particularly regarding affordability by low-income
 households. The World Bank is supporting Rwanda through the REF (P160699) to address affordability
 issues using different tools, including results-based grant financing, and allowing private sector access to
 grant financing for low-income projects.

  VI.     LESSONS LEARNED AND NEXT PHASE

  A. Lessons Learned
 104. The design and implementation of the Rwanda Energy Sector DPOs have important lessons for
 the World Bank and for other countries. The key lessons are the following:

        (a)   Financial imbalances in power companies eventually turn into crisis unless the underlying
              causes are diagnosed and addressed. A proactive approach can avoid a crisis and provide
              an opportunity for building a financially sustainable power sector. In Rwanda, the signs of
              an impending financial crisis were apparent from the large gap between the average cost of
              supply and the average revenue, a costly investment pipeline that was to deliver more high-
              cost power, and a country that was experiencing a slowdown in economic growth and facing
              external account imbalances. With the World Bank’s support, the GoR embarked on serious
              reforms to lower the costs of supply by optimizing power system planning, adopting
              competitive procurement for new capacity, diversifying source of energy, implementing
              deep operational improvement, while taking steps to improve affordability of power and
              revenue generation.
        (b)   Important reforms that have multisector impacts are likely to be better designed if the
              process of shaping them is shared with key partners from affected sectors. In the case of
              Rwanda, electricity plays a top role in the GoR’s strategic plans for economic growth and
              poverty reduction. MINECOFIN is, therefore, a strong partner with MININFRA, which is
              responsible for the energy sector. MINECOFIN also finances the power sector’s capital
              expenditures and operating deficits. The two ministries worked together to define a
              trajectory of fiscal transfers that were needed in the medium term while sector reforms
              were implemented to move toward full cost recovery.
        (c)   When preparation of the DPO started there was concern that Rwanda had overcommitted
              to building new generation capacity which would have outstripped the growth in demand
              by 2025 resulting in increased costs and the requirement for huge subsidies to the sector.
              The GoR and the World Bank worked together to rationalize the investment program, a
              process which included delaying some projects to ensure that projects would only come on
              stream when needed and thus managed to avoid a situation of excess capacity.
        (d)   While electricity sector targets for generation and capacity and access expansion are
              useful to guide development strategies for the electricity sector, their periodic review and
              adjustment is critical to adapt them to implementation experiences. Rwanda’s national
              electrification target of 70 percent by 2018 under EDPRS II was to be achieved through grid
              connections (48 percent) and off-grid connections (22 percent). The relative contributions
              of grid and off-grid connections were changed significantly during NST1 (2017–2024) on the
              realization that low-income, low-volume rural consumers could be better served through
              renewable technologies, especially solar. The targets were reset at 48 percent for on-grid
              and 52 percent for off-grid connections under NTST1. The strategy continues to be updated
              to address affordability constraints among rural households, including by providing results-
              based grant financing.
        (e)   The DPO instrument can be used flexibly in a country context with fast growth in a demand
              and supply situation, solutions to key issues are not clear cut, and/or data are not fully

                                                                                                     Page 47 of 65
The World Bank
Third Rwanda Energy Sector Development Policy Operation (P169040)



               available to decide on all results indicators and targets. The Rwanda experience shows that
               a sector specific DPO can be effective in supporting reforms when a client has committed to
               implementing wide ranging reforms in a gradual manner, but the details are not concretized
               at the beginning. The sequencing of DPO operations in a series allowed for adjustments to
               be made to prior actions and RI targets as new data became available and conditions
               changed. For example, several prior actions had their RI targets developed in the intervals
               between DPO1 and DPO2 which allowed the DPO to start without having to wait for
               preparation of every aspect of the program. A multisector DPO would not have provided the
               scope to address as comprehensive a set of reforms as flexibly as was done under the
               Rwanda DPO. A Program-for-Results is an alternative instrument for supporting sector
               reforms, but it would not have provided the flexibility to work out some aspects of the
               program during implementation.
         (f)   Serious reform proposals require more Government buy-in at high levels. After defining
               the fiscal transfer mechanism, MININFRA and MINECOFIN jointly sought approval from the
               Cabinet’s Economic Cluster. The sense of shared ownership of the policy created by this
               process has institutionalized the reform and the three-year medium-term trajectory of fiscal
               transfers to the energy sector continues to be utilized.
         (g)   Strong relationships of trust and commitment between the World Bank and clients can
               facilitate speedier preparation and phased implementation of operations without having to
               wait for all the policies to be finalized upfront. In the case of this DPO program, some areas
               needed data from that were only going to become available later and the World Bank agreed
               to proceed and to have those aspects incorporated in the next DPO in the series.
         (h)   Strong donor coordination can facilitate the achievement of a country’s sector development
               objectives and target due to the alignment of technical and financial support with the
               government leadership and ownership of strategy. Government-donor coordination in
               Rwanda’s energy sector which started under the eSWAP has been effective for successor
               multi-donor operations such as the EAQIP. The coordination is managed through a Sector
               Working Group co-chaired by MININFRA and a donor/World Bank representative.
         (i)   Electrifying the poorest 40 percent households remains a daunting challenge in low-income
               countries. Despite a wide range of well-thought-out reforms (including investment
               incentives, subsidies, minimum technical standards, lighter regulation, financing
               mechanisms for connections, and so on), off-grid connections lagged targets with clear
               indications that affordability was the main constraint. The lesson from the DPO and
               associated projects in Rwanda is the need to constantly innovate and, perhaps, to share
               experience with other countries in similar situations which are experimenting with different
               approaches.

     B. Next Phase

 105. The DPO sets the stage for the GoR objective of containing subsidies while ensuring adequate
 electricity supply to meet its economic growth and electricity expansion objectives. In September 2021,
 the World Bank approved the GoR’s Electricity Access and Quality Improvement Project (P172594), which
 will continue to help the Government pursue its development objectives, in accordance with the policies
 set up under the DPO. The project addresses electrification based on the NEP developed under the DPO,
 and increased reliability and quality of the electricity network. The project is part of a program funded by

                                                                                                        Page 48 of 65
The World Bank
Third Rwanda Energy Sector Development Policy Operation (P169040)



 different donors (AFD, European Investment Bank [EIB], AfDB, and Organization of Petroleum Exporting
 Countries [OPEC]), with similar objectives. Additionally, the World Bank is working on a set of analytical
 activities to inform the next round of IDA funding; that is aligned to the objectives of the DPO, that is,
 least-cost electrification, energy efficiency, increase in productive use of electricity, and streamlining of
 the least-cost planning process (including alignment to the government’s NDC’s). Implementation of these
 activities will ensure continuity of the reforms supported by the process to contain subsidies to the sector
 while pursuing the development objectives of the sector.
