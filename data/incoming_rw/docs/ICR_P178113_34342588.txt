    I.    PROGRAM CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES

    A. Context at Appraisal

    1.     At the time of appraisal in 2020 of the first Human Capital for Inclusive Growth Development Policy Financing
    (HCIG DPF) operation, Rwanda faced intensifying fiscal pressures placing allocations to the human development
    sectors at risk.1 While Rwanda had made impressive progress on some human development indicators, actions to
    improve human development outcome were needed to sustain equitable growth. Rwanda’s macroeconomic growth
    prospects had deteriorated at the time of appraisal, and there was a need to protect social spending in the face of
    COVID-19 and to increase fiscal allocations to the human development sectors over the medium term. Rwanda had
    been experiencing an economic boom before the pandemic, with annual Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth
    estimated at seven percent on average, between 2014 and 2019. But the pandemic interrupted Rwanda’s growth
    momentum as GDP dropped by 3.2 percent in 2020, compared to a projected expansion of 8 percent before the COVID-
    19 outbreak. The pandemic-driven deterioration in output and export growth, accompanied by increased borrowing
    needs, had resulted in a further deterioration in Rwanda’s debt position, limiting fiscal space for social spending. The
    cost of the Economic Recovery Plan initiated to mitigate the economic impacts of COVID-19 was estimated at US$900
    million over the two fiscal years 2019/20 and 2020/21, which is equivalent to about 4.4 percent of GDP on average per
    year. Public debt reached 72 percent of GDP in 2020 (from 58.1 percent in 2019). Consequently, the International
    Monetary Fund (IMF) and World Bank had revised the risk rating of debt distress from “low” to “moderate”.

2.      Rwanda had achieved all health Millennium Development Goals by 2015, expanded primary net enrollment,
and made significant progress in reducing poverty. However, low human capital had been identified as a key structural
bottleneck to realizing the country’s inclusive growth and poverty reduction agenda. The 2020 Human Capital Index (HCI)
estimated that a child born at the time in Rwanda would be only 38 percent as productive as an adult compared what
s/he could have achieved with good education and health. This weak HCI score was driven by low-quality education and
a high child stunting rate.

3.       The Government of Rwanda (GoR) was responsive to the COVID-19 pandemic, which became a prolonged
global challenge right at the start of this series, while paying due attention to addressing medium-term human capital
reforms. The pandemic was expected to worsen the human capital outlook through public health challenges and loss of
income due to containment measures. Interrupted social services could adversely affect human capital outcomes,
especially for the poorest households not reached by the social safety net system. For example, loss of household level
income combined with nutrition losses for pregnant women and young children would have long-term impacts on health,
child development, and future productivity. The GoR while remaining cognizant to these challenges, maintained strong
commitment to address human capital gaps through approval of medium-term reforms, investments in building more
resilient systems for the future, and promoting fiscal sustainability.

4.       The expansion in social services supported by the DPF was much needed and done with attention to equity,
quality, and efficiency of social spending. Rwanda had spent heavily on large-scale infrastructure in the years preceding
the DPF series (2013-2019) and major investments in the social sectors were required to improve the country’s human
capital trajectory and to support long-term inclusive growth prospects. However, in step with this expansion, the DPF

5.      The DPF series was anchored around core national development objectives by addressing identified
constraints. It was grounded in the GoR’s National Strategy for Transformation (NST1) and the World Bank’s 2019
Systematic Country Diagnostic (SCD)2 , both of which are reflected in the World Bank Country Partnership Framework
(CPF) for the Republic of Rwanda FY21-FY26 (Report No. 148876-RW) and subsequently in the Performance and Learning
Review for the CPF dated November 1, 2023 (Report No. 185559-RW). The NST1’s second pillar, Social Transformation,
aimed to develop a capable and skilled society with quality standards of living, stability, and security; and its third pillar,
Transformational Governance, sought to strengthen governance and justice as building blocks for equitable and
sustainable national development. The CPF’s Objective 1 was to improve human capital, and the HCIG DPF series was
highlighted as the main vehicle through which the World Bank would support Rwanda to address the key constraints.

Original Program Development Objective (PDO)

6.      The original PDO as approved was to support economic stability and inclusive growth in Rwanda by : (a)
promoting long-term fiscal sustainability; (b) enhancing coverage and equity in access to critical social programs; (c)
enabling learning and development of children; and (d) strengthening decentralized capacity and accountability for
accelerated human capital development. Synergistic reforms across these four pillars were expected to contribute to
improved economic stability, strengthened social services for improved human capital outcomes, and inclusive growth.

Original Pillars Supported by the Program (as approved)

7.       Pillar A: Promoting long-term fiscal sustainability. Pillar A was a critical policy priority since maintaining the
state’s capacity to deliver public services and infrastructure for inclusive growth required sufficient fiscal space and
manageable risks to debt sustainability. Rwanda was facing narrowed fiscal space and higher public debt resulting from
an ambitious investment strategy coupled with the COVID-19 pandemic shock, that had increased the country’s
borrowing needs. Rwanda’s risk of debt distress changed from “low” to “moderate” in the June 2020 Debt Sustainability
Analysis, highlighting the need to prioritize fiscal and debt sustainability through fiscal management reforms.

8.      Pillar B: Enhancing coverage and equity in access to critical social programs. Pillar B was aimed at supporting
an increase in the coverage and human capital orientation of the social safety net and improving financing and
administration of the community-based health insurance (CBHI) scheme. This would help to protect household
consumption, including offsetting the income effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on vulnerable households, and
preventing the deterioration of human capital gains. Expanding CBHI coverage and strengthening its financial
sustainability would play a key role in promoting access to health services for short- to medium-term outcomes and in
making progress towards universal health coverage. This pillar was also aimed at supporting the objective identification
of the poorest and most vulnerable households through operationalization of a dynamic social registry to ensure that
relevant social sector interventions were delivered effectively to them. The social registry was considered an enabling
reform for human capital development.

9.      Pillar C: Enabling learning and development of children. Pillar C was aimed at supporting measures to improve
the quality, coverage, and efficiency of critical child development and education services for children from early
childhood through adolescence. These would ensure that, during their formative years, young children receive
appropriate nutrition and early stimulation to prepare them better to acquire skills required to impact positively their
capacity for learning and future productivity. The measures would also support their successful and smooth transition
through primary and secondary school, with a focus on improving learning outcomes, and efforts to strengthen teacher
management, a key ingredient in eliminating learning poverty. This pillar would continue the critical effort to implement
systemic reforms that would enable Rwanda to build back better and more resilient after the COVID-19 pandemic.

10.     Pillar D: Strengthening decentralized capacity and accountability. This pillar was aimed at supporting reforms
designed to strengthen capacity at the lowest levels of government to oversee social services critical for human capital
development at the point where those frontline activities occurred. It would support the MINALOC reforms for enhancing
the quality of service delivery by supporting improved planning and data management as well as a citizen-centered
approach for promoting vertical and horizontal accountability. Capacity would be added for effective implementation of
relevant policies and programs at the administrative level closest to citizens and service delivery-related coordination
would be strengthened between different levels of government. It would also support workload-based staffing norms at
the health facility level to enhance the quality of health services as well as health facility performance.

B. Significant Changes During Implementation

Revised PDO

11.    The PDO was revised slightly from DPF2 onwards to clarify better “social sector programs” instead of “social
programs” under objective/pillar B. There was no substantive change in the objectives or pillars.

Revised Policy Areas/Pillars supported by the Program

12.      The pillars were not revised; while a few Prior Actions (PA)/triggers were revised, their policy intent was
preserved. A few PAs were modified to align better with the rapidly evolving COVID-19 context. DPF3 triggers for two
policy areas were revised to reflect what was feasible within a revised implementation timeline without risking quality
or compromising policy intent (Section II A). These changes partly reflected the pandemic’s impact on the GoR’s capacity
to carry out medium-term reforms on schedule. Revisions to triggers/PAs are in Annex 5.

Other Changes

13.      The COVID-19 pandemic began during the preparation of the first DPF in the series and its historic and
multidimensional impacts continued throughout the program. The design of the series was already responsive to the
new threat posed by COVID-19. Implementation of the reforms was a little slower than expected on a few fronts in the
second and third operations in the series, particularly for the measures that involved large-scale recruitment or
development of information technology (IT) systems. However, all reforms were implemented steadily with just a few
slightly behind the original schedule.

A.       Relevance of Prior Actions
Relevance Rating: Highly Satisfactory

14.      The PAs were highly relevant, appropriately sequenced, and mutually reinforcing towards achieving the PDO.
Most PAs were designed to support a medium-term human capital reform program, a few addressed the impact of the
unfolding COVID-19 pandemic on human capital, and all were directly relevant to the PDO with significant synergies. The
policy intent of all nine reform areas under the four pillars/objectives was preserved during the revision of triggers into
PAs in DPF2 and DPF3, and all PAs remained consistent with the PDO. The series leveraged complementary technical
assistance and investment financing support from the World Bank, benefitted from the regular consultation with other
development partners (DPs), and remained fully aligned with other DPs financing and technical support.

Pillar A: Promoting long-term fiscal sustainability (Relevance - Highly Satisfactory)

15.      All PAs in the line of this reform area are designed and sequenced to promote long-term fiscal sustainability
without jeopardizing recovery efforts. All three PAs (1, 1a and 1b) were relevant to the PDO, and successively addressed:
(a) debt transparency through semi-annual publication of a debt statistical bulletin with comprehensive coverage of public

debt and government guarantees; (b) fiscal risk management through an annual Fiscal Risk Statement that enabled
analysis of State-Owned Enterprise (SOE) risk and contingent liabilities; and (c) public investment efficiency through a
medium-term PIP based on review and optimization of the existing pipeline for improvements in the project mix. The PIP
enabled the GoR to introduce important rationalization measures, including discontinuing underperforming domestic
investment projects across sectors and prioritizing existing projects over new projects for the implementation of NST1.

16.      The PAs directly supported the PDO and were grounded in the World Bank’s macroeconomic and fiscal dialogue,
were coordinated with the IMF, and consistent with the Economic Recovery Plan to mitigate the economic impacts of
COVID-19. They were also aligned with the GoR/World Bank Future Drivers of Growth report (2018), Rwanda SCD (2019)
and the Rwanda Debt Management Performance Assessment (2015). The Rwandan public-investment-led growth model
combined with the pandemic-driven increase in borrowing increased public debt to 71.3 percent of GDP in 2020 (from
62.9 percent in 2019), leading to the downgrading in the risk rating of debt distress . The reform program under this pillar
was highly relevant to the PDO, contributing to addressing fiscal sustainability and securing fiscal space to support critical
human capital needs for long term growth. Long-term fiscal sustainability in Rwanda requires debt and fiscal risk
transparency, fiscal risk management and strengthened public investment management. The first two operations in the
series, implemented at height of the COVID-19 (in 2020 and 2021), focused on debt transparency and fiscal risk
management, while the third operation emphasized public investment efficiency.

Pillar B: Enhancing coverage and equity in access to critical social sector programs (Relevance - Highly Satisfactory)

17.     All PAs under this pillar contributed directly to enhancing coverage and equitable access to relevant programs.
The nine mutually reinforcing PAs contributed to the PDO. They were aligned with the GoR’s COVID-19 Economic Recovery
Plan and grounded in the World Bank’s State of Safety Nets Report (2018). Critical programs that were enhanced to
strengthen their contribution to human capital are the Vision Umurenge Program (VUP): the GoR’s flagship social safety
net system) and the CBHI Scheme. The social registry was foundational to achieve their results by improving the pro-poor
targeting of these as well as other key social sector programs.

18.     Emergency cash transfers and expansion of human capital sensitive schemes within the safety net. The three
well-sequenced PAs (2, 2a and 2b) allowed the GoR to: (a) approve a policy for inclusion of emergency cash transfers in
the menu of VUP and then timely deliver them to the households working in the informal sector, largely in urban areas
(the majority being female-headed) during the COVID-19 crisis to cope with income loss due to pandemic containment
measures; and expand the proportion of gender, child, and nutrition sensitive safety net schemes in the overall coverage
of the VUP to strengthen accumulation of human capital among the poor and vulnerable households; (b) institutionalize
periodic reviews of benefit adequacy of gender, child, and nutrition sensitive safety net schemes to enhance their
development outcomes; and (c) integrate gender, child, and nutrition sensitive safety net schemes targets into districts
plans and reflect them in approved budgets until FY2024/25. This sequencing of reforms ensured a progressive and
effective implementation.

19.     Enhanced access to a financially sustainable CBHI. The three well-sequenced PAs (3, 3a and 3b) under this reform
area were directly linked to the achievement of the PDO objective of enhanced coverage in access to social sector programs,
and were deeply grounded in the World Bank report, Socio-Economic Analysis of Health Insurance in Rwanda (2019). They
contributed to: (i) rapid and improved enrollment of the poorest households into the CBHI through subsidized premium
paid by the Government to access affordable and easy access to quality healthcare; (ii) definition of an affordable CBHI
benefits package that covered essential services to all enrolled households; and (iii) a switch from an expensive fee-for-
service provider payment mechanism to capitation. PA 3a was modified slightly from the original trigger for DPF2 (Annex

20.      Operationalization of new dynamic social registry for improved pro-poor targeting of social sector programs.
This reform area played a central role in enhancing equity of critical social sector programs through improving the pro-poor
targeting efficiency and equitable access of the poor and vulnerable to social sector programs, and hence closely linked to
achieving the PDO. Since 2002, Rwanda had been using a community-based targeting system known as Ubudehe that used
a few mostly subjective variables, for communities to classify households into four categories, numbered 1 to 4. Most pro-
poor programs were provided to households in Ubudehe category 1 with some also reaching households in category 2. As
a targeting tool, the Ubudehe categorization system was met with increasing grievances by households as many of them
were in fact classified in categories that apparently did not match their actual welfare status, leading to significant exclusion
and inclusion errors. This motivated a shift to a social registry-based welfare score ranking system, that uses objectively
verifiable variables.

21.      The sequential PAs 4, 4a and 4b under the line of this transformational reform built upon a rigorous analysis of
Rwanda’s Integrated Household Living Conditions Survey (EICV-V) data on targeting efficiency of the two largest social
sector programs (VUP and CBHI), global knowledge and best practices on development of social registries, and multi-years
World Bank’s assistance to social protection sector in Rwanda, inter alia, through technical assistance to MINALOC from
early engagement on the Social Registry. That technical support helped: (a) develop the system requirement specification
for the Social Registry Information System (SR-IS), based on international best practices; (b) identify variables that are best
predictors of poverty and their coefficients that were then used to calculate household welfare scorecard, supported by
SR-IS built in functionality; and (c) prepare two reference documents for the social registry, namely a detailed Operations
Manual and a Communication, Outreach and Community Validation Protocol.

22.      DPF1 supported the shift from Ubudehe to an objective social registry that ranks the households’ welfare by
applying a scorecard developed by picking strong co-relates of poverty from the EICV-V data. The DPF2 and DPF3 supported
approvals of legal framework and enforcement of social registry eligibility for beneficiaries’ enrolment in the VUP and CBHI
respectively. Given the significance of this reform on improving the households’ welfare and greater acceptance by the
community, the GoR decided to follow enhanced due diligence in strengthening quality and accuracy of welfare scorecard
data, which delayed the implementation vis-à-vis the original schedule, but surely contributed to a high-quality and well-
accepted social registry information system to serve both immediate as well as the country’s longer-term targeting
efficiency.

Pillar C: Enabling the learning and development of children (Relevance - Highly Satisfactory)

23.     All three policy areas under this pillar area were relevant to the objective of enabling learning and development
of children. The reform program complemented by the World Bank’s investment financing and technical support to
education, health, and social protection sectors helped in substantially improving the quality, coverage, and efficiency of
child development and education services from early childhood through adolescence.

24.      Supporting integrated early childhood development (IECD) services: All three PAs (5, 5a and 5b) under the reform
area were relevant and improved the implementation and efficiency of IECD programs, by: (a) empowering decentralized
entities to coordinate and monitor IECD services; (b) nutrition budget tagging to enable National Child Development Agency
(NCDA) to manage effectively spending across multiple agencies from multiple sources; and (c) institutionalizing
comprehensive budget reviews for tracking efficiency of public expenditures to achieve the IECD outcomes.

25.      Student flow efficiency and learning outcomes: Underage enrolment was one of the key contributors to the low
rate of internal efficiency in student progression.8 Only 33 percent of P1 students started the grade at the official age, while
49 percent were underage, and rest were overage. The influx of underage children had led to severe overcrowding and
strained the capacity of poorly resourced primary school teachers who typically have no training in early childhood
education. Many underage children attended school irregularly and dropped out during the year, only to reenroll in P1 the
following year. An estimated 50 percent of underage children repeated P1. All three PAs (6, 6a, and 6b) under this reform
led to large scale improvements in student progression with greater learning. They ensured that: (a) schools are mandated
to enroll students at the right age, preventing under-age enrolment and overcrowding as well as improving the teaching-
learning environment; (b) schools are equipped for systemic tracking and support to student learning with formative
assessments and remediation strategies; and (c) the MINEDUC follows an operational plan for pre-primary expansion with
an immediate focus on teachers. Underage enrolment has now been eliminated and there has been a simultaneous
expansion for pre-primary education—the GER increased from 24.4 percent in 2018 to 53.1 percent in 2023.

26.      Efficiency in teacher management and retention, and effectiveness in instructional practice. A high-quality and
stable teaching workforce has a positive co-relationship with learning outcomes. The three PAs (7, 7a and 7b) under this
reform area supported a clear pathway for teachers that motivates quality at entry, incentivizes upgrading of skills on the
job, and supports teachers’ retention. The PAs: (a) required teachers to be licensed to teach under new criteria for
professional qualifications; (b) required districts to follow common standards for job promotion; and (c) provided the
MINEDUC with a teacher management information system (TMIS) that provides a systemic database on teacher
registration, categorization, deployment, and issuance of licenses.

Pillar D: Strengthening decentralized capacity and accountability (Relevance - Highly Satisfactory)

27.   Both policy areas under this pillar were aimed at strengthening capacity and accountability for delivery of key
human capital development services at the decentralized levels. Accordingly, these PAs contributed directly to the PDO.

28.      Strengthening capacity and accountability for service delivery at decentralized levels of government . The three
well-sequenced PAs (8, 8a, and 8b) under this reform area were meant to address institutional weaknesses that impacted
service delivery by: (a) increasing human capital development staffing capacity at the lowest administrative level (Cell) of

Operationalizing a new social registry – Reforms supported the development and rollout of a dynamic social registry which
enables pro-poor targeting of critical social sector programs.
43.      Rwanda availed the DPF supported reform program to successfully develop a new dynamic social registry, which
improves pro-poor targeting of social sector programs and efficiency of public spending and hence directly enhances
equity in access to critical social sector programs. PAs 4, 4a and 4b supported the development and operationalization of
this registry which categorizes households based on objective welfare assessment, a major improvement over the previous
community-based categorization system.

44.       The process of development began with adopting policy guidelines on the operationalization of a social registry
that applies a household welfare scorecard (HWS) developed by selecting strong correlates of poverty from the
Integrated Household Living Conditions Survey (EICV). The HWS estimated household welfare using characteristics such
as demographic profile and asset ownership. A SR-IS with nine modules10 has been developed and is generating targeting
lists for the VUP, the CBHI enrollment subsidy, and other social sector programs using the HWS. Nearly all beneficiaries of
these two programs are now identified by the registry and 100 percent of beneficiaries are to be paid based on these lists
in FY24/25. After each EICV round, the social registry model will be recalibrated to predict poverty and welfare more
accurately, for continued improvement in this national system. The World Bank supported the design and implementation
of the social registry through technical assistance (para. 20).

45.     The social registry rollout was managed carefully by the GoR, given the country context in Rwanda. Analysis of
household profiling data collected in 2020 just before COVID-19 in two initial districts showed many discrepancies, and the
number of households differed from the new national census numbers. The registry’s roadmap was revised, prioritizing
high quality data over rapid rollout. Following extensive communication, ongoing community validation, and cascaded
training for government officials (including district, sector, and cell officials), the registry—known as Imibereho
(socioeconomic profile)—is replacing the Ubudehe community-based targeting system for social sector programs.
Appeals/complaints mechanisms are being implemented and annual recertification will be conducted.

46.      The social registry is interoperable with other relevant databases of the government. A Social Registry Committee
has been made functional, which brings together multiple ministries and agencies to facilitate smooth data
management/interoperability. System integration was completed with the National Identification Agency’s National ID
database, Government’s Enterprise Service Bus, Rwanda Revenue Authority, National Land Authority, Ministry of Health,
Ngirankugire (MINALOC’s system to manage social protection interventions for community initiatives), School Data
Management System, and Disability Management Information System. Further work on integrating AI and BI tools in the
SRIS is underway to enhance its quality for adaptive and shock responsive social protection system in the country.

47.      RI 5 and RI 6 are relevant, measurable, and appropriate, and the targets have been exceeded. The results are
not just a one-time achievement but mark the start of a new era of efficiency in pro-poor targeting. The RIs measure the
direct result of the social registry development and rollout and reflect progress towards the objective of increasing equity
in access to key social sector programs. The definition of the RIs is clear, the source of data is MINALOC/EICV, and the data

     III. OTHER OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS

A.    Poverty, Gender, and Social Impacts

66.     Poverty Impacts. The HCIG DPF was expected to support efforts to protect and build the human capital of the
poor and most vulnerable in Rwanda. The incidence of poverty had fallen from 66 percent (2005/06) to 52 percent in
(2016/17) (using the international poverty line of US$2.15 2017 PPP) and was projected to fall slightly to 51.5 percent in
2019. This progress ceased with the widespread income losses and increases in unemployment resulting from the COVID-
19 pandemic and the subsequent high inflation, especially in food. Starting May 2022, Rwanda experienced 18 months of
the highest monthly food inflation of the last 15 years. According to micro-simulations, poverty has increased substantially
despite the GoR’s measures to protect the population from these shocks. The simulated increases would undo poverty
gains observed from 2010/11 to 2016/17 (most recent official poverty figures).

67.      Despite the simulated increases in poverty, emergency cash transfer and the expansion of the VUP’s gender-,
child-, and nutrition-sensitive components is expected to have contributed to mitigation. COVID-19 emergency cash
transfers were delivered to 135,081 households that would otherwise have resorted to negative coping mechanisms. The
proportion of regular VUP beneficiary households enrolled in NSDS and ePW increased from 19 percent (2020) to 53
percent (2023). Other reforms supported including the introduction of a nutrition-sensitive budget, expansion of CBHI
enrollment, and expansion of pre-primary education, all poverty-focused. Importantly, the social registry is allowing the
GoR to objectively identify many of Rwanda’s poorest who were excluded and will continue efficient targeting in the
future.

68.      Gender Impacts. The introduction of COVID-19 emergency transfers and expansion of NSDS cash transfers and
ePW are expected to have a disproportional impact on women and children. Women bore a greater burden of care and
unpaid care work since the onset of COVID-19. COVID-19 emergency cash transfers were delivered to 59 percent women
beneficiaries. The significant increase in the proportion of beneficiary households enrolled in the NSDS and ePW benefited
women directly, by not only improving targeted households’ consumption and asset accumulation, but also increasing the
availability of childcare, empowering women (as the recipients of cash benefits on behalf of families) and fostering
investment in young children during a critical window of opportunity. The expansion of pre-primary education is expected
to have positive impacts on women by facilitating mothers’ labor force participation with increased family income and
welfare and creating paid jobs in the early childhood education and care economy, which tend to be predominantly
female. Also, the upgraded TMIS will allow MINEDUC to better monitor and target gender equity in teacher hiring.

69.      Despite the DPF-supported reforms that were expected to impact women positively, existing inequalities in
human capital development and utilization may have deepened after the pandemic, with a marked impact on women and
girls. School closures have affected both girls and boys. The unemployment rate rose from 15.2 percent in 2019 to 21.1
percent in 2021, only falling slightly in 2022 with women and youth disproportionately affected. Meanwhile, the last five
years have seen only modest reductions in maternal and under-five mortality rates, and the decline in fertility rates seems
to have stagnated since 2015. Stunting remains a key human capital development challenge, affecting one child in three
with the highest rates (nearly 50 percent) among the poorest 20 percent, born to mothers with relatively little education,
poor nutritional status, and large families. Gender gaps in learning outcomes have narrowed but continue to persist, with
boys outperforming marginally outperforming girls on English, Math, and Science, at various grade levels.

B. Environmental, Forests, and Natural Resource Aspects

70.      The DPF was not expected to have environmental, forest or natural resource impacts. The GoR has in place
strong institutional arrangements and a good track record for conservation of the environment. However, Rwanda is highly
vulnerable to climate risks, including severe drought, that threat the country’s human capital development through
impacts on food security, nutrition, health, and education. The HCIG DPF was expected to implement reforms to help
mitigate the impacts of different shocks. The social registry provides a platform for adaptive and shock-responsive social
protection. The flexibility in adjusting the VUP Direct Support program to respond to pandemic-related income losses sets
an example of how the program can be adjusted and used effectively to minimize harmful coping strategies and mitigate
irreversible long-term damage to human capital accumulation in the event of a future shock.

C. Institutional Change/Strengthening

71.      The DPF did not have an institutional strengthening objective, per se, but several PAs supported reforms that
would impact the institutional structure, responsibilities, efficiency, information systems and availability of up-to-date
data for decision making and/or processes of GoR. The most notable achievement was the dynamic social registry and
its information system, which provides a foundation for targeting of all social sector programs. Other notable institutional
achievements relate to the development of a procedure manual for the regular publication of a statistical debt bulletin,
the adjustments in staffing at decentralized levels, and improvements in information management systems, including: (a)
an ECD-responsive budget management system that allows the mainstreaming of nutrition into the national planning and
budgeting processes for enhanced multisectoral joint planning, budgeting and resource allocations; (b) a Comprehensive
Student Assessment Management System that documents learning records of each student linked through their unique
identification number ensuring that students can be tracked for attendance, learning, and completion; (c) a national TMIS
that can track, record, and target teacher deployment, performance, and professional development needs; and (d) an
online portal that provides a more efficient, faster and transparent recruitment process for health professionals. By
following the revised teacher performance criteria and the associated career ladder, all districts now have to follow a
common standard for promotions, transfers, and dismissal, thereby improving transparency and fairness in teacher
management.

   V.    RISK TO SUSTAINABILITY OF DEVELOPMENT OUTCOMES

79.       The Risk to Sustainability of Development Outcomes is modest . The main downside risk is macroeconomic. It
includes the risk of a sharper slowdown in global growth fueled by monetary tightening in advanced economies which is
likely to soften the demand for Rwanda’s exports and reduce fiscal space for human capital actions. Another spike in global
commodity’s prices or climate shock affecting agricultural production could further entrench inflation impacting food
security and poverty. A substantial reduction in concessional resources (diversion to other crises) or the possible reduction
in demand for Rwandan exports from neighboring countries pose downside risks. Rwanda continues to rely upon
concessional debt- about 70 percent of the external debt, with the consequent risks of a possible declining availability of
concessional financing. On the positive side, despite challenges posed by the COVID-19 pandemic and recent high food
inflation, the GoR remains firmly committed to maintaining macroeconomic stability, reducing poverty and improving the
human capital development of Rwanda’s citizens, as framed within its NST1. The government has maintained prudent
macroeconomic policies despite global, regional and domestic exogenous shocks. After contracting 3.4 percent in 2020,
Rwanda’s economy expanded by 8.5 percent during 2021-2023, on average, and is expected to stabilize at pre-pandemic
growth rates of six to seven percent in 2024-2026. With Rwanda’s strong debt-carrying capacity, one of the highest
Country Policy and Institutional Assessment ratings for low-income countries, and a continued availability of concessional
borrowing, there is room for more, but different, public investment that does not jeopardize the country’s risk profile.

   VI. LESSONS AND NEXT PHASE

A. Lessons Learned

80.      Building multisectoral synergies into the design of the program was a good way to support the lifecycle
approach to human capital development, moving away from sectoral silos. For example, cross-sectoral synergies
between PA5 and PA6b, complemented by ongoing education and social protection IPFs, have accelerated the expansion
of coverage of pre-primary and ECD services for children at home, at centers, and in school. The implementation of PA6b
focused on the preprimary teacher workforce (80 percent female). The number of pre-primary teachers on government
payroll increased from 3 (2020) to 6,675 (2023). Pre-primary expansion efforts are also reflected in the construction of
360 new classrooms and the training of about 2000 teachers on competency-based curriculum. Consequently, the gross
enrolment rate for the pre-primary level has risen from 29 percent (2019) to 49.9 percent (2023) . MINEDUC and NCDA
have begun harmonizing pre-primary Education Minimum Standards with NCDA’s ECD standards to ensure broader
coverage of pre-primary educational services using consistent quality and standards across center- and school-based ECD
services.

81.      Long-term and adaptive support helps to build and improve systems over time, starting small and evolving to
more sophisticated investments. The World Bank’s support to the VUP, Rwanda’s flagship safety net program, has been
both focused and continuous, and harmonized with support from other partners. Starting with the establishment of the
VUP, the Bank has supported improvements over a decade with a series of three DPFs in social protection, the HCIG DPF
series, and various sectoral IPFs. Other DPs also offered budget support to Rwanda in social protection, selecting some of
the same triggers as those in the DPF series. The World Bank continues to support social protection in Rwanda after the
DPF series through the Social Protection Transformation Project (P177492) where the social registry and human capital
development are a major focus. Similarly, teacher management reforms are a long-term agenda for the World Bank and
GoR partnership that is supported through complementary efforts and financing. For instance, difficulties in
operationalization of the teacher licensing process were navigated with the use of TMIS that was co-developed with the
support of various partners and implementation financed under the 2022 additional financing to the Quality Basic
Education for Human Capital Development Project (P177983).

82.     Operationalization of a social registry may require a more phased approach in some country contexts,
particularly post-conflict, so that the government builds in time to consult, sensitize, validate and “get it right”.

Ultimately, the social registry needs to not only be a clear improvement over the existing targeting system but must also
be perceived as such among all stakeholders. The Rwandan government took the time it needed to perform
comprehensive due diligence with this foundational and potentially sensitive reform for a wider ownership at the country
level.

83.      Investments in Information Technology (IT) systems provide tremendous value but require realistic planning
and management, strong support, and understanding the institutional responsibilities for IT can help avoid
implementation delays. Difficulties in designing and implementation of IT systems contributed to operationalization
delays in various sectors supported by the DPF series. Examples include IT system development to underpin the dynamic
social registry, the teacher management information system in education, and the transition to capitation from fee-for-
service in health. IT systems development requires multifaceted support through technical assistance and IPF for robust
and timely systems design and institutional set-up, and through DPF to ensure that policies and programs are aligned and
can draw on these systems when implemented. Development of IT systems in Rwanda is overseen by a dedicated agency,
the Rwanda Information Society Authority (RISA) and this applied to design and implementation of the SR-IS. While
MINALOC remained the custodian of the social registry as the ministry in charge of social protection, RISA hired a local
software development firm and supervised it, through its Digital Unit. The centralized structure for IT development is
proving to be a strategic one as all systems in the country are being developed with technical aspects that allow them to
be interlinked. This has allowed Rwanda’s Social Registry to quickly interlink with other systems such as ID, CRVS, Land
Registry, Revenue Authority systems, and others.

 B. Next Phase

  84.     The IMF in December 2023 noted that continuing efforts to foster socioeconomic resilience—including enhancing
 social protection programs, and improving human capital accumulation, among others, will be critical to navigate
 challenging times and advance living standards. The World Bank’s support of Rwanda’s human capital development and
 inclusive growth agenda continues through a range of investments. Ongoing IPFs build on the HCIG DPF agenda, including
 the Social Protection Transformation Project (P177492), the Quality Basic Education for Human Capital Development
 Project (P177983), Additional Financing to the Stunting Prevention and Reduction Project (P179499), Additional Financing
 to the COVID-19 Emergency Response Project (P178282). While all these instruments are well placed to sustain and
 enhance the outcomes of the first-generation reforms supported by this series to accelerate development of human
 capital in Rwanda, there remains a need for Bank’s support and investment in the second-generation reforms to support
 the country in utilization of human capital for reducing poverty and promoting inclusive growth. The HCIG DPF series
 rallied other DPs around the human capital agenda by aligning respective support and paved the way for the follow up
 series which can bring even greater synergy and convergence to maximize the contribution of international development
.assistance to the country level outcomes.
