I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT



 A. Country Context



       1.       Vietnam has made a strong recovery since the economic downturn in 2011, with a gross domestic

       product (GDP) annual growth rate of 6.8 percent in 2015.1 GDP is projected to grow by 6 percent in 2016

       with inflationary pressures contained and the current account in balance. While these projections are

       positive, there are certain external and domestic risks that could impact growth. The Socio-Economic

       Development Plan (SEDP) 2016–2020 document raised the issues of “slow improvement in education and

       training, science and technology, culture, social issues, health care and job creation?. According to the

       Vietnam 2035 report, another concern is the downward trend of labor-productivity growth since the late

       1990s, from nearly 7 percent in 1995 to 3.5 percent in 2013.2 Considering that the labor force is shifting

       to more productive segments of the economy,3 skill requirements are rising. The rapidly aging population

       is beginning to have an impact on the labor force—the source of sustainable economic growth—

       necessitating new types of skills and different approaches in education. The working age population of

       Vietnam is expected to shrink by around 5 percent as a share of total population between now and the

       early 2040s (United Nations [UN] 2015).4 This necessitates the creation of technology-based jobs that will

       not require as many low-skilled workers.



 B. Sectoral and Institutional Context



       2.       Vietnam has made significant achievements in expansion of enrollment and general education

       quality. The country has achieved universal preschool education for five-year-old children, as well as for

       primary education. It is approaching universal lower secondary education. Between 2001 and 2013, net

       enrollment rates at lower secondary and upper secondary levels increased from 70.0 percent to 88

       percent and 33 percent to 58.5 percent respectively5. The number of students in higher education (HE)

       grew from 124,484 students in 1991 to over 2.3 million students in 2015. Enrollment over this period

       doubled roughly every five years, so that over 20 years the size of enrollment grew by nearly 17 times its

       original level.6 Vietnam has also attained higher levels of student learning achievement. Vietnam ranks

       among the top 10 countries participating in the 2015 Program for International Student Assessment (PISA)

       on the science score.7



       3.      Vietnam has made significant gains in gender equality in HE with more women than men enrolled.

       With regard to returns female labor force participation is high. However, challenges still remain. There is

       a sizable wage gap favoring men. Women are underrepresented as authors of research-based

       publications, especially in internationally recognized journals. Further, women are particularly

 4.       Despite impressive gains in access, HE fails to meet the demands for high-quality degree programs

 to provide the population with the skills and competencies needed today for producing greater value.8

 Nearly 50 percent of interviewed employers claimed that the education system does not meet the skill

 needs of their workplace.9 Research institutes and universities produce too little research that is mostly

 not of sufficient quality and relevance for the economy’s needs. Public universities are hampered by a lack

 of de facto autonomy, which impedes setting or changing curricula without external approval, raising

 adequate revenue, or developing full-time, high-quality faculty.10



 5.      Historically, higher education institutions (HEIs) were under the tight control of the ministries to

 which they reported. Academics, hiring, budget, enrollment, and tuition were all centrally determined. In

 1996, the two National Universities, one in Hanoi and one in Ho Chi Minh City, were the first institutions

 entitled to special autonomy rights. In the 2005 Law on Education and the 2012 Law on Higher Education,

 Vietnam further loosed strictures on HEIs so that they would be free to create higher quality programs,

 raise and manage the revenue they need to do this, free to hire and pay faculty, and enroll students based

 on their institutional requirements.



 6.      To take a further step, the Government issued Resolution 77/NQ-CP dated October 24, 2014, to

 give more autonomy to 15 pilot universities11 during the 2014–2017 period. These universities no longer

 have to obtain approval on (a) the number of students they enroll; (b) the courses and degree programs

 that they can offer; and (c) the compensation provided to the faculty and staff members of the

 universities. They also enjoy more financial autonomy with regard to higher tuition fee caps and freedom

 in revenue-generation decisions.



 7.       While universities have been given more freedom, the Government started pushing forward

 stronger requirements of quality assurance. Vietnam joined the Asia-Pacific Quality Network, the

 International Network for Quality Assurance Agencies in Higher Education, and the Association of South

 East Asian Nations (ASEAN) Quality Assurance Network. The HE quality assurance system includes internal

 quality assurance, external quality assurance and quality assurance agencies. According to the Ministry of

 Education and Training (MOET), all HEIs have to establish internal quality assurance units. Currently more

 than 75 percent of universities and 50 percent of colleges have internal quality assurance units. In the

 2011–2015 period, 90 percent of HEIs are expected to have undergone external evaluation.12 In the period

 of 2016–2020, 95 percent of HEIs and programs should have completed external evaluation and are

 considered for appraisal recognition and formal issuing of the certificate of accreditation. However, due

 to the limited capacity and personnel resources, this program is behind schedule.

 Vietnam National University of Agriculture

 10.     VNUA is one of the leading HEIs in training and research for agriculture and rural development.

 VNUA has produced 6 out of 13 national rice varieties and tens of national-level agriculture technology

 Decision No. 873/QD-TTg dated June 17, 2015 with an aim to become an internationally and regionally

 for disadvantaged students such as those from poor or ethnic minority backgrounds.



 Hanoi University of Science and Technology

 C. Higher Level Objectives to which the Project Contributes



         13.      The proposed project is directly aligned with the 2012-2016 World Bank Country Partnership

         Strategy (CPS) for Vietnam18 and contributes to two key outcomes. Competitiveness Outcome 1.3 seeks

         to increase Vietnam’s “capacity for innovation and value addition.?19 The project will strengthen the

         research and training capacity of these key universities as models of autonomous universities. It will help

         universities conduct more innovative and industry-relevant research projects that directly link with, and

         serve, industry and farmers to create value-added and competitive products. In addition, the improved

         teaching programs will help students learn skills needed for their jobs and find better and more relevant

         jobs. Improved management quality will help universities streamline their costs and improve investments

         to generate higher net income. The project will address governance through increasing institutional

         capacity, transparency, accountability, participation, citizen engagement and anti-corruption measures.



         14.      The proposed interventions under the Support for Autonomous Higher Education (SAHEP) are

         also aligned with the World Bank’s twin goals of eradicating extreme poverty and increasing shared

         prosperity. Relevant research and literature has documented the positive correlation between investment

         in human capital and social and economic development. Investments in education significantly contribute

         to poverty eradication and wealth creation, as higher educational attainment significantly increases the

         probability of getting a paid job and has a large and positive impact on monthly wage earnings.



         15.      The project is also well aligned with Vietnam’s 2011–2020 Socioeconomic Development Strategy

         (SEDS), especially the Breakthrough Area of “Promoting human resources/skills development (particularly

         skills for modern industry and innovation)? through its focus on improving the quality of research,

         teaching and management in three universities that specialize in science and applied technology

         programs.

 25.     Outcome area two: Improved teaching. This outcome will be achieved through the

   Box 2. Program Accreditation and Teaching

   Institutional and program accreditation, in line with global and regional best practice, has been identified by the

   GoV as the principal mechanism for quality improvement and quality assurance in the HE subsector. Well-

   executed accreditation of institutions and programs helps ensure that the conditions for teaching and learning

   are adequate and that teaching is aligned with modern pedagogical practices. Employer satisfaction surveys, an

   integral part of the accreditation process, help ensure the relevance of teaching, while student satisfaction

   surveys and student skills tests provide for feedback on teaching that is incorporated into teachers’ practice.

   Accreditation requires that programs are regularly updated and new programs piloted. International experience

   confirms that teaching quality improves most significantly through changing teachers’ practices in the classroom

   rather than in training programs removed from the teaching and learning environment. Most of the training

   supported in this project will help lecturers and university officials with the application of accreditation

   procedures in their institutions and programs.

C. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design



      39.      The project design draws heavily upon the lessons learned from the substantial World Bank-

      supported education program in Vietnam, as well as reflections on global experience relevant to Vietnam.

      This is the seventh HE support operation.23 The other five HE operations include three Higher Education

      Development Policy Operations that focused on the overall HE policy framework, Higher Education Project

      One (HEP1) and Higher Education Project Two (HEP2) that provided small grants to several universities,

      and the New Model University Project. The lessons learnt from operating these projects were reflected

      into the project implementation arrangements, the project’s focus on quality assurance mechanism

      through institutional and program accreditation, and institutional autonomy support.



      40.     Program design and implementation. The project is designed to have five independent

      implementing agencies. Participating universities are the project owners of their respective components

      and have their own Designated Accounts. This enables them to operationalize their autonomy and helps

      reduce approval layers in project management. Second, the project supports both policy improvement

      and university policy implementation. This will have a two-way impact on policies: (a) updated in a timely

      manner based on actual conditions of universities and (b) universities have an adequate legal framework

      to implement their autonomy mandates, and then lessons learned can be fed into policy revision, if

      needed. The project implementation is expected to be faster when the universities are both the project

      owners and beneficiaries than when the line ministries were the project owners and the universities are

      beneficiaries as with previous higher education projects.



      41.      Institutional and program accreditation. The project’s quality assurance activities take into

      account the lessons learned from an overview of good international practices, including Tajikistan,

      Armenia, Estonia, the Netherlands, and the Russian Federation. Overall, internationally recognized public

      and private quality assurance agencies are generally independent from the Government. However, in

      countries where centralized systems are transitioning to a more decentralized and autonomous system,

      quality assurance is often not exclusively conducted through independent bodies but delegated to the

      Ministry of Education. International experience suggests that under such circumstances it is often more

      feasible and effective to create a quality assurance framework aligned with international standards under

      the ministry’s umbrella, at least for a transitional period. In the case of Vietnam, while the accreditation

      centers have been established as autonomous entities, the Ministry is providing clear and strong

      leadership in the form of standards and guidelines, while investing resources in strengthening the capacity

      of the centers.



      42.      Institutional autonomy. Experience in India, South Korea, South Africa and Organization for

      Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries has demonstrated a range of benefits from

      assigning additional authority over academic affairs, human resources and financial matters to

      universities. A key lesson from Bangladesh is the importance of investing in human, financial and

      institutional capacity as part of the decentralization process, and to approach the delegation of authority

      in a phased manner. In this way, stronger institutions can move forward and provide lessons that can be

 Economic Analysis

 56.      The main objectives of SAHEP are to improve the quality of teaching and enhance research and

 management capacity in the selected universities. A cost-benefit analysis (CBA) framework was employed

 to determine if the proposed activities are economically justified. CBA approaches were conducted. The

 first explored the possible direct impact of the interventions being envisioned on increasing the skills and

 employability of graduates and, ultimately, on their lifetime earnings. The second evaluated the return to

 research and development (R&D) expenditure. Further details are available in annex 4 for detailed

 discussions of the methodologies and estimation results.



 CBA for Improvement in the Skills and Employability of Graduates



 57.     One assumption is that the university students should benefit from improved teaching and

 learning environment and better research capacity of the three universities. As a result, future graduates

 of these universities should possess higher level of skills and have higher lifetime earnings streams. The

 results of the economic analysis demonstrate that the project is expected to yield positive net present

 values (NPVs) that are greater than the real cost of capital of 5 percent in all of the scenarios considered

 in the sensitivity analysis. The NPV is between US$67.3 million and US$704.9 million depending on the

 scenario used.



 CBA for R&D Investment



 58.      The objective of the second CBA approach is to determine the contribution of public R&D

 investment to the economy’s output by estimating the return to each dollar invested in R&D activities.

 The study seeks to measure the contribution of public R&D expenditure to economic growth, which is the

 more relevant parameter for the economic analysis conducted here for SAHEP. Even though the CBA

 exercise conducted shows very high rate of return estimates to each dollar invested in public R&D

 activities (yielding NPVs in the range of US$2.5 and US$3.2), R&D investments are inherently risky and

 past returns may not be a good predictor of future returns. Nevertheless, the high rates of return observed

 for the eight economies25 in the sample over the 1996-2014 period seem sufficient to justify the

 investment and do explain why advanced economies possessing ample stock of knowledge are

 significantly richer than the less advanced ones.

B. Technical



      59.      SAHEP supports the Government’s key education priority—a HE system that is sustainable,

      flexible, responsive, and aligned with labor market and future human resource requirements of Vietnam.

      SAHEP will contribute to higher quality research and teaching and more effective and sustainable

      institutional management in three autonomous science and applied technology universities, and generate

      lessons that can be applied to the wider HE system.



      60.      The project design incorporates international trends in HE quality enhancement and assurance to

      strengthen university autonomy and management for a more sustainable, robust and resilient system.

      The formalization, support and monitoring of research groups at HE institutions will raise the overall

      effectiveness of research, especially in the new laboratories to be constructed under the project. Research

      weeks will help close the gap between research and teaching in a campus-wide activity at which

      researchers and the community interact. The annual AIR will bring together the different ways that each

      institution in the project is using the increased autonomy under the new autonomy policies to improve

      research, teaching, and management.



      61.      The project design also builds on international experience in improving the quality of teaching in

      HE institutions. SAHEP will improve the quality and relevance of learning programs by supporting

      universities to improve the learning environment and implement program accreditation initiatives that

      ensure that teaching personnel are (a) are using on the most up to date course contents and pedagogy;

      (b) are engaged in frequent reflection on their teaching; (c) receive regular feedback from students and

      employers on the quality and relevance of their teaching and subject matters; and (d) are required to

      frequently revisit their teaching programs in light of peer review and assessment.



      62.    The lessons learned from the three selected universities will feed into the MOET review of the

      autonomy pilot universities and help strengthen governance, financing, and quality assurance policies and

      key subsystems that are critical for building a higher quality, more relevant, sustainable and competitive

      HE system that will produce the level and quality of high-level human capital required for realizing

      Vietnam’s development vision.



      63.      SAHEP also incorporates a stronger focus on gender issues in HE, and will provide for a more

      systematic disaggregation of all relevant data by sex. The enhanced monitoring of key gender specific

      indicators will provide a basis for more systematic and targeted interventions to reduce gender disparities

      in both enrollment and research and teaching personnel. Citizen engagement in MOET consultations on

      policy review of university autonomy will be monitored and reported.

F. Environment (including Safeguards)



      74.     The project triggers three environmental safeguards policies, namely Environmental Assessment

      (OP 4.01), Physical Cultural Resources (OP 4.11) and Pest Management (OP 4.09). The project is proposed

      as Category B for environmental assessment, given the project’s location and the nature of activities for

      which the project is envisaged not to generate significant environmental adverse impacts.



      75.      The project will mainly involve the following investments and activities that have potential

      environmental impacts: (a) construction of lecture rooms, laboratories, internal road and drainage

      system; (b) operations of the laboratories, including the use and disposal of chemicals and waste water

      discharged; and (c) possible use of pesticides in the agriculture research. It is anticipated that education

      infrastructure development including new construction and upgrading of classrooms, administrative and

      working offices, and laboratories at a small to medium scale under Component 1 will cause construction

      related impacts such as noise; dust; vibration; construction wastes; and health and safety of workers,

      faculty, staff and students. Potential impacts during operation may include domestic wastewater and solid

      wastes, chemical wastes from laboratories, and potential use of synthetic pesticides in plant research.

      During project implementation the universities will undertake activities to enhance quality and relevance

      of research that might have potential environmental and social implications, risks and impacts, and

      therefore, are subject to World Bank safeguard policies where applicable.



      76.     Environmental and Social Management Plan. To assess the overall environmental impacts of the

      project investments and propose mitigation measures, the three Component 1 project owners (VNUA,

      HUST, and IUH) each prepared an ESMP that includes measures to address the identified environmental

      and social impacts caused during the pre-construction, construction, and operations phases, including

      waste water and solid waste management. The ESMP also includes guidelines for pesticide purchases and

      uses for research purposes, occupational and community health and safety requirements, safe handling,

      management and disposal of hazardous laboratory chemicals and synthetic pesticides, and trainings of

      farmers/farm workers and researchers. The ESMP includes requirements for screening of the research

      and studies to determine their environmental and social impacts and mitigation measures during project

      implementation.



      77.     Pest Management. The project may finance procurement of small amounts of pesticides for

      agriculture research, posing potential health risks for researchers and communities. The ESMP includes

      guidelines for pesticide purchases and uses for research purposes and trainings of farmers/farm workers

      and researchers.



      78.     Physical Cultural Resources. The project implementation of the IUH subcomponent will require

      relocating 31 graves and thus the ESMP for IUH has included measures to address the impact. Also, all

      three ESMPs have included an Environmental Code of Practice (ECOP) that covers a chance find procedure

      to address issues related to physical cultural resources (PCRs) encountered during construction.

 1.      The project development objective is to improve research, teaching, and institutional capacity at

 selected autonomous universities and strengthen the national higher education management system. The

 project has two components.



 Component 1: Improved research, teaching, and institutional management capacity at three selected

 autonomous universities (US$157.00 million equivalent, of which IDA US$140 million equivalent).

 2.      This component is divided into three subcomponents, one subcomponent for each university.

 These universities face particular challenges in that the costs of basic facilities and equipment for applied

 science and technology in industry and agriculture have high initial costs that are beyond the reach of

 their current revenue generating capacity, especially in light of the expansion in enrollment that has not

 been matched by increases in investments in physical, technical, human and institutional resources. Each

 university has its own characteristics. While HUST is a leading research-based institution in science and

 technology, VNUA focuses on teaching and research in agriculture, and IUH is strong on teaching applied

 knowledge for industry. Table 1.1 provides an overview of the universities.

 30.      Subcomponent 2.1: Strengthening the national accreditation system (US$1.20 million

 equivalent, of which IDA US$1.00 million equivalent). The GoV is pursuing a policy of devolving increasing

 autonomy to universities and at the same time strengthening the quality of its institutions through

 building a national accreditation system. Presently national accreditation is provided through four centers

 of education accreditation (CEAs) that have evolved from different initiatives. The Government has

 recently established a new set of standards for accreditation that these CEAs are currently

 operationalizing. There is considerable need for strengthening the capacity of the CEAs to implement the

 new standards and strengthen their ties with regional and global accreditation networks. Support for

 these centers will include acquisition of hardware, software and equipment for tracking status of

 institutions under accreditation. The accreditation information systems will be linked to the HEMIS and

 include center-specific data relating to progress against accreditation standards.



         (a)   2.1.1 Capacity building on HE quality assurance. A core group of 30 experienced accreditors

               from the CEAs will be established and trained to become master trainers in HE quality

               assurance. These master trainers from CEAs will later provide cascaded training for about

               2,000 qualified university accreditors in HE institutional accreditation, program

               accreditation. There will be technical assistance with the support of international experts

               from AUN/ASEAN Quality Assurance Network.



         (b)   2.1.2 Development of training materials on the new accreditation standards. With the

               support of national and international experts, the core group of accreditors from the four

               CEAs will develop training materials on HE institutional and program accreditation according

               to the newly adopted accreditation standards. These materials will be used in capacity-

               building activities including face-to-face workshops and self-paced e-learning modules. A

               manual for developing a culture of internal quality assurance for HEIs will also developed, to

               be used as a self-assessment checklist for HEIs before initiating the accrediting process.



         (c)   2.1.3 Conducting HEI institutional and program accreditation. The four CEAs in

               collaboration with a network of 2,000 trained accreditors will start the process of HEIs

               institutional and program accreditation to meet the agreed targets. All the participating

               universities will work with the CEAs to carry out their institutional and program accreditation

               process and monitor the progress against the agreed intermediate result indicators.

      1.      Management structure. VNUA, HUST, and IUH are each responsible for implementing their own

      respective subcomponents under Component 1. MOET is responsible for the implementation of all of the

      subcomponents of Component 2 except the eLibrary subcomponent, which will be implemented by the

      NEU. The MOET is also responsible for overall monitoring and coordination of the project implementation.



      2.       Each of the project owners will establish an appropriate PMU, headed by a project manager who

      will have primary responsibilities (including procurement, FM and technical activities) for the execution

      of their respective subcomponent. These PMUs will report project progress of their subcomponent and

      component to their line ministry (VNUA reports to MARD, HUST, and NEU reports to MOET, and IUH

      reports to MOIT) and the World Bank, copied to the PMB located in MOET. The PMB will compile reports

      and prepare a biannual consolidated project implementation report, to present to the GoV and the World

      Bank.



      3.      MOET will ensure close coordination and collaboration among project implementers and relevant

      GoV agencies (including the Office of the Government, the Ministry of Planning and Investment, Ministry

      of Finance, MOIT, and Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development) and other people and entities

      involved with the project and will report the overall project progress to the World Bank and related GoV

      agencies.



      4.      PMB. A Project Management Board (PMB) for all education projects under MOET has been

      established in August 2016. It is planned that this PMB will be in charge of the oversight of SAHEP from

      the Government side. The PMB will provide inputs to annual implementation plans and consolidate

      project reports submitted by subproject implementing universities to generate the overall SAHEP M&E

      reports. At the same time, the PMB will directly implement activities under the MOET component. The

      Information and Communication Technology Department of MOET will be consulted for technical issues

      of the to-be-developed HEMIS. The project will share the cost of project management (with other MOET

      projects) to be done by the PMB core teams for procurement, FM, administration, M&E and safeguards.

              (a)   Coordination with the World Bank, MOET leadership and concerned MOET’s departments

                    national level for overall implementation guidance, institutional arrangements, and

                    technical guidance/support;



              (b)   Implementation of the PMB activities, including, but not limited to, major technical

                    assistance contracts, training events, procurement of goods, civil works, and services,

                    training, technology transfers, and so on for sub-components of MOET;



              (c)   Management of the DA at MOET and of fiduciary duties including annual financial audits;

Procurement

     22.    PCRA. The capacity assessment of the IAs (MOET PMB, NEU, HUST, VNUA and IUH) was conducted

     and updated by the Bank team during the project preparation process (August 2016 - February 2017). This

     assessment has resulted in the following findings:



              (a)   MOET PMB. This is a central PMU under MOET, established recently in August 2016.

                    Currently, MOET was managing more than 60 projects funded by the government budget

                    and many other projects/programs financed by the international development partners

                    (Asian Development Bank [ADB], World Bank, Danish International Development Agency

                    (DANIDA) of Denmark, Italy, and so on). Gradually, the PMB will be assigned either the role

                    of investment owner or coordinator of those ongoing projects and the ones under

                    formulation of MOET. For the proposed project, SAHEP, the PMB will be the investment

                    owner of the MOET respective component. Although the PMB itself has not yet accumulated

                    any substantial procurement experience due to its recent establishment, the PMB staff in

                    their previous jobs have experience in project management and procurement

                    implementation in the Government-funded projects. They are familiar with national bidding

                    procedures regulated by the Government. International bidding following the requirements

                    of the multilateral development bank remains a challenge for the PMB officers.



              (b)   HUST. The University has experience in managing the projects financed by the Government

                    and several international financial institutions such as the World Bank, Korean International

                    Cooperation Agency (KOICA), and Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA). HUST has

                    conducted procurement of goods, civil works, and consultancy following the Government’s

                    and international financiers’ procurement regulations, where the most commonly used

                    procurement methods are national competitive bidding and Shopping. They implemented

                    two international competitive bidding (ICB) procurements of goods in 2009 under a World

                    Bank-funded education project (HEP2). The values of those ICB contracts were from

                    US$300,000 to US$500,000 each.



              (c)   VNUA. The university has implemented the projects financed by the Government and

                    various international financiers such as the World Bank, EU Commission, Belgium, DANIDA,

                    JICA, USAID, and NGOs. They are familiar with national competitive bidding, Shopping, and

                    IC methods. Some ICB goods packages were procured under the World Bank-financed HEP2

                    project in 2010; however, the contract values were rather small (from US$200,000 to

                    US$350,000 each). The price of the largest civil works contract procured by VNUA is US$3.5

                    million, funded by the state budget.



              (d)   NEU. It has experience in procurement of contracts financed by the Government, World

                    Bank, JICA, DANIDA, and Canadian International Development Agency (CIDA). They carried

                    out ICB procurements of some goods contracts ranging from about US$90,000 to US$3

                    million each during 2004-2011 under two World Bank-funded education projects (HEP1 and

                    HEP2).



              (e)   IUH. The university has been responsible for implementation of procurement activities

                    funded by the Government and the university budgets, following the Government

                    procurement regulations. The most commonly used methods are national competitive

 27.      The market assessment in the PPSD reveals the following key findings:



          •     Civil works are not complex and national contractors are capable and experienced in such

                works implementation, in addition experience shows that the International contractors are

                not often interested in such works. The Borrower therefore proposed RFB method with

                single stage one envelope, open competitive approach to both the national and

                international markets for Works procurement.



          •     The research and training equipment (e.g. lab equipment) to be procured under the project

                is available in the domestic market, however the majority are manufactured abroad and

                imported into Vietnam, the volume and value of equipment are large (from US$5 million to



                                                                                                              Page 57 of 96

               US$10 million for each category or group of equipment). National companies covered in the

               survey demonstrate past experience in supplying goods contracts of around US$1 million to

               US$2 million. According to the World Bank’s statistical data on its portfolio in Vietnam during

               the years 2012-2014, foreign companies in general are not interested in a goods contract of

               less than US$3 million, which is also the Bank’s current indicative international bidding

               threshold for goods in Vietnam. Given these facts RFB single stage, international open

               competitive bidding for specialized equipment serving research and training is suggested.



         •     The IT hardware and software required for MOET and NEU’s subcomponents can be supplied

               by the national bidders, given their track record of implementing similar scope of works.



         •     Regarding e-materials for e-Library subcomponent under NEU’s management, there are

               quite many international suppliers (for example, publishing houses/companies) operating in

               this area, and some of them have authorized the Vietnamese companies as their agents with

               technical support capacity. However, the information provided by the NEU about national

               market is limited to a few companies that have implemented the contracts with values much

               less than the cost estimate of the e-material package under this project. Therefore, the

               international market approach is suggested for this package.



         •     For separate items of civil works and goods of similar nature, bids should be invited for

               combination of lots (that is, slice and package procedure) to encourage the participation of

Environmental and Social (including Safeguards)



      37.      Social and resettlement safeguards. Construction of a new campus for IUH will require the

      acquisition of land of 263 households and affect 68 renters. An RPF and an RAP were prepared detailing

      how land acquisition, compensation, support, and resettlement will be carried out. A PMU under the

      management of IUH will be established and will be responsible for the implementation of resettlement

      activities through the Resettlement Committee of District 12. IUH is responsible for providing sufficiently

      and timely budget for resettlement and supervising the implementation. The PMU will conduct regular

      internal monitoring and recruit an external organization to conduct periodical independent monitoring of

      implementation to ensure that the campus and resettlement site complied with the agreed RAP.

      Monitoring reports will be submitted to IUH and the World Bank every 6 months.



      38.      Although the Resettlement Committee of District 12 has already implemented resettlement for

      the World Bank- and Asian Development Bank-funded projects, training on the implementation of RAP

      for all staff of the Resettlement Committee and IUH is still needed and shall be carried out at an early

      stage of the project implementation.



      39.      Gender. Gender analysis was carried out during the SA and indicated that among affected

      households, men are more involved in income generation activities (IGA) than women. Only 22.4 percent

      of women are involved in IGA, mostly in the business sector, and work as housewives. Different from

      women, men are more engaged in the area of business in the role of business owner, involved in retailed

      businesses, or work as governmental officers. One of the stereotypes regarding the role of men and

      women in the family is that while women are typically expected to take care of family, including cooking,

      child care, and other housework, men are typically responsible for earning income and participating in

      social activities outside the family. In the context of the project area where there is a fast transition from

      a rural to an urban area, although men and women appear to have an equal role with regard to income

      generation, child care, and social activities, the survey indicates that women still spend most of their time

      doing household work compared to men.



      40.     With regard to income generation, men still dominate jobs that require manual/heavy work and

      works that require more social interaction with clients. As the survey indicated, men contribute to 51–

      100 percent of family income. This evidence roughly matches the labor distribution, where men are more

      involved in income generation whereas women are more involved in housework.



      41.      Actions. The subproject of the IUH campus affects business of 38 households due to land

      acquisition, where 22.4 percent of women are working and there are 14 women-headed households to

      be affected by the subproject. To ensure that affected women are treated equally as men in

      compensation, livelihood restoration, and job opportunity, consultation and participation following

      actions should be implemented during project implementation: (a) besides compensation, women-

      headed households will be provided a special allowance of not less than VND 3 million per household to

      mitigate their vulnerability; (b) 60 percent of affected women shall participate in public consultations to

      ensure that actions are gender informed and responsive; (c) affected women shall be prioritized to work

      in the subproject with equal payment as men for the same job to improve income of their families; (d)

      women shall be involved in livelihood restoration program to ensure that their income sources have been

      restored and improved after the project; and (e) land use right certificate issuing for relocated households

      shall include the names of both the husband and wife to ensure equal land ownership. Implementation

 Environmental



 42.     The project triggers three environmental safeguards policies, namely Environmental Assessment

 (OP 4.01), Physical Cultural Resources (OP 4.11), and Pest Management (OP 4.09). The project is proposed

 as Category B for environmental assessment, given the project’s location and the nature of activ ities for

 which the project is envisaged to not generate significant adverse environmental impacts.



 43.     The project would bring positive impacts as it helps improve the training, research, and

 management quality of the three universities by developing infrastructure; innovation of training

 programs; and strengthening of international cooperation through training exchange and scientific

 research on environmental protection, new materials, materials for renewable energy, and sustainable

 agriculture.



 44.      The project would mainly involve the following investments and activities that have potential

 environmental impacts: (a) construction of lecture rooms, laboratories, and internal roads and drainage

 systems; (b) operation of the laboratories, including the use and disposal of chemicals and discharged

 wastewater; (c) use of pesticides in the conducted agricultural pilots. It is anticipated that education

 infrastructure development, including new construction and upgrading of classrooms, administrative and

 working offices, internal roads and drainage systems, and laboratories at a small to medium scale under

 Component 1 will cause typical construction-related impacts such as noise; air pollution due to dust and

 exhaust emission; vibration; construction waste including solid and liquid waste; traffic disturbance; and

 occupational health and safety risks of workers, faculty, staff, and students. Potential impacts during

 operation may include domestic wastewater and solid waste, chemical waste from laboratories, and

 potential risk of use of synthetic chemical pesticides in plant research and demonstration models. These

 models are anticipated to be at a small to moderate scale though.



 45.       Three ESMPs have been prepared, by each project owner (university). These ESMPs are part of

 the project feasibility study and have been prepared in accordance with the country’s environmental

 regulations and requirements of the World Bank’s OP/BP 4.01. The ESMPs have included (a) measures,

 that is, ECOP and specific mitigation measures to address the identified environmental and social impacts,

 including general and site-specific ones caused during the preconstruction, construction, and operations

 phases; (b) national guidelines for pesticide purchases and use for research purposes, including

 occupational and community health and safety requirements, safe handling, management and disposal

 of synthetic pesticides, and training of farmers/farm workers and researchers for subprojects, including

 use of pesticides; (c) the prudent practices in the laboratory to ensure laboratory safety for students and

 staff who work in the laboratory during project operation, (d) a Grievance Redress Mechanism; and (e)

 institutional arrangements for monitoring ESMP implementation. The ESMP for VNUA includes measures

 for compensation for farmers in case of failed demonstration activities.



 46.     PCRs. Project implementation for IUH will require relocating 31 graves. Thus, the ESMP for IUH

 has included measures to address the impact on 31 graves. In addition, all three ESMPs include ECOP that

 covers a chance find procedure to address issues related to PCRs encountered during construction.

 Strategy and Approach for Implementation Support



       Project Implementation Approach



       1.       The World Bank’s implementation support strategy for the project is aligned with implementation

       risks identified in the Systematic Operations Risk-Rating Tool (SORT) and builds on implementation

       experience of completed and ongoing education projects. IA risks, social and environment safeguard risks,

       and FM and procurement risks are considered Moderate to Substantial, based on fiduciary and safeguards

       assessments conducted during project preparation. While the PMB members are familiar with IDA

       projects, participating universities lack experience in implementing large projects. In addition, the lack of

       strong linkage between MOET, as an overall project coordinator, and participating universities, as

       independent IAs, may create difficulties for project M&E. To address these shortcomings, lessons learned

       from other completed and ongoing projects in education and other sectors have been reflected in the

       design to ensure the readiness of the IAs for project execution and to avoid to the extent possible

       inefficiency and delays during the implementation stage. Risks are identified in detail and the specific

       mitigation measures have been included in the SORT. TA supporting project implementation will be

       actively and intensively provided by the World Bank team, including provision for technical review of

       construction and equipment procurement proposals. These include the organization of multiple training

       courses on World Bank procedures for all project IAs, especially on procurement, FM, and safeguards.



       Task Team Project Supervision Approach



       2.      Close project supervision will be undertaken by a multidisciplinary team consisting of safeguards,

       fiduciary, and technical specialists based in Washington, DC and Hanoi offices. Most of the team members

       have been involved in supervision of education projects. Project supervision will continue emphasis on

       foreseeing future issues and realistic action plans. For the first two years of the project, besides routine

       semiannual supervision missions, interim missions will be conducted to address new issues. The task team

       leaders who are based in Vietnam will enable daily project implementation support.



Implementation Support Plan and Resource Requirements



       3.      The implementation support plan highlights the World Bank’s support to implementation of the

       risk mitigation measures in the SORT and to ensure achievement of the PDO. The World Bank’s

       implementation support plan consists of scheduled full supervision missions every six to twelve months

       and short review missions focusing on problem solving and timely follow-up monitoring. A midterm

       review conducted after about two and half years of implementation will identify and implement any

       structural changes. Enhanced fiduciary supervision is foreseen given the complexity of the operation,

       particularly at the start of the operation. Enhanced safeguard supervision may be needed during the first

       three years of the project when most of civil works and resettlement monitoring will constitute a

       substantial workload.

 Assessing the Return to R&D Expenditure

 86.     In the preceding section, we assumed that there will be no monetary benefits arising from other

 sources apart from the direct benefit accruing to graduates in the form of higher lifetime earnings plus

 externalities. More realistically, the increase in public R&D expenditure will result in greater research

 output, such as better seeds and fertilizers for farmers and product innovation for the manufacturing

 sector. The objective of this section is to determine the contribution of public R&D investment to the

 economy’s output by estimating the return to each dollar invested in R&D activities.

 87.      To accomplish this, the study in this section uses a growth accounting framework (Solow 1956;

 Swan 1956)32 to investigate the contribution of R&D expenditure to economic growth. Specifically, an

 endogenous growth model (Romer 1990)33 employed here assumes that the residual growth factor

 unaccounted for by the conventional capital and labor inputs is due to technological innovation created

 in the R&D sectors using the existing stock of knowledge, human capital, and R&D facilities. The R&D

 activities conducted by the business and the public sector are also separated. In particular, the

 contribution of public R&D expenditure to economic growth is measured, which is the more relevant

 parameter for the economic analysis conducted here for SAHEP.

 88.      The analysis focuses on fourteen economies (13 countries) in the ASEAN+3 group, which include

 Brunei Darussalam; Cambodia; Myanmar; Indonesia; Lao People’s Democratic Republic; Malaysia;

 Thailand; the Philippines; Singapore; Vietnam; China; Hong Kong SAR, China; Japan; and Democratic

 Republic of Korea. Out of the panel of 14 economies, only 8 have long enough GERD data series that can

 be used to estimate the key elasticity parameters using the econometric models explained in the following

 section. These are Malaysia; Thailand; the Philippines; Singapore; China; Hong Kong SAR, China; Japan;

 and Democratic Republic Korea. The business and public R&D intensity, defined as the ratio of GERD to

 GDP, for the 8 economies are presented in figure 4.5, where it can be seen clearly that the private business

 sector in each economy generally accounts for the bulk of total R&D spending.

 2.       The project is situated within the overall development vision of Vietnam to strengthen its HE

 system to produce the high-level skills and applied knowledge needed for economic growth and

 development in a highly competitive context. As part of this strategy, the GoV has incrementally

 granted a significant degree of autonomy to universities over academic, human resource, and

 financial matters. This project provides for investment in three selected autonomous universities

 that focus on science and applied technology to strengthen their physical, technical, human, and

 institutional capacity. This will permit the universities to improve the quality of teaching, research,

 and management, provide lessons for the wider HE system, and contribute to high-level human

 resources and knowledge. Furthermore, in line with the GoV strategy, greater autonomy will be given

 to these institutions as they show the aforementioned improvements.

 3.       The project has two components, the first targeting improved research, teaching, and

 management in three selected universities and the second strengthened higher education system to

 facilitate overall quality assurance and learning from the autonomy pilots.

 4.       Project outputs in the areas of research, teaching, and institutional management at the three

 universities and in key systems at the national level will be measured. Outputs at the university level

 consist of increases in physical resources (laboratories, classrooms, and so on); technical resources

 (equipment, software, and so on); human resources (training, TA, and so on); and institutional

 resources (committees, panels, research groups, accreditation processes, and so on). The key to the

 logical chain is the institutional arrangements that will help ensure that the physical and technical

 investments as well as the training and TA will be brought together in ways that international

 experience has demonstrated are more likely to have a positive impact on the quality of research

 and teaching. Successful countries, for instance, have used international peer review to strengthen

 the quality of research and accreditation programs to ensure the quality of teaching programs.

 5.      The intermediate outcomes, therefore, are a series of institutional arrangements and

 processes that will help ensure that the physical resources and the added human capacity are used

 in a way that strengthens research, teaching, and institutional management. Improved management

 and use of the autonomy that has been granted to universities will further enhance the quality of

 teaching and research, which will increase the output of higher-level skills and knowledge and

 innovation required by a rapidly developing society and economy. More successful universities will

 attract more financial and human resources, which will improve the universities’ autonomous status

 and thereby contribute to more improved research and teaching.

 6.      At the same time, certain outputs in terms of institutional capacity at the national level will

 position the wider system to benefit from the lessons emerging from the three participating

 universities. In the longer term, this will ensure that the wider system contributes more effectively

 to addressing the high-level human resources and innovation that the rapidly developing society

 requires.

 7.     Over the longer term, improved research, teaching, and management at universities that are

 extended based on experience to other universities as they move toward autonomy will help build a

 high-quality HE education system that meets the economic and development needs of Vietnam.
