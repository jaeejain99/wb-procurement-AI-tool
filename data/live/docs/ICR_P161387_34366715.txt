I. PROGRAM CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES



A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL AND THEORY OF CHANGE

   Context



   1. At appraisal in 2017, Kenya was experiencing a period of stable economic growth while undertaking reforms

      in public institutions. Progress on a range of governance reforms was accelerated by the Constitution 2010,

      which gave way to the decentralization process, with certain government functions, staff, and funds transferred

      to 47 counties. Concurrently, national ministries, departments, and agencies (MDAs) were restructured,

      oversight bodies like Parliament and the Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission (EACC) were strengthened, and

      the Office of the Controller of Budget (COB) and the Office of the Auditor General (OAG) were established as

      Supreme Audit Institutions from the defunct Office of the Auditor and Controller of the Budget. The Kenyan

      government also made improvements in national revenue administration and expenditure management through

      the enactment of the Public Financial Management (PFM) law in 2012 and attendant regulations in 2015.

   2. Despite notable progress, governance reforms did not fully translate into desired functional changes. Public

      sector management systems did not deliver on core functionalities. The main challenges included ineffective

      oversight and accountability, weak implementation and enforcement of laws and regulations, as well as

      inefficient public investment management (PIM) and procurement. Kenya’s 2012 Public Expenditure and

      Financial Accountability (PEFA) included thirteen C and nine D scores across various indicators. The country

      ranked 146 out of 176 countries in the 2016 Transparency International Global Corruption Index, indicating the

      persistence of rent seeking behavior in the public sector. Issue in public procurement, such as lack of integration

      of policies and systems, lack of transparency, and limited technical expertise, led to delays and cost overruns.

      Budget predictability was also a challenge as ineffective cash management systems undermined planning of

      expenditures for service delivery causing budget shortfalls and underfunding of MDAs. Finally, human resource

      data was fragmented and inconsistent, which limited decision making on the wage bill, human resource

      management and budgeting.

   3. The Program to Strengthen Governance for Enabling Service Delivery and Public Investment in Kenya (GESDeK)

      was designed to help the government address these challenges. The Program centered on policies and systems

      foundational for achieving the results desired under the Public Financial Management Reform Strategy (PFMRS)

      from 2018 to 2023. The main objective of the PFMRS was to ensure “a public finance system that promotes

      transparency, accountability, equity, fiscal discipline and efficiency in the management and use of public

      resources for improved service delivery and economic development.” PFMRS for 2018-2023 represented the

      first time that the Government of Kenya (GoK) was adopting a results-oriented outlook regarding PFM reforms.

   4. GESDeK introduced and piloted several policies and systems that constitute the cornerstones of a well-

      functioning PFM system. GESDeK promoted engagement and interventions across a wide range of foundational

      matters regarding each step of the PFM cycle from the macro-fiscal framework and the credibility of the budget

      to financial reporting and external oversight. Participating MDAs were incentivized to produce the inputs needed

      for the achievement of the desired results under the Program.

Rationale for PforR Support, and Program Scope and Boundaries



6. The PforR instrument was selected to incentivize GoK to focus on achieving impactful outcomes in PFM . The

   entrenched PFM and governance challenges required long-term engagement, which made Development Policy

   Financing (DPF) an unsuitable instrument at the time. Furthermore, the government had a robust results-

   oriented PFM reform program, for which adequate resources had been historically allocated from the national

   budget and complemented by technical assistance (TA) from the Kenya Accountable Devolution Program (KADP)

   multi-donor trust fund. Accordingly, an Investment Project Finance (IPF) was not deemed necessary nor

   requested by GoK during implementation. Lastly, the World Bank provided advisory support and policy dialogue

   during the formulation of the results-oriented PFMRS for 2018-2023, making a PforR aligned to the Strategy the

   appropriate instrument.

7. The Program boundaries were defined around six Results Areas (RAs) tracked through the achievement of 27

   disbursement-linked indicators (DLIs). The DLIs served as PDO indicators and intermediate indicators. The RAs—

   described in greater detail in the following sections—included: (i) prioritized public investments; (ii) reliable

   funding for service delivery and public investments; (iii) efficient and transparent procurement; (iv) consolidated

   staff data; (v) timely and quality financial statements and audits; and (vi) strengthened fiduciary assurance and

   transparency. Each RA contained key steps towards the achievement of improved functional performance of

   Kenya’s public finance system for effective service delivery, aligned with the PFMRS. GESDeK supported five out

   of the seven thematic areas of the PFRMRs, as shown in Figure 2.

Program Development Objectives (PDOs)

8. The Program's objective was to improve resource management utilization and transparency in selected service

   delivery MDAs. The PDO statement in the PAD and Financing Agreement were the same.



Key Expected Outcomes and Outcome Indicators

9. The Program-supported results areas were expected to lead to: (a) “improved utilization” and (b) “improved

   transparency” of resources management in selected MDAs. The key expected outcomes and related indicators

   are reflected in Figure 3. The DLIs at PDO level were designed to measure implementation of reforms by line

   MDAs responsible for service delivery and public investment, ensuring the functional deployment of the

   intended reforms. DLIs were developed along the results chains with some focusing on outputs and others closer

   to outcomes. The DLIs selected as PDO level indicators were those associated with the later years of the

   operation, thus more closely aligned with the outcome level.

Program Results Areas and DLIs

10. RA 1 aimed to strengthen public investment management. The associated DLI was “number of projects with

    capital allocations above KSH.100 million which were in compliance with procedures in PIM manual.” To achieve

    this target, activities under RA 1 included: (a) the development of a PIM manual which included project

    identification, appraisal, budgeting, implementation, reporting, and monitoring and evaluation (M&E); (b) the

    establishment of a dedicated PIM unit with basic staff in place and trained to perform the National Treasury (NT)

    gate-keeping role; and (c) the enhancement of Electronic Project Monitoring Information System (e-ProMIS) to

    deliver requirements of the PIM manual, with interface between e-ProMIS and Integrated Financial Management

    Information System (IFMIS) tested and live. The goal was to strengthen planning and oversight over public

    investment projects. Projects would be selected by MDAs based on sound appraisal criteria, alignment with

    economic and strategic priorities, and realistic projections of their returns. This in turn was expected to lead to

    higher returns on public investments with minimal delays and cost overruns, value-for-money, and strong

    accountability.

11. RA 2 supported efforts to strengthen cash planning and cash management. The associated DLIs are listed in

    Figure 3. Activities under RA 2 included: a) encouraging greater realism in revenue forecasting used in the

    budget; (b) supporting the preparation of borrowing plans consistent with delivering cash for MDAs based on

    their cash plans; (c) introducing a new cash management and exchequer system which ensured all GoK releases

    to MDA bank accounts were made automatically following MDA approvals in IFMIS; and (d) the development

      and implementation of guidelines that required that revised MDA cash plans protected service delivery and

      infrastructure budget priorities. The objective was to ensure efficient and effective budget utilization, accurate

      and timely accounting and reporting, and effective control, scrutiny, and review of expenditure of public

      resources.

  12. RA 3 focused on strengthening procurement and asset disposal functions in the public sector. The activities

      under RA 3 included upgrading and implementing the e-procurement system to meet the requirements of

      procurement entities, oversight institutions, and suppliers in accordance with relevant laws and regulations. It

      also supported the establishment of mechanisms for encouraging compliance with the Public Procurement and

      Asset Disposal Act (PPAD) Act of 2015 in the context of the upgraded system and greater transparency through

      the State Procurement Portal (SPP). Finally, it aimed to ensure that the e-procurement system and SPP were up

      to date and included appropriate information on tenders, awards, contract implementation, and complaints

      follow-up by disclosing procurement data and following Open Contracting Data Standards (OCDS).

  13. RA 4 supported efforts to consolidate staff data and strengthen payroll management. The associated DLI was

      “number of MDAs whose payroll data has been uploaded to Government Human Resource Information System

      (GHRIS) and are up to date.” To achieve this target, outputs included: (a) increased GHRIS functionality to handle

      consolidated human resource data from MDAs and interfacing with IFMIS; (b) the development and issuance of

      regulations and standards on itemized payroll details consistent with enhanced GHRIS functionality and sharing

      of data; (c) restructuring and uploading of data from different payroll systems across service delivery MDAs; and

      (d) publishing of basic wage bill data by administrative/service delivery unit, consistent with the Chart of

      Accounts (CoA). This would in turn enable sound decision-making on wage-bill and human resource

      management.

  14. RA 5 aimed to enhance the timeliness, quality, and relevance of financial statements and audit reports. The

      related interventions were: (a) implementation of risk-based audit strategies; (b) adoption of the audit

      management software; (c) revision of audit methodologies in line with international standards; (d) ability of the

      IFMIS to generate MDA financial statements; and (e) adoption of International Public Sector Accounting

      Standards (IPSAS) compliant financial statements by MDAs. The goal was to ensure that timely and quality audit

      reports would be presented in a manner that facilitated stakeholders’ ability to understand and scrutinize the

      impact on service delivery.

  15. RA 6 interventions aimed to strengthen fiduciary assurance and transparency. The supporting activities

      included: (a) MDAs, and increasingly the public, could access multi-year itemized and facility level budget and

      outturn data for all MDAs in searchable form through the budget module of IFMIS; and (b) updated manuals and

      Quality Assurance (QA) framework for internal audit would be in place to strengthen assurance and risk

      management. Achievement of these results would ensure that stakeholders inside and outside government

      would have access to appropriate and relevant information on service delivery. Information would be timely,

      reliable, trusted and used in decision-making and accountability processes.



B. SIGNIFICANT CHANGES DURING IMPLEMENTATION

  Revised PDOs Outcome Targets, Result Areas, and DLIs

  16. The Program was restructured on October 29, 2021. The PDO remained the same, but changes were made to

      select DLIs, DLRs, and associated targets and two new RAs were introduced. Other changes included the

      reallocation of disbursement amounts and a no-cost extension of the Program closing date by 12 months to

      December 31, 2023.

17. RA 1: Changes to DLI 1 (Prioritized Public Investments). The modifications under this RA included: (a) replacing

    references to the “PIM Manual” with “PIM Guidelines,” which were disseminated widely across MDAs, and

    constituted the foundation for the draft PIM Regulations, a Prior Action under a DPF series; (b) new actions for

    the completion of the inventory of public investment projects to address challenges regarding nonperforming and

    stalled projects in the development budget that became a Government priority under the focus on fiscal

    consolidation in the wake of the COVID-19 crisis; and (c) deleting references to e-ProMIS as the platform for the

    automation of the PIM process as GoK opted for a more advanced and suitable technology.

18. RA 2: Changes to DLI 2.1 (Reliable Funding for Service Delivery and Investment Projects), DLI 2.2 (Improved

    Revenue Projections), and DLI 2.3 (Reliability of Domestic Financing). DLI 2.2 and DLI 2.3 were deleted due to

    challenges regarding persistent difficulties in revenue forecasting in an uncertain fiscal environment. However,

    the substance of the related reforms was not lost as it was incorporated in the International Monetary Fund (IMF)

    program and the Inclusive Growth and Fiscal Management DPF (P168204). The modifications to DLI 2.1 were

    intended to align the next phase of actions to improve cash management practices anticipated under this RA with

    the provisions of the new Cash Management Framework that was developed by the NT as part of the PFMRS and

    with support from the Program. The new DLRs under DLI 2.1 aimed at leveraging the power of automation to

    consolidate the gains achieved and to institutionalize a more efficient process for cash planning and approval

    decisions within the NT.

19. RA 3: Changes to DLI 3 (Efficient and Transparent Procurement). Adjustments were made to the timeline for the

    intended number of MDAs using a full-fledged e-procurement system in compliance with the PPAD and following

    the OCDS. The modifications aimed to address the delays experienced in the formulation of the e-procurement

    roadmap and the procurement process for the development of the new e-GP platform.

20. RA 4: Changes to DLI 4 (Consolidated Staff Data). The modification was to adjust the timeline for the intended

    number of MDAs whose payroll data were uploaded to GHRIS. The modification addressed the delays experienced

    in the formulation of the roadmap for the development of GHRIS and the subsequent implementation of the

    roadmap.

21. RA 5: Changes to DLI 5.1 (Timely and Quality Financial Statements and Audits) . The targets for DLI 5.1 –

    percentage of MDAs for which the NT had reviewed the quality of annual financial statements generated from

    IFMIS and submitted to the OAG within four months, were increased from 30 to 40 percent for 2021, and 50

    percent for 2022. The change acknowledged the OAG’s swift and successful response to the COVID-19 pandemic

    by availing the Auditor General to perform a special audit of the COVID-19 funds spent by selected MDAs and all

    47 counties. A new DLR was introduced regarding special audits on the use of funds disbursed to MDAs for

    expenditures relating to programs to address the COVID-19 crisis. The restructuring also adjusted the number of

    months that the DLR expected to achieve between the receipt of consolidated financial statements and their

    submission to Parliament (from four to seven months in 2021 and three to five months in 2022) due to the delays

    experienced in the appointment of a new Auditor General that caused a backlog of pending audits.

22. RA 6: Changes to DLI 6.1 (Transparent Institutions) and DLI 6.2 (Strengthened Fiduciary Assurance and Risk

    Management). DLI 6.1, which looked at the number of MDAs for which information regarding program and

    project expenditure and transfers to service delivery units was publicly available, was revised to target

    information at program and sub-program levels for all MDAs, as well as at the service delivery unit level for one

    MDA. DLI 6.2, which targeted annual and quarterly MDA Internal Audit Reports being prepared and undergoing

    quality assurance in line with enhanced procedures for audit quality assurance, risk management, and follow up,

    was changed with increase in the original target from 20 to 25 MDAs. The modifications under this RA overall

    aimed at: (i) adjusting the timelines in DLRs under DLI 6.1 regarding the development of the citizen budget portal

    due to delays experienced in the development of the technical specifications for the platform; and (ii)

    institutionalizing the improvements achieved regarding the introduction of improved internal audit processes by

23. RA 7: New DLI 7 (Transparency and Oversight of State Corporations). Discrepancies between budget allocations

    and outcomes, as well as manual processes for budget management and reporting, were among the challenges

    affecting the governance, management, and oversight of State Corporations (SCs) in Kenya. Improving the

    governance of SCs was an increasingly pertinent topic in the context of the Kenya’s aspirations to become a

    middle-income country rooted in private sector-led growth. The new DLI 7 aimed at developing and introducing

    a Government Investment Management Information System (GIMIS) across SCs for the preparation and

    submission of data on corporate, budget, and statutory requirements of SCs.

24. RA 8: New DLI 8 (eCitizen as an Integrated Platform for Government Service Delivery). The Government Digital

    Payments (GDP) Unit within the NT ensured that citizens, non-citizens, and business entities had access to

    government services online and could make payments electronically and conveniently on the eCitizen platform,

    using their preferred method of payment, 24-hours a day and from wherever they may be in the world. GDP had

    an ambitious three-year plan to reengineer the eCitizen portal and onboard additional services onto the platform

    to make eCitizen the medium of choice for citizens and businesses to conduct their transactions with the

    Government. The new DLI 8 aimed at substantially increasing the number of public services onboarded on the

    eCitizen platform, which would be reengineered in line with citizen-centered design principles with Program

    support. The DLI would also support the formulation of a change management and communications strategy,

    regarding the scope, nature, introduction, and rollout of services offered on eCitizen. The overarching objective

    of the initiatives proposed for inclusion in the Program was to enhance the uptake of eCitizen by the public sector

    and citizens.

Rationale for Changes and their Implication for the Original Theory of Change

25. After the Mid-Term Review (MTR), the WB, AFD, and GoK agreed to changes that would enable the

    achievement of envisioned outcomes given early implementation challenges and a new strategic vision. The

    rationale for restructuring was primarily to: (i) better align GESDeK with the PFMRS for 2018-2023, considering

    that the Program had begun before the Strategy was completed; and (ii) respond to the government's economic

    recovery goals and higher ambition in the aftermath of the COVID-19 disruptions. The Program's duration was

    extended by one year to make up for the initial delays caused by the prolonged election period in 2017 due to

    the nullification and repeat of the presidential election. Additionally, the MTR revealed that certain actions and

    DLIs had not been achieved as planned. The underperformance of some RAs threatened the achievement of the

    Program objectives, especially those involving automation processes which had stalled due to delays in

    government procurement processes.

26. Achievement of the DLI regarding the introduction of IT systems to improve PFM business processes suffered

    delays. DLI 1c was off-track due to delays in launching the procurement process for enhancing the PIM system.

    Additionally, the intended enhancements to the e-ProMIS platform expected to operationalize the PIM guidelines

    and streamline the PIM process was deemed inadequate to respond to the functional requirements for a full-

    fledged Public Investment Management Information System (PIMIS). The NT decided to invest in developing a

    new PIMIS that would ensure the achievement of the related RA.

27. Automation initiatives for public procurement and HR management faced similar challenges. The consultations,

    review, and approval process for the e-Procurement Plan and Roadmap took longer than anticipated during the

    planning stage, impacting the achievement of DLIs that depended on the completion of this action (i.e., DLR 3b

       and DLR 3c.) Progress toward the achievement of DLR 4b was also limited due to the delays in the procurement

   28. Revenue collection actions were undermined by institutional challenges to revenue forecasting and the COVID-

   29. New thematic areas were introduced to strengthen the Program’s focus on enabling service delivery and

       facilitating accountability and transparency. The new RAs were: (i) Management Information System (MIS) for

       SCs; and (ii) expansion of eCitizen portal services. Improving the governance of SCs was critical for Kenya’s goals

       of private sector-led growth and provision of quality public services to citizens and businesses. The introduction

       of eCitizen would enable citizens and businesses to perform many widely used, fee-based transactions online,

       which would reduce fraudulent invoicing for government transactions, resulting in revenue growth for the

   30. These changes led to a satisfactory implementation progress after the MTR . The alignment of the PFMRS for

       2018-2023 with the GESDeK RAs guaranteed adequate resource allocation and financing to achieve the desired

       reforms. Secondly, government prioritized and provided the authorizing environment, appropriate coordination,

       and monitoring framework – described later in the M&E section – that accelerated the achievement of results.



II. OUTCOME



A. RELEVANCE

   Relevance of PDO

   31. The PDO is rated as highly relevant to Kenya’s PFM reform efforts and is aligned with the current Country

       Partnership Framework (CPF) for Kenya (FY23-28). Objectives 1 and 2 of the CPF are “boosting Kenya’s fiscal and

       debt sustainability” and “improving public expenditure transparency and effectiveness.” The CPF corresponds to

       measures that will increase domestic revenue mobilization and strengthen the mechanisms for resource

       allocation and spending. It is aligned with Kenya’s PFM reform momentum since NT established the PFM Reform

       Program in 2006. The reforms in consecutive budget cycles aimed to contribute to fiscal sustainability, strengthen

       financial governance in the public sector, use the budget as a tool to reduce poverty levels, increase transparency

       and accountability in the management of public funds, and improve service delivery to the citizens.

   32. The Program objectives were strongly aligned with the PFM Reform Strategy of 2018 to 2023 and reinforced in

       the new PFMRS for 2023 to 2028. The new strategy focuses on the following results areas: (a) sustainable and

       predictable fiscal space to deliver government programs; (b) strategic and transparent spending on public

       investment and service delivery in line with national and county policy commitments; (c) reliable cash for service

       delivery and public investments; (d) value for money in procurement and contract management; (e) value for

       money, performance, and accountability in staffing for service delivery; (f) effective management of public

       resources in education and health institutions and other service facilities; (g) disciplined financial management;





                                                                                                             Page 12 of 59

    The World Bank

    Program to Strengthen Governance for Enabling Service Delivery and Public Investment in Kenya (GESDeK)

    (P161387)







      and (h) accurate reporting and accountability delivered through audit, oversight, and follow-up. The strategy

      emphasizes automation as a crosscutting enabler underpinning these RAs and mainstreaming climate and gender

      considerations across the PFM cycle.

  33. GESDeK paved the way for a new PforR operation, GESDeK II (P180287), approved by the Board in December

      2023. GESDeK II builds on the achievements registered under GESDeK and will constitute the principal

      government program supporting the implementation of the PFMRS for 2023–2028. The strategy encompasses

      several dimensions of government-wide institutional efficiency, accountability, and transparency across the

      public sector, while maintaining the result-oriented outlook introduced under its predecessor. It incentivizes the

      institutionalization of reforms regarding revenue mobilization, expenditure efficiency and accountability,

      government coordination, and external oversight, among other priorities. This is a testament that the PDO

      remains highly relevant for GoK’s PFM reform agenda.

  Relevance of DLIs

  34. The relevance of DLIs to the achievement of the Program objectives is rated as Substantial. Prior to

      restructuring, GESDeK comprised 27 DLIs, of which 16 were IRIs, and 11 were output and outcome indicators.

      While the output and outcome indicators were scalable, the intermediate result indicators were foundational.

      They referred to the interventions necessary to bring about the expected change and were scheduled to be

      achieved during the first year of implementation. Delays or failure to deliver the IRIs increased the risk of failure

      to efficiently achieve PDO because of knock-on effects.

  35. While the large number of DLIs created implementation risks, this approach was necessary to establish the

      foundations of a robust PFM system. The level of detailed engagement and comprehensive scope was necessary

      at the time for two reasons: the breadth of the reforms that the government wanted to achieve under the reform

      strategy and the fact that it was adopting a result-oriented focus for the first time. As part of this approach, the

      participating MDAs were incentivized to produce the inputs needed for the achievement of the desired results.

      While necessary, the number of DLIs did prove challenging during implementation, which was a lesson that

      informed the design of GESDeK II, defined by fewer DLIs.

  Rating of Overall Relevance



  36. Based on the assessment above, the overall relevance is rated as High.

III. KEY FACTORS THAT AFFECTED IMPLEMENTATION AND OUTCOME



A. KEY FACTORS DURING PREPARATION

  52. A key factor during preparation that affected implementation was that GESDeK was the first-ever PFM PforR

      in Kenya. The novelty of the instrument led to a mismatch in expectations and limited understanding of the PforR

      concept created weaknesses in work programming, planning, and coordination at Program inception. To address

      this capacity gap, the Bank provided TA to facilitate the development of work plans. Furthermore, the timing of

      the Program design coincided with the drafting of the PFMRS for 2018-2023. As a result, GESDeK had to be initially

      anchored under its predecessor, which was more input-driven compared to the output orientation key for the

      success of a PforR. The Bank team navigated this challenge by continuously sensitizing GoK on the main elements

      of a PforR, thereby charting a path to a better understanding of the instrument and achievement of results.

 B. KEY FACTORS DURING IMPLEMENTATION

  53. Factors subject to the control of government and/or implementing entities. Overall, the Program’s success and

      achievements can be attributed in part to the NT, whose performance was strong across the life of the Program,

      as well as the strong engagement with the responsible units of Ministry of Public Service, Performance and

      Delivery Management, and the OAG. The Program Coordinator ensured effective coordination and timely inputs

      from different sectoral focal points. The main challenges the Government faced were related to delays at the

      onset of the Program that had a knock-on effect on planned interventions. The slow start to GESDeK was mainly

      due to an extended election period in 2017. The Program became effective in the middle of the fiscal year when

      the budget and workplans had already been prepared and the budget passed by Parliament. Thus, the Program

      effectively started after one year, which impacted the achievement of results planned in the first year.

  54. Factors subject to World Bank control. The project benefited from the fact that both Task Team Leaders (TTLs)

      were field-based, and one remained the same throughout implementation. Regular missions were conducted,

      and strong relationships were established with government counterparts. This allowed the team to interact

      regularly with NT, which facilitated the successful implementation of the Program. The World Bank task team

      was able to work closely with the GoK and rapidly respond to emerging issues. Relatedly, a key factor that played

      a role in the success of the Program was the timely restructuring at the MTR.

  55. Factors outside the control of government and/or implementing entities. The COVID-19 pandemic created

      delays in achieving the DLIs scheduled for the first two years of the project. The containment measures

      implemented to fight the COVID-19 pandemic led to delays in implementing some planned activities, particularly

      those related to capacity building, development of work plans, and engagement with stakeholders. Engagement

      with key stakeholders was carried out mostly through virtual meetings.

A. QUALITY OF MONITORING AND EVALUATION

   M&E Design

  56. The M&E arrangements were robust. They included five key tools, namely: (a) the Results Framework (RF); (b)

      Results Team Reporting; (c) the Program Action Plan (PAP); (d) IVA for DLIs; and (e) implementation support

      missions and other diagnostic exercises. The PFM Reform Secretariat at the NT coordinated the implementation

      of PFM reforms. To effectively implement GESDeK, Result Teams, responsible for the achievement and reporting

      of specific DLIs, were introduced in the governance structure. Ernst & Young (EY) LLP Kenya was contracted by NT

      strengthened by several key initiatives including: (a) development of the GESDeK POM that outlined the strategies,

      rules, procedures, and guidance for the implementation of the PforR; (b) development of the M&E Framework for

      the PFMRS for 2018-2023; and (c) formulation of a Communication Strategy (CS) for 2018-2023 outlining the

  M&E Implementation

 57. Reporting data for the RF indicators was done throughout the Program's life. The selected performance

     indicators were tracked regularly, and the RF was updated promptly during supervision missions. The POM

     outlined the detailed verification protocol, checklists, and supporting evidence required for the IVA process, which

     enabled the quality of the independent verification practices. The IVA’s approach included: (i) statements of the

     validity of supporting evidence, methodology, and tasks undertaken by the IVA; (ii) confirmation of the application

     of DLI protocols by implementing agencies and statement of the activities carried out by the IVA to do so; (iii)

     confirmation of whether or not DLRs had been achieved or whether the IVA was unable to reach a conclusion on

     the achievement of the results; and (iv) statement of any outstanding issues, responsibilities, timelines, and

     documentation of the process of resolving any issues with implementing agencies. In addition to the IVA process,

     implementation support missions were conducted annually by the World Bank and AFD. To strengthen the Result

     Team mechanism, the PFMRS engaged a Program Coordinator at the Secretariat to coordinate, support, and work

     with each Result Team, including planning, budgeting, monitoring, and reporting on the achievement of the DLIs.

  M&E Utilization

 58. Data was made available regularly for the RF and was used to inform the analysis for this ICR . The first IVA report

     was produced covering two financial years, 2017/18 and 2018/19, and formed the basis for disbursement of

     Program funds for 2020/21. Thereafter, three IVA reports were produced through the life of the Program. The

     confirmation of achievement of results was the basis for disbursement of funds. While utilization was satisfactory,

     some measurement challenges were observed. For instance, in the beginning of implementation, the full

     automation of the cash management function was delayed, and the institutional arrangements for cash

     management did not work as programmed. The IVA report concluded that a lack of relevant data hampered the

     measurement of DLR2.3 b and DLR2.1(c), which led to restructuring of this DLR. Despite these minor challenges,

     data collection and availability were adequate thought the operation.

C. BANK PERFORMANCE

  Quality at Entry

 63. The quality at entry was Satisfactory. As mentioned in previous sections, this was the first PFM PforR for the NT,

     the OAG, and the State Department for Public Service (SDPS) in Kenya. The unfamiliarity with the PforR model

     resulted in a slower initial uptake and a lag in achieving results during the first year. The selection of DLIs could

     have been further simplified, especially given the implementing agencies' first PforR experience. However, a

     comprehensive set of DLIs represented an opportunity to establish the foundational elements of a robust PFM

     system. Additionally, the design could have anticipated some of the challenges regarding the difficulty in procuring

     IT systems and tying DLIs to the legislative process. The Bank team included these as difficult but critical areas to

     achieve substantial PFM reforms and mitigated some of these challenges by providing extensive technical

     assistance and close implementation support to the GoK.

  Quality of Supervision

 64. Quality at supervision was Satisfactory. The Bank task team conducted regular implementation support missions

     and was able to quickly resolve issues as they emerged. During COVID-19, access to the client was limited, but was

     resolved with virtual meetings. After the MTR the Program was restructured reflecting sound judgement and a

     timely response in addressing challenges that threatened to undermine achievement of results. The Program

     progressed from Moderately Satisfactory at mid-term to Satisfactory at closing. This was due to strong technical

     assistance from various development partners, including the World Bank, who formed a PFM Sector Donors

     Working Group, and the implementing agencies’ eventual grasp of the PforR instrument as well as their ownership

     of the results given GoK’s prioritization of the reforms in the PFMRS for 2018-2023.

 D. RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME

  66. The risk to development outcome is low. GESDeK established essential foundations for the implementation and

      performance monitoring structure to deepen PFM reforms across MDAs. The government has a robust results-

      oriented PFM reform program, for which adequate resources are and will be allocated from the national budget,

      including for the capacity building and TA resources required to accelerate reform momentum. These resources

      are and will be complemented by TA from development partners. The continuation of the PforR instrument under

      GESDeK II will ensure the sustainability of development objectives related to PFM reforms.

  67. The Program expenditure framework, including under follow-on operation, GESDeK II, remains fiscally

      sustainable. The government’s medium-term fiscal trajectory is sound, and the PFM reform program is included

      in the medium-term expenditure framework. The PFM reform program has been in place since 2006 and is

      reliably funded. Participating MDAs are granted the authority to incur commitments and expenditures to enable

      them to achieve results under the PFMRS based on the work plans that they submit to the PFM Reform

      Secretariat at the beginning of the fiscal year. The PFM reform program has further flexibility regarding in-year

      budget reallocations to meet the advisory and capacity-building needs that may emerge during the fiscal year.

  68. Macroeconomic, political, and governance risks can impact the sustainability of the development outcomes

      achieved. The fiscal consolidation agenda may face challenges due to a demand for higher spending to lower the

      cost of living, potential introduction of subsidies, and revenues falling short of projections. A cycle of climate-

      induced natural disasters could pose fiscal pressures due to the urgent need to rebuild damaged infrastructure

      and provide social protection. Also, the substantive and politically sensitive nature of the reforms proposed by

      GoK regarding enhancing revenues and accountability in public spending bear political economy implications.

  69. The WB will mitigate macroeconomic and political risks through the interventions under GESDeK II. The reforms

      that GESDeK II will support are designed to increase revenue collection and improve efficiency in spending, which

      should mitigate fiscal pressures over the medium-term. The World Bank will liaise with the IMF and other

      development partners to monitor fiscal stability and recalibrate Program actions during implementation. GESDeK

      II makes an intentional effort to forge an alliance between the Executive and Parliamentary liaisons. It is expected

      that this effort will provide a channel of engagement that mitigates the extent to which political contestation may

      affect reform progress.



V. LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS



  70. In designing DLIs, it is important to avoid indicators that depend on the legislative approval process. In GESDeK,

      DLR 2.2a “Actual domestic (tax plus non-tax) revenue collections as a percentage of the annual budget” – was not

      achieved in three consecutive years due to significant differences between actual revenue collection and the

      budgeted revenue. The growing divergences between actual revenue collection and the budget were attributed

      to multiple factors, including institutional arrangements for budget formulation where the legislature could

      significantly alter the proposed revenue mobilization measures. However, reform momentum was not lost as

      reforms related revenue were incorporated in the IMF program and the Inclusive Growth and Fiscal Management

      DPF (P168204), further strengthening the link between the PforR instrument and other existing programs.

      Nonetheless, it is recommended that future program designs should carefully consider the potential impact of

      legislative processes on the achievement of DLIs. Specifically, DLIs should be formulated to minimize reliance on

      legislative approval, which can introduce unpredictability and delay. Therefore, to enhance the likelihood of

      achieving DLIs, it is advisable to select indicators that are within the direct control or influence of the implementing

      agencies.

    71. Opting for fewer focused DLIs has the potential to enable higher disbursements and greater achievement of

        strategic outcomes. The 27 DLIs under GESDeK, while considered necessary at the time of appraisal, ultimately

        proved cumbersome, making it difficult to track outcomes. Having fewer DLIs related to strategic outcomes, with

        the right level of ambition, yet realistic, makes the PforR instrument more effective. This streamlined approach

        allows for a more focused and strategic allocation of resources, ensuring that each DLI is closely aligned with the

        Program's core objectives. It simplifies the M&E process, making it easier for implementing agencies to track

        progress and measure outcomes effectively. Fewer DLIs reduce the administrative burden, allowing teams to

        concentrate on achieving results rather than managing a complex set of indicators. This focus on key outcomes

        can also enhance the clarity and transparency of the program, making it more straightforward for stakeholders to

        understand the program's goals and the criteria for success.

    72. Quality of technical design complemented by adequate financing and TA were critical success factors in the

        achievement of results. GESDeK was primarily centered on the policies and systems that were foundational for

        the achievement of the results desired under GoK’s PFMRS. The Program promoted engagement and

        interventions across a wide range of foundational matters regarding reform steps of the PFM cycle, including the

        macro-fiscal framework, budget credibility, financial reporting, and external oversight. This level of detailed

        engagement and comprehensive scope was necessary given the country’s PFM and public sector reform

        trajectory. The Program introduced and piloted several policies and systems that constitute the cornerstones of a

        well-functioning PFM system. Anchored on the results based PFMRS for 2018-2023, GESDeK incentivized GoK to

        accelerate the achievement of the results in a manner that was coordinated and sufficiently financed by the NT.

        The incentive structure was such that PFMR Secretariat had the flexibility to allocate and reallocate government

        resources based on demands from implementing MDAs who also responded to program-based budgets linked to

        performance management targets. The reform efforts were complemented by TA from various development

        partners including the World Bank who formed a PFM Sector Donors Working Group. Having a multi-donor trust

        fund, KADP, to provided TA and quality assurance was critical, particularly regarding ICT related reforms.

    73. Alignment of the PforR Program to reflect evolving government priorities was critical. External disruptions due

        to COVID-19, leading to increase in global prices, slowed down implementation and achievement of the targeted

        results under the Program. This was further exacerbated by change of government with realigned priorities.

        Following MTR, the operation was restructured to reflect the changing government priorities to mitigate COVID-

        19 interruptions, promote economic resilience and recovery, and rollout e-government services as well as SC

        reforms to bolster fiscal consolidation. Despite spending budget cuts across government, funding for the

        earmarked reforms under the PFMRS and the Program was viewed as a quasi-budget support to boost financing

        of government priorities amid fiscal and economic meltdown.
