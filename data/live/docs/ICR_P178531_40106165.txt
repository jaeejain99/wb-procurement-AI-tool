I.

PROGRAM CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES
A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL

Context
1. This Implementation Completion and Results (ICR) report assesses the achievements of the expected results of
the Nepal Finance for Growth (F4G) Development Policy Credit (DPC) programmatic series. The F4G series is a
three-operation programmatic engagement to support the Government of Nepal’s efforts to strengthen financial
sector stability, diversify financial solutions, and increase access to financial services. The first operation in the
series was approved in August 2020, followed by the second and third operations in February 2022 and April
2024, respectively.
2. Nepal’s economy faced severe pandemic-related disruptions, a fragile recovery, and renewed macroeconomic
pressures. The COVID-19 pandemic sharply disrupted the economy at the time of the first operation (F4G1), with
growth slowing from 7.0 percent in FY19 to 1.8 percent in FY20 as tourism collapsed, remittance inflows
weakened, supply chains disrupted, and domestic demand declined. Fiscal pressures increased as the deficit
widened to 7.3 percent of gross domestic product (GDP) in FY20 from 2.6 percent in FY19 and public debt rose
from 30.2 to 38 percent of GDP over the same period. By the second operation (F4G2), growth had begun to
recover modestly but macroeconomic conditions remained fragile, with a widening current account deficit, rising
inflation, and declining foreign exchange reserves reflecting strong import demand and global commodity price
pressures. By the time of the third operation (F4G3), growth had slowed again to about 1.9 percent amid
monetary tightening and import restrictions, while inflation rose to a seven-year high of 7.8 percent and fiscal
pressures increased due to declining revenues.
3. Nepal’s political environment remained highly volatile throughout the series. At the time of F4G1 appraisal,
Nepal was consolidating its federal governance structure following the 2015 constitutional transition, and by F4G2
appraisal, parliament had been dissolved and reinstated twice, disrupting the legislative calendar and changing

ministerial counterparts. By F4G3 appraisal, Nepal had experienced multiple governments transitions since the
start of the series, shifting reform priorities, reassigning staff in key agencies, and delaying the legislative reform
agenda.
4. Nepal’s financial system faced structural weaknesses that constrained its ability to support private sector–led
growth. The sector was heavily dominated by banks, which accounted for about 88 percent of total financial
sector assets in FY20, with the Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) serving as the sole prudential supervisor. Although the
sector had undergone consolidation—from 199 institutions in 2012 to 62 by 2021 —weaknesses persisted in
corporate governance, shareholder transparency, and related-party lending practices. While commercial banks
appeared well capitalized, with a capital adequacy ratio of 13.7 percent compared to the regulatory minimum of
11 percent, and reported non-performing loans remained low at 1.8 percent in mid-2020, these indicators were
subject to uncertainty due to COVID-19 regulatory forbearances and loan moratoria. Supervisory practices also
remained largely compliance-based rather than risk-based, and Nepal’s Anti-Money Laundering and Combating
the Financing of Terrorism (AML/CFT) framework lagged international standards.
5. Non-bank segments of Nepal’s financial system remained shallow and underdeveloped, limiting the availability
of diversified and long-term financing instruments. Capital markets were nascent with capitalization of 45
percent of GDP at the end FY19, and equity trading largely concentrated in financial sector stocks, which
accounted for more than 80 percent of listed companies, while fixed-income markets lacked depth. Markets for
specialized investment vehicles were largely absent. The insurance sector, comprising 40 companies with a
penetration ratio of 2.6 percent of GDP, was also underdeveloped and invested primarily in short-term bank
deposits rather than long-term assets aligned with its liabilities, limiting its contribution to capital formation. In
addition, disaster risk financing mechanisms were largely absent despite Nepal’s high exposure to climate-related
hazards.
6. Access to financial services remained uneven. At the time of appraisal of F4G1, only about 45 percent of the
adult population held a formal bank account, reflecting persistent financial inclusion gaps. Although digital
financial services were expanding rapidly, the use of digital payments remained limited, particularly among
women and rural populations. Gender disparities were significant, with only 42 percent of women holding a bank
account and just 13 percent making or receiving digital payments, according to the 2017 Global Findex. Access to
finance for micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs) was also constrained, with only about 16 percent of
MSMEs having access to bank credit and an estimated financing gap of US$3.6 billion by the time of F4G3. These
constraints reflected reliance on immovable collateral and weak credit infrastructure, including an outdated legal
framework for the Credit Information Bureau that limited data sharing and lenders’ ability to assess borrowers’
creditworthiness.
7. Nepal has been advancing the financial sector development agenda through national strategies focused on
financial stability, financial inclusion, digital payments, and disaster risk financing. Financial sector stability and
oversight reforms were initially guided by the Financial Sector Development Strategy (FSDS 2016–21), which
established the medium-term framework for strengthening regulation and supervision across banking, insurance,
and capital markets. Financial inclusion reforms were supported by the government’s broader financial sector
development agenda emphasizing expanded access to formal financial services for rural and underserved
populations. Reforms to improve digital payment efficiency were guided by Nepal Rastra Bank’s Retail Payments
Strategy. Financial resilience objectives were further advanced through the Disaster Risk Finance Strategy. Nepal’s
broader commitment to green and resilient development was reflected in the Kathmandu Declaration (2021) and
its Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement.

Rationale for Bank Support
8. The F4G DPC series supported Nepal’s COVID-19 recovery while addressing structural weaknesses in the
financial sector. The first operation was prepared as remittance inflows and tourism revenues collapsed, exposing
vulnerabilities in a bank-dominated financial system with shallow capital markets, weak credit infrastructure,
underdeveloped insurance, and large unbanked populations. Aligned with the World Bank Group (WBG) COVID19 Crisis Response framework, the series recognized that a well-functioning financial system was critical for
channeling recovery financing, remittances, digital payments, and credit to households and firms. Beyond the
immediate crisis response, the program aimed to strengthen financial sector stability through improved
supervision and AML/CFT compliance, diversify financial solutions through capital market and insurance reforms
and disaster risk finance, and expand access through digital payments, movable collateral reform, and financial
literacy.
9. The F4G series was closely aligned with the World Bank Group’s Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for Nepal
(FY19–23). The series supported two CPF pillars. Under Pillar 2 (Private Sector-Led Jobs and Growth), it advanced
Objective 2.3, which prioritizes financial sector stability and inclusion, as well as the development of insurance
and capital markets. Under Pillar 3 (Inclusion and Resilience), the series supported Objective 3.3, which focuses
on strengthening resilience to shocks and climate impacts, including exposure to natural disasters and external
demand shocks. The series also contributes to the CPF’s cross-cutting gender theme through initiatives on
financial literacy, digital payments, and the collection of sex-disaggregated financial sector data under Pillar 3.
10. The F4G series reform agenda was grounded in strong analytical underpinnings. The 2014 Financial Sector
Assessment Program (FSAP) served as the main diagnostic, highlighting compliance-based supervision, material
non-compliance with Basel Core Principles (BCPs), AML/CFT weaknesses, and gaps in credit infrastructure and
non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs). Subsequent analytics—including the Country Private Sector Diagnostic
(CPSD, 2018), Infrastructure Sector Assessment Program (InfraSAP, 2019), Post-Disaster Needs Assessment
(PDNA, 2015), Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR, 2022), Global Findex (2017, 2021), financial
inclusion report (IFC/UNCDF, 2023), and International Finance Corporation (IFC) studies on Transaction-Specific
Constraints for Foreign Debt Financing in Nepal (2019) and Nepal Bond Markets Assessment (IFC/SEBON, 2023)—
informed reforms on capital markets, disaster risk finance, climate-related finance, international borrowing,
movable collateral, Deposit and Credit Guarantee Fund (DCGF), and green bonds.
11. The F4G series was prepared and implemented in close coordination with other World Bank operations and
major development partners to ensure complementarity and avoid duplication. The series formed part of a
coordinated cluster of four programmatic DPCs in Nepal — alongside the Fiscal Policy for Growth, Recovery and
Resilience DPC, the Energy Sector DPC, and the Green, Resilient, and Inclusive Development (GRID) DPC — with
reform topics deliberately assigned to the most appropriate operation, allowing the F4G series to concentrate
exclusively on financial sector regulatory reforms. Coordination with the International Monetary Fund (IMF) was
formally managed, with the Extended Credit Facility (ECF) complimentary addressing loan classification,
provisioning and macro-financial stabilization. 1 Among other development partners, UK Foreign, Commonwealth
and Development Office (FCDO) and IMF provided technical assistance for Supervisory Information System (SIS)
operationalization; IFC provided advisory services on Environmental and Social Risk Management (ESRM), digital
finance, and movable collateral; United Nations Capital Development Fund (UNCDF) supported financial inclusion
program; and ADB supported disaster risk finance development and the Public Debt Management Office (PDMO)

13. The program was organized around three reform pillars:
Pillar 1: Strengthening Financial Sector Stability: This pillar focused on enhancing the resilience and integrity of the
financial system by moving towards risk-based supervision, improving the regulatory and legal framework for banks
and financial institutions, and AML/CFT compliance.
Pillar 2: Diversifying Financial Solutions: This pillar aimed to broaden the range of financial instruments and markets,
with a particular emphasis on disaster risk finance, capital markets and insurance sector development.
Pillar 3: Increasing Access to Financial Services: This pillar sought to enable international borrowing by banks,
establishing a digital payment interoperability ecosystem, enhancing financial literacy and reforming credit
infrastructure.

Other Changes
14. The country context evolved during implementation of the F4G series. The series was implemented amid the COVID19 shock, a fragile recovery, and subsequent macroeconomic adjustments, alongside institutional changes under
Nepal’s evolving political context. These developments required adjustments to triggers, timelines, and elements of
the results framework, while preserving the core objectives and reform pillars of the program.
15. The F4G series was initially designed as a two-operation programmatic series but was extended to three operations.
During preparation of F4G2, adjustments were made to the original F4G1 triggers to reflect evolving priorities and
allow additional time for complex legislative reforms by incorporating a third operation. A climate agenda was
introduced in F4G2 through new prior actions on environmental and social risk management for banks, climate risk
disclosure in capital markets, and climate-related financial risk disclosures for insurers. F4G3 further deepened the
climate reform track through prior actions on green bond regulation, property insurance risk-based pricing, and
climate-related commitments of the DCGF. At the same time, several actions were deferred to the third operation to
allow additional time for stakeholder consultations and legislative processes delayed by COVID-19.
16. A waiver of Bank Procedure (BP) 8.60 was granted to maintain the programmatic continuity of the series. BP 8.60
sets a 24-month limit between operations in a programmatic DPF series, after which the series is considered lapsed.
Due to legislative and institutional delays, the period between the approval of F4G2 and the preparation of F4G3

II.

ASSESSMENT OF KEY PROGRAM DESIGN AND OUTCOMES

17. The F4G DPC series underwent several adjustments to its indicative triggers and results framework during
implementation to reflect legislative delays, evolving policy priorities, and technical readiness. Some triggers initially
identified under F4G1 were deferred to later operations due to delays in legislative and regulatory processes, while
technical assistance continued to be provided across the series to maintain reform momentum. In the transition to
F4G3, eight trigger components from F4G2 were dropped in whole or in part (see Annex 6), reflecting coordination
with other reform initiatives and the recognition that certain reforms were not technically or legislatively feasible
within the series' timeline. At the same time, the program expanded its scope in F4G2 by integrating a climate reform
agenda across its pillars, with F4G3 further deepening this agenda. The results framework was adjusted accordingly,
two new indicators were introduced in F4G2 to capture the climate agenda (Result Indicator (RI)#3 on climate risk
reporting and RI#9 on green guarantees in the DCGF portfolio); two indicators linked to dropped prior actions were
removed in F4G3 (RI#6 on Private Equity / Venture Capital (PE/VC) financing and RI#9-old on Credit Information
Bureau (CIB) enrollment); RI#7 was subdivided into two sub-indicators to separately capture digital payment volume
and gender-disaggregated usage; and targets for RI#5, RI#8, and RI#9 were revised to reflect updated baselines and
market developments (see Annex 6).
18. The changes to the trigger and prior action framework had a mixed impact on the reform agenda and the likelihood
of PDO achievement. For financial sector stability, several changes strengthened the reform design, such as (i)
transferring the loan classification reform to the IMF ECF reflected appropriate division of labor; and (ii) introducing
ESRM (PA#2) embedded climate risk management in commercial bank lending. The objective of diversifying financial
solutions had mixed impact. Some changes strengthened reforms, including (i) upgrading insurance supervision
triggers and (ii) reformulating the Disaster Risk Finance (DRF) trigger into a binding Nepal Insurance Authority (NIA)
Property Insurance Directive that introduced risk-based pricing. However, other changes weakened the agenda: (i)
dropping the Securities Board of Nepal (SEBON) institutional strengthening trigger left institutional gaps unaddressed;
(ii) removal of the public building registry trigger weakened disaster risk financing agenda; and (iii) dropping the
Government Securities Dematerialization trigger removed an important capital market reform. Under the objective
of expanding access to financial services, the impact was mixed: (i) dropping the Credit Information Reporting Act
(CIRA) trigger left credit information gaps unresolved; while (ii) establishing a dedicated NRB financial literacy division
with a gender mandate strengthened inclusion monitoring; and (iii) inclusion of the DCGF trigger ensured further
development of the risk sharing mechanisms in support of the MSME access to finance.

Rating: Satisfactory
19. The relevance of prior actions for F4G series is rated Satisfactory. Almost all prior actions maintained a clear and
logical link to the achievement of the PDO objectives across the three pillars and were broadly aligned with Nepal’s

development priorities, framed at an appropriate level of ambition, and grounded in strong analytical diagnostics. The
reforms were technically sound and mostly appropriately sequenced, with coherent pathways from policy adoption
to expected outcomes. The programmatic three-operation structure was well-suited to the multi-agency, multi-year
nature of the reform agenda. However, sequencing was weaker in few instances, namely: (i) the Environmental, Social,
and Governance (ESG) disclosure requirement lacked prescribed format, content standards and tracking mechanisms

Pillar-1: Strengthening financial sector stability
20. The first pillar supported strengthening of financial stability through risk-based banking supervision, AML/CFT
compliance, and climate risk integration in financial sector oversight.
21. Prior Action (PA) #1 across the three operations aimed to strengthen Nepal’s banking supervision framework. The
2014 FSAP identified significant weaknesses in the NRB’s supervisory approach, which relied largely on compliancebased inspections and manual reporting processes. Under F4G1, the reform program supported amendments to the
NRB’s handbook for risk-based onsite supervision, strengthening methodologies for risk identification and supervisory
inspections. F4G2 supported the operationalization of a comprehensive risk-based supervisory infrastructure through
the adoption of a new Offsite Supervision Manual to guide continuous risk monitoring and the mandatory rollout of
the Supervisory Information System (SIS) to enable standardized electronic regulatory reporting by banks and financial
institutions. The indicative trigger on loan classification and provisioning standards was dropped from F4G3 as it was
addressed under the IMF’s ECF, without affecting achievement of RI #1 or the PDO. Under F4G3, reforms were further
strengthened through Council of Ministers’ (CM) approval for parliamentary submission of amendments to the Banks
and Financial Institutions Act (BAFIA) to enhance the NRB's legal authority to identify and address related-party
lending risks. These measures collectively expected to improve bank supervision and strengthen financial stability,
contributing to the achievement of RI #1, measured by compliance with the three-supervision related Basel Core
Principles.

B. ACHIEVEMENT OF OBJECTIVES (EFFICACY)
Rating: Moderately Satisfactory
35. The efficacy of the F4G DPF series is rated Moderately Satisfactory, reflecting the breadth and depth of reforms
delivered across the three operations. The program introduced laws, regulations, policies, and directives with systemwide impact across Nepal’s banking, capital market, and insurance sectors—many extending beyond what the nine
results indicators capture. All 27 prior actions were completed despite significant political volatility and the COVID-19
pandemic.
•

Under Pillar 1 (Strengthening Financial Sector Stability), the program strengthened Nepal's supervisory
architecture and addressed some of the systemic vulnerabilities identified in the 2014 FSAP, establishing the
institutional foundation of financial stability. RI#1 was partially achieved with BCP#10 improving to Largely
Compliant, though BCP#8 and #9 remain Materially Non-Compliant, reflecting that the transition from
regulatory architecture to effective supervisory practice requires sustained capacity building beyond the
series timeline. RI#2 was technically achieved but is qualified by Nepal's FATF grey-listing in February 2025.
RI#3 was partially achieved, with the shortfall reflecting the absence of prescribed disclosure format standards
and a market-capitalization-weighted tracking system. Overall, while individual indicators reflect an
incomplete transition, the institutional groundwork laid under this pillar supports an assessment that the
financial stability PDO was largely met, with full realization of outcomes expected over the medium term.

•

Under Pillar 2 (Diversifying Financial Solutions), the series delivered a comprehensive regulatory
transformation of Nepal’s capital market and insurance sectors, establishing the institutional infrastructure
for a more diversified, climate-aligned financial system. Of the two RIs, RI#4 was not achieved despite key
preparatory steps—including the Disaster Risk Finance Strategy and catastrophe risk modelling—as frequent
government changes weakened momentum and the required political decision on market placement was not
taken within the program period. Placement of the instrument will be further pursued under the Global Shield
initiative. RI#5 was achieved and exceeded, pointing out to substantial diversification of investment by
institutional investors, outside of the government securities. Early signs of insurance sector portfolio
diversification are also emerging, indicating that the regulatory incentive framework established under the
series is beginning to take effect.

•

Under Pillar 3 (Increasing Access to Financial Services), the program's impact was strongest. International
borrowing (RI#6) and digital payment transactions (RI#7A) significantly exceeded their targets. RI#7B and RI#8
were partially achieved; and RI#9 was not verifiable due to the Nepal Green Finance Taxonomy not being
operationalized by DCGF impacting ability to classify and report on green guarantees, though the binding
regulatory framework is in place and market uptake is expected to follow over time. Overall, the supported
reforms improved access to financial services, including for women-owned businesses, through enabling
regulations, enhanced risk-sharing mechanism, and improved credit infrastructure. Implementation of these
reforms will be further supported by SIF IPF.

Taken together, the program delivered substantial regulatory and institutional reforms across all three pillars. Weaker
outcomes were concentrated in nascent areas—green finance, climate risk disclosure, and disaster risk finance—where
institutional readiness and data systems were still developing. This pattern, together with a challenging implementation
context marked by political volatility and COVID-19, supports a Moderately Satisfactory rating.

updated on-site and off-site supervision manuals and the D-SIB framework—supervision in practice remains largely
compliance-oriented rather than genuinely risk-based. On-site inspections continue to rely heavily on standardized
checklists, supervisory intensity is not consistently differentiated based on bank risk profiles, and integration between
off-site analysis and on-site supervisory planning remains limited. The F4G series delivered the foundational
infrastructure for risk-based supervision — including the SIS, CAELS-based offsite surveillance, and ESRM frameworks
— but the transition from regulatory architecture to effective supervisory practice requires sustained capacity building
and institutional culture change that extends beyond the series timeline.
37. RI #2, tracking improvements in Nepal’s compliance with FATF standards on risk-based AML/CFT supervision, was
achieved, though qualified. The Asia/Pacific Group (APG) mutual evaluation report (September 2023) confirmed that
Nepal reached Partially Compliant status on FATF Recommendation 1 and achieved a Moderate Level of Effectiveness
on Immediate Outcome 1, meeting the targets established in the Results Framework. 8 The 2024 follow-up report (June
2024) upgraded nine FATF recommendations, with Nepal now rated Compliant or Largely Compliant on 28 of the 40
FATF recommendations. RI#2 deliberately focused on FATF Recommendation 1 (out of 40) that assesses the
foundational risk-based approach in AML/CFT since this was the main focus of the supported reform agenda. A key
milestone was the amendment to the ALPA, enacted by Parliament in March 2024, which introduced mechanisms for
identifying ultimate beneficial owners and addressed a major gap in the AML/CFT framework. However, Nepal's
placement on the FATF grey list at the February 2025 Paris Plenary 9—after RI#2 was verified through the APG's report
in June 2024—is a notable qualification. The grey-listing reflects persistent gaps across seven FATF action plan
commitments, the majority of which are beyond the scope of the series. 10 The World Bank is providing continuous
technical assistance and capacity building in preparation for the next National Risk Assessment and forthcoming APG
mutual evaluation to support Nepal's efforts to exit the grey list.

46. The overall outcome rating for F4G DPF series is Moderately Satisfactory. This rating reflects the relevance and

RISK TO SUSTAINABILITY OF DEVELOPMENT OUTCOMES

57. The risk to the sustainability of development achieved under the F4G series is assessed as Moderate driven by
Political and Institutional and technical risks.
58. Political and legislative risks. During F4G series, Nepal faced political turmoil, staff turnover, and shifting policy
priorities that could derail reform momentum. Parliamentary elections held on March 5, and the formation of a new
government may lead to changes in policy priorities. The BAFIA and CIRA remain before Parliament at series close and
are exposed to lapsing across a government transition, which would delay the banking governance and MSME credit
access reform agendas. The risk can be mitigated through continued technical engagement with authorities and
implementing agencies to sustain reform momentum and maintain progress on the reform agenda despite political
transitions.
59. Institutional and technical risks. First, the green finance outcomes are at significant risk due to incomplete
implementation architecture. While ideally a Nepal green taxonomy would have been established by the government
to guide regulators and market players, this was not realizable in then prevailing political and institutional context.
Nevertheless, given the priority accorded to improvements in climate risk management in Nepal – an incremental
approach was adopted in consultation with the principal financial sector regulators to initiate the reforms at their
levels with a longer term objective of consolidating into an overall green taxonomy. This entailed increased risks and
impacted the DCGF efforts to introduce a green guarantee mandate, and constrained the issuance of thematic bonds
and their scaling up within the timeframe of the F4G series. Second, Nepal remains on the FATF grey list at series close,
and formal grey list exit requires sustained operational effectiveness across the NRB, Financial Information Unit, and
Department of Money Laundering Investigation across successive governments. Finally, the supported reforms are
complex and require continued implementation support. The risk can be mitigated through continued technical
assistance and policy engagement, including leveraging the SIF IPF to further support the DCGF and the CIB, alongside
ongoing technical assistance on insurance sector reforms and DRF.
VI. LESSONS AND NEXT PHASE
A. LESSONS LEARNED
60. In small, capacity-constrained economies, reform agendas under multiple parallel DPC operations must be carefully
calibrated to institutional absorption capacity and supported by embedded technical assistance. Nepal’s experience
illustrates that simultaneous policy operations across several sectors can place significant demands on a limited pool
of senior officials and technical staff. During the F4G series, implementation occurred alongside several other DPC
programs, stretching the capacity of key agencies and contributing to delays of some reform actions. This experience
highlights the importance of aligning reform ambition with agency-level capacity, prioritizing reforms where
implementation readiness exists, and concentrating technical assistance where capacity constraints are binding. In
such contexts, longer-term embedded technical assistance within implementing agencies can be more effective than
periodic advisory support in sustaining reform quality and delivery. Particularly in very new reform areas, DPC design
needs to be especially realistic about sequencing, institutional readiness and data availability.
61. Early and systematic IFC engagement significantly strengthens DPC operations in financial sector development. The
F4G series demonstrated this through a comprehensive one WBG partnership, with essential contributions from the
IFC advisory work, active across all pillars of the F4G program including on the identification and the design of the PAs,
and the ongoing policy engagement with the regulators on PA implementation. For example, In Pillar 1, IFC provided
technical assistance to NRB for drafting the ESRM guidelines and reporting format. In Pillar 2, IFC's Nepal Bond Markets
Assessment (2023) informed the green and labeled bond framework and IFC's CPSD (2018) and InfraSAP provided the
analytical foundation for the shift toward risk-based capital and ORSA in the insurance sector. IFC advisory services

62. Results indicators should rely on publicly available, regularly collected, and easily accessible data, supported by
monitoring and evaluation systems. Several indicators in the F4G series faced measurement challenges that limited
the program’s ability to monitor progress effectively. The climate disclosure indicator could not be precisely verified
because the securities regulator lacked a system to track disclosure coverage by market capitalization, and the green
guarantee indicator was not operationally measurable due to the absence of a defined tracking framework. In
addition, market-based instruments that require a political decision for placement should not be set as results
indicators unless there is demonstrated government commitment beyond technical preparedness. In programmatic
DPCs, such issues should trigger mid-course adjustments rather than persist to closing. In F4G, these constraints were
evident during implementation but not addressed during the transition from F4G2 to F4G3. M&E was rated
Satisfactory in F4G2 despite these limitations, reflecting a focus on prior action completion rather than the
functionality of data systems. These limitations reduced the program’s ability to manage for results and weakened
accountability for some of its most ambitious targets. Future operations should ensure that results indicators are
linked to administrative data systems maintained by implementing agencies, and where such systems do not yet exist,
program design should include actions to establish the necessary monitoring infrastructure before the indicators are
introduced.
63. Multi-regulator and multi-agency coordination mechanisms must be explicitly embedded into DPC programs
design. The F4G series engaged a broad set of regulatory and implementing agencies simultaneously across multiple
reform tracks and three pillars. While each agency delivered its individual prior actions, the fragmentation of
regulatory authority across this broad institutional landscape slowed progress on reforms. For example, the climate
finance agenda illustrates this most clearly: the NRB's ESRM framework, SEBON's mandatory ESG disclosure
requirement, and the NIA's Climate-Related Risk Disclosure Directive operated as three parallel frameworks with no
common standard, no shared taxonomy, and no inter-agency mechanism to ensure that the disclosures produced
across the three sectors were comparable or mutually reinforcing. The green bond and green guarantee frameworks
introduced under SEBON and DCGF respectively required a shared definition of "green" that neither agency developed
independently. The lesson is that in multi-agency financial sector programs, inter-agency coordination mechanisms
should be explicitly embedded in program implementation arrangements.
64. Political transitions pose a significant risk to reform continuity in programmatic DPC operations and should be
explicitly addressed in program design. Nepal experienced several government transitions during the five-year
implementation period, which created disruptions through leadership changes, staff turnover, and shifting policy
priorities across counterpart agencies. These dynamics particularly affected legislative reform tracks, where several
key reforms depended on parliamentary approval and were vulnerable to political timing. Although political instability
was correctly identified as a substantial risk at design, the mitigation measures relied largely on continued dialogue
and coordination rather than structural safeguards within the program architecture. Future operations could further
strengthen resilience by embedding transition protocols — including systematic handover arrangements and
continuity commitments at the technical level — to preserve institutional knowledge and reform momentum across
government changes. Additionally, in politically volatile settings, reform design should rely more on careful
sequencing, choice of prior actions, and less dependence on legislative steps that are highly sensitive to political
timing.
65. DPC results frameworks need to account for time lags between legal reforms and observable outcomes. In the F4G
series, prior actions established the correct policy foundations and causal pathways, but achieving observable
behavioral and market outcomes requires time beyond the program period. This reflects a structural limitation of the
DPC instrument itself— results indicators are tied to a compressed program horizon, while high-impact policy reforms
