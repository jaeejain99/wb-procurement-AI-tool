I. PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES



 A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL



   Context

   1. In the decades that preceded effectiveness of the Ghana Productive Safety Net Project (GPSNP), Ghana

   experienced significant economic growth with reduced poverty rate while inequality at different levels

   persisted. The annual gross domestic product (GDP) growth rates recorded in Ghana for 2005 to 2017 ranged

   from 3.7 percent to 14 percent with the lowest growth rate recorded in 2016 and the highest in 2011. 1 The

   average annual growth rate recorded for the same period was 6.8 percent. The largest reduction in poverty –

   2 percent per year – was reached from 1991–1998. Subsequently, the rate of decline in poverty fell to 1.4

   percent in 1998–2005, 1.1 percent in 2005–2012, and dropped to 0.2 percent per year between 2012 and

   2016. 2 In aggregate, Ghana’s overall poverty rate – measured using the national poverty line – fell from 52.7

   percent in 1991 to 23.4 percent in 2016, leading to the attainment of the Millennium Development Goal 1

   target of halving poverty. Inequality (as measured by the Gini coefficient) rose from 0.42 to 0.43 between 2012

   and 2016. These trends in poverty and inequality indicate that the gains made in poverty in earlier periods

   were being diluted when GPSNP was approved in 2018.



   2. An analysis of profiles of poverty before GPSNP shows the spatial dimension of poverty as well as key

   drivers of transitions into and out of poverty in Ghana. 3 Poor households were less likely to transition out of

   poverty if they lived in areas with poor access to electricity, market, severe droughts, and high temperatures.

   Ecological conditions were also found to impact the likelihood of poor households moving out of poverty. The

   World Bank’s 2020 Ghana Poverty Assessment 4 suggests that where people live (regions) is more important

   than what they do (jobs) in determining their chance of breaking out of poverty. Indeed, in 2016/17, the

   poverty incidence of 2.5 percent in the Greater Accra Region was about 21 percentage points lower than the

   national average. 5 While half of the regions, namely Western, Volta, Northern, Upper East, and Upper West,

   experienced worsening poverty rates between 2012/13 and 2016/17, the other five regions the poverty

   incidence rates decreased. 6 Upper West region had the highest poverty rate among all the 10 regions in Ghana,

   with a rate of 70.9 percent. 7



   3. In response to the slowing economic growth, widening inequality and deepening poverty, the

   Government of Ghana (GoG) enhanced its social protection (SP) policy and institutional landscape to ensure

   support to the poor and vulnerable. In 2013, the GoG established the Ministry of Gender, Children and Social

   Protection (MoGCSP) with a mandate to coordinate SP in Ghana. Through the MoGCSP, the National Social

   Protection Policy (NSPP) was launched in 2016. Government further demonstrated its commitment to the SP

   sector through enhanced programs. The GoG established and progressively expanded its safety nets programs,

   invested in systems reforms to strengthen efficiency, transparency and objectivity of SP delivery and increased

   financing to the sector over time. The beneficiary reach of the Livelihood Empowerment Against Poverty (LEAP)

   program increased between 2008 and 2018 from 1,700 to 213,000 households with progressively increased

   financing from GHS2 million (US$ 1.9 million equivalent) to GHS168 million (US$38.6 million equivalent) within

   that period. Initiated in 2010, the Labor-Intensive Public Works (LIPW) program reached 167,243 cumulative

   beneficiaries by 2018 and created community assets that improved lives in communities. Government also

   initiated efforts to improve complementary programs with access to basic services, particularly health care for

   LEAP households. In 2015, the Government launched the Ghana National Household Registry (GNHR) to collect

   household data for SP targeting and rolled out electronic payment systems. A Productive Inclusion (PI) program

   to provide sustainable livelihoods (as an exit from receipt of cash transfers) was piloted between 2016 and

   2018, with support from the Japanese Social Development Fund (JSDF). 8



          Rationale for World Bank assistance



   4. The GPSNP sought to contribute to attaining the goals outlined in both Ghana and the World Bank’s

   sectoral policies and strategic documents. The project also provided the opportunity for the Bank to build on

   gains made in the predecessor project, the Ghana Social Opportunities Project (GSOP).9 Lessons drawn from

   the GSOP completion report show that GPSNP was preceded by- and needed to build on increasing political

   will. Specifically, GoG financing to LEAP was noted to have increased from GHS 2 million (US$1.9 million

   equivalent) at GSOP’s inception to GHS 168 million (US$38.6 million equivalent) in 2017. Government also

   demonstrated a similar commitment to the LIPW through the passing of the LIPW Policy in 2016. The rationale

   for continued social assistance under GPSNP was thus to build on this momentum to reduce poverty in a

   targeted manner, which as stated earlier, had deepened at the time of GPSNP’s approval.



Theory of Change (Results Chain)



     5.      The GPSNP had 3 safety net programs: PI, LIPW and the LEAP cash grant program. In recognition of

     the deep poverty experienced by the project’s target beneficiaries, the LEAP program sought to primarily

     provide regular cash benefits to smoothen the consumption of economically disadvantaged households and

     to promote human capital investments including accessing health care through membership in the National

     Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS). Economically disadvantaged households were provided with seasonal jobs

     under LIPW during the agricultural off-season to sustain their consumption. Theory suggests that for the poor

     to escape poverty, interventions that structurally improve their income-generating capacity are needed.

     Toward this end, PI aimed to address multiple constraints that the poor experience in enhancing their

     livelihoods. 10 The Complementary Livelihood and Asset Support Scheme (CLASS) sub-component of the PI

     program provided selected LEAP and LIPW beneficiaries with the opportunity to establish their own micro-

     enterprises or enhance existing ones through skills training, provision of a start-up grants, and mentoring.

   Project Development Objectives (PDOs)



    7.     The PDO as identified in the Project Appraisal Document (PAD) was to ‘support the Government to

    strengthen safety net systems that improve the productivity of the poor’. ‘Productivity’ in this PDO referred

    to improvements in the assets of the poor, which included both productive assets (such as livestock or tools,

    or a household enterprise) and human capital (such as improved nutrition and educational outcomes), which

    would enable beneficiary households to better meet their immediate and future basic needs.

   Components

Component 1: Productive Inclusion (Allocated US$10 million; Actual US$6 million)



      9.    The objective of this component was to support productive inclusion activities for extreme poor

      households in targeted communities. The component had two sub-components: Sub-component 1.1:

      CLASS; and sub-component 1.2: Linkages to Agricultural Support (LAS). CLASS sought to increase access to

      income-generating activities for extreme poor households, building on JSDF pilot project. LAS sought to

      improve agricultural productivity of extreme poor households by facilitating linkages to existing agricultural

      interventions.



      Component 2: Labor Intensive Public Works (LIPW) Program (Allocated US$28 million Actual US$21

      million)



      10. This component which provided temporary employment to beneficiaries sought to extend income-

      earning opportunities to poor households through payment to beneficiaries in exchange for their labor in

      rehabilitating or maintaining assets that improve community assets and promote climate change mitigation.

      Component subprojects include rehabilitation or maintenance of rural feeder and access roads; small earth

      dams and dugouts; and climate change mitigation interventions (afforestation).



      Component 3: Livelihood Empowerment Against Poverty (LEAP) Cash Transfers (Allocated US$23 million;

      Actual US$27.77 million)



      11. The component sought to smoothen consumption among extreme poor households through the

      provision of regular cash transfers to the households. The provision of LEAP grants also sought to enable

      beneficiary households to invest in their human capital and ultimately break the inter-generational poverty

      cycle.



      Component 4: Social Protection Systems Strengthening 12 (Allocated US$15 million; Actual US$11.54

      million)



      12. This component sought to strengthen social protection delivery systems to improve the efficiency,

      effectiveness, and transparency of safety net interventions through: (i) assisting GNHR in its collection,

      analysis and dissemination of quality household data to facilitate targeting for social programs; (ii) designing

 Revised Components

 20. The original five project components were maintained but revisions were made to component costs

 and scope through the July 2020 and April 2022 restructurings as follows:



  i. The July 2020 restructuring reduced funding to components 1 and 2 from US$10 million to US$6 million

     and from US$28 million to US$21 million, respectively, to increase funding allocation for components

     3 (from US$23 million to US$31 million), 4 (from US$15 million to US$16 million), and 5 (US$7 million

     to US$9 million). The increased funding allocation to component 3 was to facilitate a one-time cash

     transfer to newly targeted beneficiaries to alleviate COVID-19 associated economic hardship (as part

     of government’s COVID-19 emergency relief program). For component 4, it was to facilitate GNHR’s

     rapid data collection on targeted individuals for support, and to scale up SWCES to support the COVID-

     19 relief response through the validation of data collected by GNHR and receipt of information and

     complaints by alleged affected persons who reached out for support. Originally, the GNHR was

     designed to collect household-level data, but this restructuring enabled the registry to leverage the

     existing system to swiftly adapt its tools for collecting individual-level data which was needed because

     the crisis mostly affected individuals in the urban areas, who had different demographics than rural

     households. The registry was instrumental in providing targeted persons with one-time cash transfers.

     The SWCES, which was also originally designed to coordinate social protection information sharing and

     grievance redress, through this restructuring was expanded to support GNHR data validation.

     Increased funding for component 5 was also needed to finance increased operational costs in project

     implementation resulting from additional project activities due to the emergency response to COVID-

     19.



  ii. While project allocations were changed during the COVID-19 crisis to make additional funds available

      for cash transfers as discussed above, later reallocations through the April 2022 restructuring became

      necessary due to reduced project financing (mostly in cash transfers) resulting from FCDO’s Trust Fund

  A. KEY FACTORS DURING IMPLEMENTATION



 Factors subject to government’s control



 53. Over the lifetime of the project, MoGCSP underwent four administrative changes in its ministerial

 leadership, and several staffing changes. These changes, characterized by prolonged transition

 processes, delayed project implementation by 2 years, especially for component 4 of the project. Other

 project components implemented by the ministry equally experienced delays as a result of slow

 administrative approvals. In some cases, the change of Ministers created a shift in ministry priorities,

 which translated into the halting of activities. The World Bank project team worked closely with the

 ministry to mitigate impacts through advocacy and negotiations within the ministry and beyond,

 knowledge sharing particularly concerning evidence of potential project results should the challenges be

 rectified and increased technical support to mitigate further delays.



 54. Limited staffing delayed project implementation. While the PI and LIPW components were both

 implemented by the MLGDRD, the two components required separate staff particularly for MIS, given the

 increased scale, which was not adequately recognized at the inception of the project. The use of the same

 MIS staff for both components overburdened the MIS officers. This delayed delivery of MIS needs

 contributed towards the underachievement of PDOI-5 (Percent of LIPW payments and LEAP grants made

 on time to beneficiaries). In the case of MoGCSP, management administrative changes resulted in project

 technical staffing gaps which delayed project implementation.



 55. Overall prolonged administrative approvals generally delayed implementation activities. The

 administrative approval steps for both Implementing Agencies and key implementers at the local levels

 were generally prolonged. For example, financial requests requiring clearances between the MLGDRD and

 MoF required thirty-five (35) approval steps. Signing of work completion at the district levels and

 Factors outside of government control



 57. The deep macro-economic crisis in 2022 due to the polycrisis resulting from the economic

 aftershock of the COVID-19 pandemic and further shocks to the global supply chain of food and

 fuel (partly due to Russia’s invasion to Ukraine), (including nationally-induced fiscal stress), constrained

 the timely release of counterpart funding for the payment of LEAP beneficiaries. 48 LEAP cash transfers

 have been largely delayed leading to the underachievement of PDOI-5 (Percent of LIPW payments and

 LEAP grants made on time to beneficiaries) as discussed under the ‘efficacy section’.



 58. The pandemic also created other challenges, particularly loss of project funding and delayed

 procurement activities. The FCDO withdrew part of its funding support for the project due to the

 negative impact of the pandemic on the UK economy. Procurement activities were delayed because of

 disrupted supply chains due to border closures and lockdowns. The combined economic impact (high

 inflation and depreciation of the local currency) of the COVID-19 pandemic and Russia’s invasion of

 Ukraine resulted in price hikes and the need for price variation. These often resulted in the cancellation

 and re-initiation of procurement activities. Despite these challenges, implementation of most project

 activities during the pandemic generally did not experience delays as the Implementing Agencies

 continued implementation in innovative ways including adapting work schedules to accommodate shift

 periods and reducing work group size to limit beneficiary crowding at work sites under the LIPW. This

 contributed to the overall outcome rating of Satisfactory.



 59. Erosion of LEAP benefit and CLASS grant value due to high inflation in the last year (2022) of the

 project. Following the global economic crisis’ rippling effect on Ghana in 2022, which emanated from the

 COVID-19 pandemic and Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, the value of beneficiary benefits under the LEAP

 program (last revised in 2015) and the purchasing power of CLASS start-up cash grant were eroded.

 Although erosion of the benefit value over the years were significant, this was accelerated in recent years

    73. Overall, Bank performance is rated Satisfactory. The World Bank provided active support to the

    Government of Ghana during the preparation and implementation of the Project. The Bank team

    provided expertise and lessons learned from previous projects, including the importance of building

    systems that support social protection programs and the need for skilled technical support at the

    community level. The Bank ensured adequate controls of fiduciary, safeguards, and procurement

    compliance on the use of project funds, and project progress was well documented. An MTR was

    conducted to assess the project's achievements toward the PDO and the effectiveness of its

    implementation arrangements. The Bank team ensured improved communications and knowledge

    sharing of project outcomes and maintained a local presence in Ghana throughout the life of the project.

    The team’s rapid action during COVID-19, and effective guidance on the implementation of the

    digitization agenda are all commendable.



D. RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME



         Rating: Moderate



   74. The overall risk to the operation’s outcomes is deemed moderate. The factors that either contribute

   toward or hinder the sustainability of achieved outcomes can be summarized as follows:



  75. Limited government funds flow to social assistance programming. In the medium term, Ghana’s

  GDP growth is projected to slow, and inflation is expected to remain above the Bank of Ghana (BOG)’s

  target of 6-10 percent. 53 This poses a moderate risk to development outcomes including the sustainability

  of household enterprises established through the operation, purchasing power of the LIPW wage

  payments and the LEAP cash grant. In the past, slow growth, high inflation and limited fiscal space

  resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic, Russia’s invasion of Ukraine as well as existing national

  vulnerabilities, meant that funding projected in the national budget throughout the project’s duration for

  social protection was limited and most disbursements were delayed. The government has demonstrated

  evidence of its commitment to the social protection sector through past macroeconomic downturns. For

  example, the flagship cash grant program – LEAP, established in 2008 with just 1,000 households has

  grown to almost 350,000 households as documented in the ICR. Moreover, the LEAP program has existed

  and expanded through multiple political transitions. Further demonstrating political will, prior to the onset

  of the polycrisis 54, GoG’s contribution to the cash transfers program increased progressively to almost 70

  percent by 2019. The GoG has requested IDA-financing to augment gaps in its assistance starting with

       controls. Additionally, maintaining consistency of the core team throughout the duration of the

       project was key in ensuring the availability of institutional memory to guide implementation. It is

       recommended that follow-on projects consider the engagement of core staff that are locally present

       to support project implementation with minimal attrition rate.

        to efficiency gains, and providing a wholistic approach to tacking development issues, it is

        important to adequately align project design features. With reference to linkages to agricultural

        activities previously discussed, the inadequacy of this alignment resulted in the restructuring of the

        project to revise the intermediate indicator related to the activity to avoid missing the achievement

        of that indicator. Future projects to be designed should therefore endeavor to properly align design

        features, including implementation timelines, with ongoing projects.



   Lessons relevant to the client



   iv. Ensuring project implementation continuity in the event of administrative changes and associated

       transition periods. The GPSNP faced a high risk of not meeting its development target primarily due

       to delays in overall programing and halting of specific components due to administrative changes

       (particularly of high-level management and decision makers) and prolonged transition periods.

       Project implementation arrangements should identify strategies for ensuring implementation

       activities are not halted throughout the project implementation period.



   v. While the provision of counterpart funding is commendable, the absence of measures to ensure

       the funds are safeguarded and committed to the project can derail attainment of project

       outcomes. GPSNP included government counterpart financing demonstrating government’s high

       commitment to social protection (approximately 70 percent of the needed budget for cash

       transfers). However, challenges (including slow administrative releases in both sound and

       challenged macro-economic situations) experienced by the project with the release of funds for

       LEAP grant payments meant that a significant amount of projected financing did not actually reach

       the program. Future counterpart financing commitments should include a ring-fencing mechanism

       to ensure that the allocations are actually disbursed (on time) to the program.

      vi. Implementing Agencies should ensure continuous availability of needed staff with the right

          capacity throughout project duration Gaps in staffing at various times of the project’s

          implementation led to delays in implementation. As such, periodic needs assessments to confirm

          the capacity and availability of staff, particularly as the project expands, will be crucial to successful

          implementation.



      vii. Procurement contracts must include terms that allow for price variations and other modifications

           in the event of unforeseen circumstances to avoid contract cancellation and re-initiation leading

           to delayed procurement activities. The macro-economic situation resulting from the COVID-19

           aftershocks and high inflation and currency depreciation due to Russia’s invasion of Ukraine

           affected market predictability and pricing leading to contract non-performance. The introduction of

           the price adjustment clause for local currency contracts (national market approach) on a case-by-

           case basis is recommended to help curb inflation and local currency depreciation. Under the follow-

           on project, GPSNP 2, price adjustment clauses have been introduced in procurement contracts.



      viii. Also, given the low technical capacity at the decentralized level for procurements, it is critical to

            include required funding and develop an operational workplan that promotes continuous

            capacity building of the local staff. This will support decentralized procurement activities to

            eliminate or reduce the need for consistent hands-on support from the national office. Continuous

            capacity building will also aid in addressing the gaps in technical capacity and delayed

            implementation in the event of staff attrition (which is very common) at the local levels.



      ix. Digitized delivery systems promote efficient program delivery and timely shock response. The

          elaborate digitized systems developed under the project facilitated efficient delivery of project

          activities including beneficiary targeting, payments and real-time monitoring of cash transfers,

          household data collection for the social registry and electronic tracking of beneficiary attendance

          under the PI and LIPW components but for some challenges as discussed in the efficiency section

          above. These systems also created the platform for government’s shock response. Moving forward,

          government must continue to make available needed staff and tools to consolidate and complete

          systems’ integration for improved identification, tracking and reporting of the SP system.



      x. Need for expedited administrative approvals to prevent implementation delays (fiduciary). Under

         the LIPW component of the project, the MLGDRD and MoF successfully reduced payment approval

         steps from 35 days to 20 days without compromising on due diligence. While this is indicative of the

         possibility of a reduction of payment approval processes, a further reduction in administrative

         processes for all Implementing Agencies will be key to the overall timely project delivery.



      xi. There is a need for a robust cost of living adjustment framework to ensure benefit levels keep

          pace with inflation. This is especially the case for the LEAP program. It is estimated that the LEAP

          benefit level under GPSNP was only 5 percent of total consumption of LEAP households at project

          closing eroding from 40 percent at project inception 57. In line with the SP PER, the ICR recommends

          a regular and institutionalized process for the upward revision of the LEAP benefit level to at least

                                               ANNEX 4. EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS



1. The ICR assesses efficiency as follows: (i) a comparative analysis of operational costs of the PI-CLASS

grants relative to other countries; (ii) an economic analysis of the LIPW program; and (iii) a review of LEAP’s

effectiveness at reducing poverty severity.



         A comparative analysis of operational costs of the PI-CLASS grants relative to other countries.



2. As noted earlier, the allocation to the component responsible for this indicator was revised down

during the July 2020 restructuring from US$10 million to US$6 million. Despite this 40 percent drop in

funds, the Project reached more beneficiaries than expected, i.e., there was only a 20 percent drop from

the originally targeted individuals despite the 40 percent fall in available resources. The unit cost per

beneficiary fell from the originally implied US$320 at effectiveness to US$294.83 at closing. Despite these

changes, the CLASS grant ceiling was increased from US$200 to US$250 during the 2022 restructuring,

implying that the efficiency gains did not come at a cost to beneficiaries. 58 See Table A4.1 for an illustration.

According to the Implementation Unit, about 85 percent of the component 1 cost was directly expended

on beneficiaries (66.74 percent as start-up grants, 13.6 percent for training and 4.58 for mentoring).

Analysis shows that, at 60 percent, the CLASS cash grant accounts for a higher share of the program

compared to Mauritania (40 percent) and is closer to Burkina Faso (70 percent). 59 The cost of the grants

largely reflects the cost of living in different contexts. The training components, including life skills and

micro entrepreneurship trainings, were delivered for US$50–100 per beneficiary depending on the country

studied. By comparison, data from MLGDRD suggests that skills training under CLASS cost $38.16 per

beneficiary.
