       I.        STRATEGIC CONTEXT





    A. Country Context

1.       Viet Nam has achieved strong economic growth which has resulted in poverty reduction in the past few

decades. Since the late 1990s, economic reforms have transformed Viet Nam from one of the poorest countries in the

world to a lower-middle-income country with a nominal gross domestic product (GDP) per capita of US$4,164 in 2022.

Between 2012 and 2022, real GDP grew at an average annual rate of between 5.5 and 8.0 percent.1 Economic growth

coupled with the Government’s strong focus on inclusive social development has enabled Viet Nam to drastically reduce

the incidence of extreme poverty and promote prosperity. The poverty rates fell from 21 percent in 2010 to 5 percent in

2020 (lower-middle-income class poverty line of US$3.20 per day, 2011 Purchasing Power Parity).



2.       Much of Viet Nam’s growth has been driven by two main economic transitions: (a) the 1986 Doi Moi that

allowed some liberalization of the economy as well as private domestic and foreign investment; and (b) World Trade

Organization membership in 2007 and adherence to many bilateral and multilateral trade agreements, which sharply

reduced trade tariffs. These major policy shifts led to increased investment in public infrastructure including transport as

well as private investment in export-oriented manufacturing, engendering a successful export-oriented development

model, initially buoyed by low manufacturing costs. In 2021, Viet Nam’s trade as a percentage of GDP was 186 percent,

which made it the eighth highest in the world.2 Computers and electronics, agricultural products, and garments and

textiles are main export categories.



3.      However, as Viet Nam’s labor costs have increased, and with the emergence of other countries with low

manufacturing costs, it has become increasingly important for export cost competitiveness to be driven by other factors

such as efficient supply chains and lower logistics costs. The scope for continued tariff reductions is essentially exhausted,

so further reductions in trade costs need to be achieved by reducing costs tied to non-tariff measures and logistics. Viet

Nam’s logistics cost as a percentage of GDP is about 20 percent, which is high compared to countries such as the United

States of America, Australia, and Singapore, whose costs are below 10 percent of GDP.3 Addressing these issues and

sustaining cost competitiveness has become a primary objective of the Government of Viet Nam (GoV), as evidenced for

example by the increased focus on logistics by the Ministry of Transport (MOT).



4.       The southern part of Viet Nam (Mekong River Delta and Southeast Viet Nam, population 36 million4) includes

the main commercial capital Ho Chi Minh City (HCMC) and is a major engine of trade and growth for Viet Nam, which

calls for a focus on logistics improvements. Taken together with the Red River Delta in North Viet Nam, these regions

account for about 60 percent of the population. Of these two economic poles, the southern part of Viet Nam is larger in

terms of economic activity and accounts for over 70 percent of Viet Nam’s seaport container throughput. The Mekong

Delta is also the leading agricultural and aquaculture region of Viet Nam for both domestic consumption and exports.

Southern Viet Nam also hosts many key manufacturing centers.

    B. Sectoral and Institutional Context



The Inland Waterways Transport Sector



5.      Viet Nam’s domestic freight transportation market is heavily reliant on inland waterways transport (IWT) and

road transport. Based on ton figures, IWT accounts for 15 percent of the total freight transportation, while road transport

accounts for 80 percent.5 In terms of ton-km figures, IWT accounts for about 19 percent of the total, while road transport

and coastal shipping account for 25 percent and 54 percent, respectively.6 This highlights the importance of Viet Nam’s

reliance on inland waterways and coastal shipping for long-distance cargo transport. Viet Nam is privileged to have an

extensive natural waterways network that carries a much higher proportion of the national freight (19 percent in terms

of ton-km) compared to other leading IWT regions of China, the United States of America, and the European Union, which

carry about five to ten percent each. In fact, there is no country in the world except for the Netherlands that uses the

waterways more for freight transport than Viet Nam.7



6.       Investment in IWT infrastructure is strongly supported by international studies that show that IWT leads to

significantly lower logistics, environmental and accident costs compared to trucking. According to the GoV’s statistics

shown in Table 1, the average cost per ton-km of road transport can be up to 13 times more than that of IWT.8 Road

transport also contributes to about 80 percent of the transport sector’s greenhouse gases (GHG) emissions In Viet Nam.9

Therefore, shifting more cargo onto IWT and onto larger vessels is critical for decarbonizing Viet Nam’s transport sector,

especially as IWT consumes only 17 percent of road transport’s energy consumption (ton-km).10 Another important benefit

of IWT is that it is 50 times safer than road transport in terms of persons killed per ton-km.11

Nam’s national waterways network can handle vessels above 300 tons, which is an extremely low proportion compared

8.      In 2021, the GoV established the national IWT Master Plan, ‘Inland Waterways Infrastructure Planning for 2021–

2030, Vision to 2050’, which aims to grow the IWT sector as an important mode in the Viet Nam transport network,

with a focus on key corridors. Viet Nam has one of the most extensive natural waterways networks in the world, which

has the potential to connect directly to domestic coastal shipping between the southern and northern deltas of Viet Nam

and international maritime shipping. However, the IWT modal share has declined over the years due to limited

government funding and lack of infrastructure upgrades. The lack of infrastructure upgrades has limited vessel sizes and

resulted in long inefficient routes to circumvent low navigation standards, making IWT a less attractive mode of

transporting goods. The government hopes to reverse this declining trend and augment the current modal share by over

20 percent by 2030, through strategic investments in the key corridors, in collaboration with development partners such

as the World Bank. The master plan focuses on nine key IWT corridors nationwide, with a few key corridors located in

southern Viet Nam.



Challenges and Opportunities in Southern Viet Nam’s Waterways Logistics



9.       Southern Viet Nam is home to a dominant waterborne logistics network in the country, including the largest

port in the Mekong Delta at Can Tho, the largest port in Viet Nam at HCMC15 in terms of volumes, and the main deep-

sea port at Cai Mep Thi Vai (CMTV). Collectively, this waterways network is critical to serving Viet Nam’s trade. The

Mekong Delta is the main producer of rice which is the country’s largest agricultural export as well as a key producer of

seafood products, sugar and fertilizers.16 The southern Viet Nam’s light industry includes textile, leather and wood

processing industries. Significant construction materials such as cement, steel, sand, and stone are all produced in

southern Viet Nam. These products are mostly carried via the waterways, where the waterways transport modal share is

up to 70–75 percent compared to roads at 25–30 percent.17 Dong Nai is one of Viet Nam’s primary manufacturing centers,

whose products are also sent to the HCMC/CMTV ports for domestic and international trade via the waterways. According

to the 2021 national IWT masterplan, inland waterways freight tonnage in the southern region grew at a strong average

annual rate of nine percent.18



10.      Despite its economic significance, IWT infrastructure in southern Viet Nam faces several waterways logistics

challenges if it is to sustain its track record of promoting national growth and poverty reduction for the country going

forward. IWT infrastructure is both underdeveloped and underfinanced compared to global best practices, resulting in

several bottlenecks. A number of key rivers and canals have increasingly become constraints for larger vessels, due to

shallow and narrow waterways, sharp bends, and bridges with insufficient vertical and horizontal clearances. As a result,

the waterways in Viet Nam are of low navigation standards with an average vessel size of about 100 tons while

internationally, vessels can be a few thousand tons. Many vessels in the Mekong are old and frequently overloaded. The

ratio of the propulsion power over the cargo transported by these vessels is generally low. As a result, the push tugs for

barges struggle when required to execute maneuvers, particularly at portions of the canal with sharp bends, which also

increases the probability of accidents. These challenges have resulted from chronic underinvestment in capital

expenditures for IWT. As discussed above, the IWT sector receives only a small portion of the total transport state budget

annually due to limited fiscal space.

11.      The limited waterways navigability result in small vessel sizes and longer detours for larger vessels, which

significantly increase logistics costs and time, preventing a more sustainable multimodal logistics model. Due to the

limited navigation standards in many of Viet Nam’s waterways, vessel size is limited and larger vessels must make long

detours from points of production to consumption, increasing transport time and costs. A typical vessel size in Viet Nam

is 100-300 tons. Global best practices show that operating costs for IWT per ton-km reduce exponentially from 500-ton

vessels onwards due to economies of scale.19 In Viet Nam, the average cost per ton-km of road transport can be up to 13

times more than that of IWT. The inability to accommodate larger container vessels on waterways also prevents

containerization, which is a global trend of cargo equipment standardization that supports multimodal logistics.

Multimodal logistics is more sustainable due to the ability to complement container trucking with other modes such as

container IWT.



12.      There are capacity constraints for waterborne cargo to be transported from the hinterlands to the CMTV deep-

sea port, to be loaded directly on large vessels bound for international export destinations. This causes further logistics

cost increases and reduces export competitiveness. Currently, HCMC port handles the most maritime volumes. However,

the HCMC port is a congested city port, and its limited depth allows it to only handle vessels which are about 2,000 twenty-

foot equivalent unit (TEU) or 30,000 deadweight tons (DWT),20 which are bound for destinations in Asia only. Many of

these regional vessels will have to conduct an extra leg of shipment and stop over at overseas transshipment ports, to

transfer Vietnamese exports to large vessels bound for countries in Europe and the Americas, increasing logistics costs.

CMTV is the country’s main deep-sea port, with the ability to handle vessel sizes of around 18,000 TEU or 200,000 DWT.

If Vietnamese exports can be carried competitively via waterways to CMTV to be shipped to the Americas and Europe

directly, this would significantly reduce logistics costs. The elimination of the extra overseas transshipment leg is estimated

to save several hundreds of dollars per container to and from Viet Nam.21 This project intends to increase the capacity of

waterways from Can Tho and Dong Nai cities to CMTV, which is part of the GoV’s masterplan to better leverage the deep-

sea port at CMTV.



13.      The safety of IWT has improved over the years, but the trend of accidents fluctuates and continues to be a

concern. Overall, the number of IWT accidents and fatalities in Viet Nam has reduced since 2012, but this masks some

fluctuations. The total number of vessel accidents in the project area diminished from 28 in 2011 to 10 in 2017 but

increased again to 14 in 2018 before reducing to 10 in 2021. The accidents result from the increasingly high traffic on the

waterways, long distances, sharp bends and lack of navigation aids. Better IWT capacity, efficient routes and navigation

aids will reduce the risk of groundings and potential for collisions, casualty risks, and costs from such accidents.



14.     Better connections between Can Tho in the Mekong Delta and Dong Nai manufacturing center with the

HCMC/CMTV ports, via the east–west and north–south corridors, are considered one of the most strategic and effective

ways of improving connectivity in southern Viet Nam (see map in Annex 2). The east–west corridor is one of the most

direct ways for cargo from the Mekong’s largest port of Can Tho to reach HCMC, the largest port in Viet Nam in terms of

volumes. However, many sections are only of navigation Class III standards, have sharp bends and low bridge clearances,

and can take vessels up to 300 tons only.22 As a consequence, larger vessels instead have to follow the River Tien to River

Hau to Can Tho, an additional distance of over 92 km. The north–south logistics corridor is also crucial for the movement

of goods from Dong Nai to HCMC/CMTV. Dong Nai is one of Viet Nam’s main manufacturing centers and the source for

35.     Having sufficient disposal sites for dredged materials is a key factor for the successful completion of the

dredging contracts. Dredged materials in the Mekong Delta should be placed in open disposal sites for 12 –18 months

before being utilized for other purposes. It is important to find disposal sites in less densely populated areas. Potential

disposal sites should be thoroughly surveyed and identified by the detailed engineering design consultant.



36.     Maintaining constant monitoring of environmental and social (ES) impacts from the disposal sites is important.

As mentioned earlier, dredged materials in the Mekong Delta should be placed in open disposal sites for 12–18 months.

Therefore a ‘Dredged Materials Management Plan’ which will be part of the Contractor-Environmental and Social

Management Plan (C-ESMP) will have to be comprehensively prepared by the contractor as well as reviewed and approved

by the supervision consultant before any dredging activity starts. Monitoring the ES impacts from the disposal sites is a

part of this plan. Baseline parameters of the dredged materials will need to be collected after they are placed in the

disposal sites during and at the end of the exposure period to ensure the safety of the dredged materials before utilization

for other purposes. Safety measures (proper fencing) will have to be applied at the disposal sites to avoid potential

accidents.



37.     Timely and sufficient allocation of counterpart funds for resettlement compensation is another key factor for

completing civil works contracts as planned. Resettlement compensation for affected people/public utilities is required

for any infrastructure project in Viet Nam. Funding from government budget (counterpart funds) is allocated for this

compensation. In some projects, counterpart funds were not allocated in a timely and sufficient manner, which caused

delays to the contracts and thus project implementation progress. The MOT has committed that counterpart funds will

be incorporated in the Medium-Term Investment Plan (MTIP).



38.     Adequate time for procurement should be factored and procurement processes expedited where possible for

timely implementation. The procuring entities may tend to follow the domestic practices, which may require lengthy

internal procurement reviews and approval processes, causing delays in the project implementation. The MOT and the

Project Management Unit-Waterways (PMU-W) agreed to ensure the PMU-W will be adequately staffed with experienced

personnel, specifically with experience in World Bank’s procurement procedures.



39.      Effective collaboration between implementing agencies (such as PMU-W) and managing entities (like Viet Nam

Inland Waterways Administration and provincial authorities) is essential to prevent delays in project implementation.

The success and sustainability of project investments depend on the seamless coordination between these entities. The

assigning of clear roles and responsibilities by the implementing ministry is crucial for establishing strong collaboration,

along with results-based management, regular supervision, and guidance to ensure a smooth transition and operation of

the infrastructure.

48.     The scope of work of the project includes (a) widening and deepening the channels to meet higher navigation and

navigation channel for safety, (d) rebuilding the Cho Lach 2 Bridge for higher vertical and horizontal clearances, (e)

rebuilding roads to connect with the existing road system, (f) rebuilding drainage sewers, and (g) installing the navigation

aids system. The class of waterways is consistent with Circular No. 46/2016/TT-BGTVT dated December 29, 2016, on

‘Technical Classification of Inland Waterways’ and the approved Inland Waterways Infrastructure Planning for 2021–2030,

Vision to 2050. The specifications of the navigation channels and the structure of work items conform to the current

Vietnamese standards and guidelines.



Paris Alignment for Climate Mitigation and Resilience



49.      The proposed project activities are in the Joint Multilateral Development Banks’ (MDB) list for Universally Paris-

Aligned (UA) activities from a climate mitigation perspective. Project activities include IWT infrastructure upgrading with

some minimal replacement of local roads connecting and providing access to the waterways. Activities related to IWT

infrastructure upgrading will enhance the capacity, safety, and reliability of the targeted IWT corridors, and enable a

freight modal shift from trucking to IWT for cargo transported between Can Tho and HCMC (via the East-West Corridor),

as well as Dong Nai to HCMC to CMTV (via the North-South Corridor). It will reduce road traffic from currently overloaded

National Highways such as National Road Number 1 and National Highway 51 and result in significant emission reductions.

In addition to the modal shift impact, the proposed East-West IWT corridor will reduce the length of the IWT routes by 92

km for the Can Tho-HCMC route compared with the existing IWT route and therefore GHG emissions. The proposed road-

related investment is for reconstruction of 6 km of existing low-capacity rural roads near the waterways, which are

expected to be affected by civil works for waterways widening. These road-related works are for rural roads without

capacity expansion for general traffic. Neither the waterways nor the road-related constructions will lead to deforestation

or loss of carbon sinks.



50.     The transition risks and carbon lock-in risks for the proposed IWT corridors are considered low. Although small

amounts of coal and petroleum products are expected to be carried by waterways transport using the project corridors

as part of their overall service, as with all transport modes globally, their share over the overall freight demand on the

project corridors are immaterial. Sugar, cashew, wood, fertilizer, sand and stones together contribute to nearly 85 percent

of total demand. Coal and petroleum products accounted for only three percent of freight demand on targeted IWT

corridors in 2019. By 2040, their shares are expected to increase to about nine percent in total, with Viet Nam’s

51.      The inherent physical climate risks exposed to the project infrastructure is high. For the project area, the primary

physical climate risks exposed to the IWT infrastructure include increased water level variations and flooding. Sea level

rise is expected to increase the risk of tidal inundation and enlarge the corrosive zone inland. High water flow velocity

from flooding leads to higher risks of riverbank erosion, thereby undermining structural stability.



52.       Specific engineering designs are incorporated in the project. The residual physical climate risks to the project

outcome are low and thereby the proposed project activities are also aligned with the climate change adaptation

objectives of the Paris Agreement. The project infrastructure design is informed by a detailed climate and disaster risk

screening. Specific adaptation measures are incorporated in project engineering design in order to mitigate the identified

climate risks. This includes that (a) waterways canals will be widened and deepened to adapt to high tidal inundation risks.

The designed water level for all project corridors takes into account of water level variation under seasonal and tidal

changes to ensure undisrupted navigation; (b) the crest elevation of all waterways embankment and bridges are increased

by 30 cm based on climate projection through 2050 under Representative Concentration Pathway (RCP) 8.5 (high-emission

scenario) to mitigate flooding risks; (c) about 80 percent of the waterways embankment will use a climate resilient

technology that combines reno mattress and concrete blocks to enhance soil stability and embankment structure in order

to prevent riverbank erosion. Therefore, this project includes three climate related indicators which are (i) Length of

waterways improved to desired navigation standards with climate resilient features in the East-West Corridor, (ii) Length

of waterways improved to desired navigation standards with climate resilient features in the North-South Corridor and

(iii) Length of embankments constructed with climate friendly/resilient features in East-West Corridor.



53.     The project is expected to generate significant climate change co-benefits both for climate change mitigation

and climate change adaptation. The entire project finances infrastructure for IWT as a low-carbon alternative mode of

transport and enables a freight modal shift from currently congested road corridors. All the infrastructure components

are designed to adapt to higher risks of tidal inundation, flooding, and embankment erosion due to sea level rise and

climate change as described above.

 D. Environmental and Social



67.      Potential ES risks and impacts. The key features of the project include (a) upgrading of two high-traffic and

congested East–West and North–South Corridors to accommodate larger vessels and more logistics flows and (b)

installation of navigational aids for safety. The interventions will reduce logistics costs, transit time, GHG emissions, and

vessel accidents. As a result, the project will result in positive ES impacts by generating high climate change mitigation co-

benefits (as IWT is a low-carbon transport mode compared to road and railways) and promoting socioeconomic

development and access to service delivery and safety in the transport sector.



68.      The environmental risk of the project is classified as Substantial. The main environmental, health, and safety risks

and adverse impacts likely to occur are associated with the size of the project investment and the nature of its

interventions, including (a) dredging activities in five canals and rivers, (b) embankment protection of three canals and

rivers, (c) reconstruction of a bridge, (d) bend corrections at four canals and rivers, (e) reconstruction of roads, and (f)

upgrading of drainage sewers. If the design and implementation of these interventions are not properly carried out, the

project could generate (a) safety risks due to the removal of unexploded ordnances (UXOs); (b) water pollution and

turbidity due to dredging activities; (c) impacts on aquatic life, riverine ecosystem services, and water supply along the

canals and rivers; (d) water and land pollution due to the disposal of dredged and excavated materials; and (e)

occupational and community health and safety risks. In terms of the sensitivity of the receiving environment, the proposed

interventions would not be implemented within critical natural habitats, cultural heritage sites, and/or densely populated

areas. The most sensitive environmental receptor in the neighboring area is the Can Gio protection forest. However, the

project activities will not be implemented within the Can Gio protection forest, and land acquisition of the Can Gio forest

is not required. Furthermore, several river bend cuttings have been avoided, and bend corrections will be conducted

instead for safety, minimizing the dredging significantly and avoiding isolated populations. These environmental risks and

adverse impacts are expected to be medium to substantial in magnitude, site-specific, predictable, and/or reversible and

can be readily and reliably managed through the environmental management hierarchy and mitigation measures.



69.      The project is expected to use a substantial amount of resources and materials for construction of the river and

canal embankment, bridge, roads, and irrigation and drainage works for agriculture. The ESIA has identified and assessed

in detail the risks and impacts related to the release of pollutants, waste generation, management of dredged/excavation

materials and hazardous waste, impacts on adjacent communities, and resource use efficiency. Mitigation measures have

also been proposed and included in the Environmental and Social Management Plan (ESMP).



70.     The ESIA confirmed that the dredging/excavation would not have significant impacts on the environment because:

resources, not waste, and can be used for ground levelling and gardening. A dredged material management plan will be

prepared to manage the safe handling, storage and disposal of the dredged/excavated materials. Risks and impacts due

88.     Procurement risk is rated Substantial. The major procurement risks include (i) the PMU-W staff may lack sufficient

knowledge to apply the latest World Bank Procurement Regulations updated in 2023; (ii) non-compliance with World Bank

procedures (including governance and corruption issues) may occur during project implementation; and (iii) poor

performance of contractors/consultants would also contribute to delayed contract execution. To mitigate the major risks,

the World Bank agreed with the PMU-W on a detailed action plan, including (i) PMU-W to be staffed with personnel

experienced with previous Bank-funded projects, who will be further trained; (ii) relevant staff will receive training on the

World Bank’s Procurement Regulations, including mitigation of fraud and corruption risks; and (iii) the PMU-W has

developed a PPSD and proposed procurement arrangements to align the procurement activities with the PDO, maximize

value for money, identify appropriate procurement methods in the PP, and monitor the contract management plan.

Further details are in Annex 1.



89.      Social risk is rated Substantial. The potential adverse social risks and impacts and their mitigation measures are

discussed in Section IV of the PAD and relevant ES instruments. Key potential social risks and impacts of the project mainly

refer to the implementation of the LAR activities, and related social-related aspects. An ESIA/ESMP has been prepared to

address risks linked to social, land, livelihood, SEA/SH, and labor-related issues. The RPF relates to land acquisition,

resettlement, and livelihood restoration. The EMDF has been prepared to consult and enhance project benefits among

the project-affected ethnic minority groups. The SEP addresses public consultations and grievance management at the

project level. The LMP addresses the labor-related project risks and impacts.





90.     Environmental risk is rated Substantial. The potential adverse environmental risks and impacts and their

mitigation measures are discussed in Section IV of the PAD and relevant ES instruments. The main potential adverse

environmental risks and impacts include (i) safety risks due to UXO clearance; (ii) water pollution and turbidity; (iii) impacts

on aquatic life, riverine ecosystem services, and water supply along the canals and rivers; (iv) water and land pollution due

to the disposal of dredged and excavated materials; (v) dust, noise, and air pollution; and (vi) community and worker

health and safety from the vehicles and equipment that transport dredged materials and road construction spoils from

the project sites. These potential adverse impacts have been assessed as site-specific, predictable and/or reversible, and

can be readily and reliably managed. An ESMP, as part of the ESIA, was prepared that details the extensive ES risk

mitigation measures, including site-specific mitigation measures to address site-specific impacts during construction and

operation. An ESCP was also prepared that specified all the material measures and actions required for the project to meet

the ESSs over specific timeframes.

                               ANNEX 1: Implementation Arrangements and Support Plan



1.      The MOT will have the overall responsibility of overseeing the implementation of the project, reporting to the

GoV, coordinating the activities of central and provincial government agencies, and fulfilling the World Bank requirements.

As the owner of the project, the MOT will approve the overall feasibility/construction investment report and the overall

PP and provide policy/strategic guidance and instructions to the PMU-W. The responsibility for approving bidding

documents and evaluating contractor selection results lies with the PMU-W authorized by the MOT in accordance with

Decision No. 729/QD-BGTVT dated June 15, 2023 regulating decentralization in the management of construction

investment projects financed by public investment capital managed by the MOT.



2.      The PMU-W is responsible for the project implementation. It is the MOT’s authorized executing agency of the

project and will have the overall responsibility for preparing survey methodologies and plans, detailed engineering

designs, and cost estimates. The PMU-W will be in charge of the procurement of goods, works, and services related to all

project components and supervision including contracting and payments. The PMU-W will be responsible for coordination,

quality assurance, training, M&E, and day-to-day supervision of project activities. Many staff in the PMU-W are familiar

with the World Bank requirements as they have been implementing three inland waterways projects funded since 1997

up to now.



3.     VIWA is responsible for the operation and maintenance of infrastructure along national rivers. This includes the

maintenance of aids to navigation as well as waterways maintenance, including for the project waterways after the project

completion.



4.       The PPCs and DPCs, in accordance with Decree 47/2014/ND-CP, will be responsible for LAR of the project. The

project province will assign the respective project districts to implement LAR in its administrative jurisdictions whereas

the PMU-W will manage the overall environment and social safeguard risks and coordinate with the project provinces and

districts to implement LAR. The MOT will provide counterpart funding for LAR.

                                          ANNEX 2: Detailed Project Description





1.      This project includes three components: (a) upgrading of sections of the East–West Waterway Logistics Corridor,

(b) upgrading of sections of the North–South Waterway Logistics Corridor, and (c) project implementation support.



2.       The East–West Corridor has a total length of about 197 km, connecting the Can Tho port with the HCMC port via

the Hau River–Tra On River–Mang Thit River and canal–Co Chien River–Cho Lach canal–Tien River–Ky Hon canal– Cho Gao

canal - La canal–Vam Co River–Nuoc Man canal–Can Giuoc River–Soai Rap River. The existing East–West waterway corridor

has the navigation standards of Class III. Currently, a typical vessel of only about 100–300 tons travels on this corridor due

to limited navigability. In many sections of the waterways, navigation aids are not available and some sections of the

corridors (such as Mang Thit and La) have small turning radiuses that put vessel operations at risk. The Cho Lach 2 bridge

has insufficient vertical and horizontal clearances, which poses limitations to vessel sizes.



3.       The North–South Corridor has a total length of about 82 km, connecting the Dong Nai port with the HCMC port

and the CMTV port via the Dong Nai River–Nha Be River–Long Tau River–Dong Tranh River–Tac Cua River–Go Gia River–

Thi Vai River. The North–South Corridor faces similar challenges as those of the East–West Corridor. Currently, a vessel of

about 3,000 tons can travel on this corridor but is unable to navigate full time and must wait for high tides.



4.       In the FS, a multi-criteria analysis method was conducted including the following criteria: (a) transport cost benefit

per US dollar of investment and annual maintenance costs; (b) increase of safety of navigation, defined by navigation time

(lower navigation time results in fewer accidents); (c) ES&R impacts including scale, severity, duration, and significance of

impacts on the socio-economic, physical and biological environments; and (d) total investment for the required upgrading

interventions. A total of 11 corridor alternatives were analyzed and indexed based on their relative transport benefit-to-

cost ratio, transportation time, ES&R impacts, and investment required. Weights were also assigned to the four criteria.

Considering that the benefit-to-cost ratio, safety, and investment criteria are closely related, those criteria were assigned

a combined weight of 50 percent and the remaining 50 percent was determined by the ES&R criteria. Overall, the East–

West and North–South Corridors selected provided the best results based on the multi-criteria analysis.



Component 1: Selected sections of the East–West Waterway Logistics Corridor

and upgrading infrastructure bottleneck sections of the corridor to meet Viet Nam Class II Inland Waterways Standards,

high tides (see Figure A2.1). In particular, this component will support the (a) widening and deepening of the waterways

Thit, Cho Lach, and La canals; (c) reconstruction of the Cho Lach 2 bridge for higher and wider vessel clearances; (d) bend

corrections at Mang Thit, Cho Lach, and La; (e) the reconstruction of roads to connect with the existing roads at Mang

Thit and Cho Lach; (f) reconstruction of drainage sewers; and (g) installation of navigation aids at Tra On, Mang Thit, Cho

Lach, Nuoc Man and Can Giuoc.

                                                ANNEX 3: Economic Analysis





A.      Introduction



1.       This annex presents the economic analysis of the project, covering the upgrading of waterways sections of the

East–West and North–South Corridors over a period of 30 years. It first briefly summarizes the scope of the project, then

the applied methodology of the economic analysis, and finally it estimates the economic rate of return, NPV, and the

sensitivity analysis for the project.



B.      Background



2.       The southern part of Viet Nam has a diverse and plentiful system of inland waterways. However, due to limited

investment and upgrading, waterway corridors are not properly utilized to their potential. The constrained waterways

limit the size of vessels, resulting in low consolidation of cargo. Small vessels and long inefficient routes increase costs,

accidents, and GHG emissions. Road trucking emits up to six times the emissions of IWT. Hence, to properly utilize the

potential of sustainable IWT and boost Viet Nam’s growth which is significantly driven by the southern part of Viet Nam,

the country needs to pursue investments in waterways. The project will improve the navigation standards and capacity of

the East–West waterway transport corridor linking the port of Can Tho (the largest in the Mekong Delta) with HCMC (the

largest port in Viet Nam). This corridor is connected onward toward the CMTV port (main deep-sea port of Viet Nam). The

project will also upgrade the North–South corridor linking the key manufacturing centers of Dong Nai/Binh Duong to

HCMC and the CMTV deep-sea port. The project connects the four largest ports in the southern part of Viet Nam.



C.      Project Scope



3.      Below is the summary of the proposed infrastructure to be undertaken by the project. Annex 2 provides the

detailed scope of the project.



4.       Component 1: Selected sections of the East–West Waterway Logistics Corridor. This component will finance the

upgrading of sections of a key east–west inland waterway corridor, connecting the Can Tho Port and the HCMC port, from

navigation Class III to Class II, allowing for larger vessels and a shorter route. In particular, this component will support the

(a) widening and deepening of selected waterways; (b) construction of embankments; (c) reconstruction of the Cho Lach

2 bridge for higher and wider vessel clearances; (d) bend corrections; (e) reconstruction of roads to connect with the

existing roads; (f) reconstruction of drainage sewers; and (g) the installation of navigation aids.



5.      Component 2: Selected sections of the North–South Waterway Logistics Corridor. This component will finance

the improvement of waterways sections of a key north–south corridor, connecting the Dong Nai port–the HCMC port–the

CMTV deep-sea port, allowing larger vessels. The component will support (a) the widening and deepening of waterways

at the Tac Cua River; (b) bend correction works at the Tac Cua River and (c) navigation aids at the Tac Cua and Dong Tranh

Rivers.



D.      Methodology of the Economic Analysis



6.      A Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA) was carried out in accordance with the World Bank Guidelines of Economic Analysis

of Investment Operations. The CBA used inputs from a VISUM transport model configured for the SWCLD. The VISUM







                                                                                                                           Page 48

         The World Bank

         Southern Waterway Corridors and Logistics Development Project (P169954)





transport model is a modelling software package utilized by global transport professionals for transport planning and

investment projects.



Details of Methodology



7.      First, freight transportation volumes in the southern part of Viet Nam were determined and forecasted using the

GoV statistics, estimates of production, consumption, export and import of commodities, logistics surveys, regional

transport statistics, traffic counts, population growth, and GDP forecasts. In 2021, Viet Nam’s population was

approximately 6.3 percent higher than the population in 2015. Between 2012 and 2022, real GDP grew at an average

annual rate of between 5.5 and 8.0 percent.51 Due to the COVID-19 pandemic situation, Viet Nam’s GDP grew by only 2.9

percent in 2020, and 2.6 percent for 2021.52 The country’s GDP growth is forecast at 5.8 percent in 2023, and 6.5 percent

a year from 2024-2025.53 Building materials, steel, sand and stone as well as agricultural and seafood products are the

leading commodity groups being transported by inland waterways. Following the country’s economic growth, these

commodity groups will grow strongly to support the high demand of infrastructure, consumption, and exports.



8.      These parameters served as inputs in the VISUM transport model. In this model, the road and inland waterways

network of the southern part of Viet Nam was constructed in line with the GoV’s transport masterplans and information

about commodity production and attraction per region, the origins and destinations per commodity, and the modal split

were incorporated. The transport model assigned transport flows per transport mode (in tons) to transport flows on the

network. The results of this assignment were ton-km and vehicle-km and vessel-km (on various vessel types) for the whole

network in the southern part of Viet Nam. The transport model calculated the effects of the upgrading of the project

corridors, with and without project. This provided inputs for the CBA. The difference of ton-km and vessel-km between

the ‘without project’ and ‘with project’ scenarios, determined the economic benefits of the project.



E.       CBA Inputs and Results



Costs



9.       The total investment costs to upgrade the East–West Corridor and North–South Corridor are estimated at US$158

million54, including counterpart funds.



10.    The maintenance costs are estimated as a percentage of the investment costs and are based on historical

maintenance costs of similar IWT upgrading projects in Viet Nam.



Economic Benefits



11.      The project will generate direct and indirect benefits to river transport users because of the improved corridors.

The project will reduce the length by 92 km on the East–West Corridor and upgrade the North–South Corridor to allow

larger vessels (200 TEU container vessels and 5,000 tons bulk vessels) to travel on the project corridors. As a result, the

project will have the following quantifiable economic benefits: (a) reduction in VOCs, (b) reduction of vessel accidents,

and (c) reduction in GHG emissions.

12.     Transport cost benefits. These occur due to the reduced travel distance of 92 km and consequently lower

pollutant are calculated by multiplying the reduction of pollutants (in kg or tons) by the shadow price.55 The total CO2

emission reduction is estimated at 3.4 million tons (over a period of 30 years). Discounted benefits total US$101 million.

                                   ANNEX 4: Paris-Alignment Assessment and Climate Co-Benefits



Alignment with Viet Nam’s Low-Carbon and Climate Resilient Transition



1.      Inland waterways transport (IWT) is a low-carbon transport mode compared to road and trucking. It consumes

only 17 percent of energy consumption per ton-km freight transported compared with trucking on road56, which is the

dominant alternative mode in Viet Nam. IWT in general produces only one-sixth of the carbon dioxide emissions of road-

based trucking. In Viet Nam, road transport contributes to about 80 percent of the transport sector’s GHG emissions.

Further mainstreaming IWT in Viet Nam’s transport mix is vital for decarbonizing Viet Nam’s transport sector.



2.      Under Viet Nam’s NDC 2022 and the Decision 876/QD-TTg, developing IWT is highlighted as a key strategy to

achieve the emission reduction targets. The NDC 2022 emphasizes the ‘conversion of modes of transportation of

passengers and goods’ from high-emissive modes (road transportation) to low-emissive modes (railways and IWT). The

Decision 876/QD-TTg further sets priorities to upgrade key IWT transport routes nationwide, investing in roads and bridges

connecting to IWT networks, upgrading key domestic ports and navigational channels, and promoting energy efficiency

improvements of vessels, ships, and ports.



3.       In parallel to reducing GHG emissions from the transportation sector by promoting the IWT sector, Viet Nam also

sets priority in enhancing the climate resilience of IWT infrastructure and services. Under NDC 2022, IWT is highlighted as

highly vulnerable to climate change impact, as the Red River Delta and Mekong River Delta regions where most of IWT

network is located, are highly exposed to flooding and structural corrosion and damage of IWT infrastructure from sea

level rise and saline intrusion.



4.       In the World Bank’s CCDR for Viet Nam published in 2022, one of the top recommendations to decarbonize Viet

Nam’s transportation sector is scaling up investment and supporting policies to facilitate modal shift from road transport

of freight to waterborne transport systems including IWT. The CCDR specifically recommends Viet Nam to promote

container traffic on barges using IWT along the Can Tho-HCMC route, which account for five percent of total intercity

freight flows in Viet Nam.



5.       The proposed operation to invest in Viet Nam’s southern waterway logistic corridors is aligned with Viet Nam’s

national strategy for low-carbon and climate resilient transition, as described in NDC 2022 and the Decision 876/QD-TTg.

It focuses on improving the East-West IWT corridor linking Can Tho-HCMC (a priority route highlighted both in the GoV’s

Master Plan and the World Bank CCDR), to improve freight cargo volumes using waterways in the surrounding hinterlands

instead of the currently overloaded and congested National Road Number 1. It also promotes modal shift from trucking

along National Highway 51 to the North-South IWT corridor, to Viet Nam’s main maritime ports. It is estimated that the

proposed operation will lead to a total GHG emission reduction by 3.457 million tCO2e over the economic lifetime of 30

years compared to the baseline emissions without the project. The IWT infrastructure to be upgraded under the project

will be designed specifically to adapt to identified climate risks.

Paris Alignment - Climate Change Mitigation



7.      The proposed project activities include IWT infrastructure upgrading, with some minimal replacement of roads

connecting to the waterways. All activities are in the Joint MDB list for Universally Aligned (UA) Activities from a climate

mitigation perspective:



    a) Activities related to waterways infrastructure upgrading will enhance the capacity, safety, and reliability of the

       targeted IWT corridors, and thereby enable a freight model shift for cargo transported between Can Tho and

       HCMC, as well as Dong Nai to HCMC to CMTV from currently overloaded National Highways such as National Road

       Number 1 and National Highway 51 to IWT routes (Figure A4.1). In addition to the modal shift impact, the

       proposed East-West IWT corridor will reduce the length of the IWT routes by 92 km for the Can Tho-HCMC route

       compared with the existing IWT route and therefore GHG emissions. In order to minimize the material risk of

       contributing to deforestation and loss of carbon sinks, the IWT corridor is specifically designed to avoid affecting

       forested land.



    b) The proposed road-related investment is for reconstruction of 6 km of existing low-capacity access rural roads

       near the waterways, which are expected to be affected by civil works for waterways widening. It is estimated to

       account for less than two percent of project financing. Considering that these road-related works are for rural

       roads without capacity expansion for general traffic, and without contribution to deforestation and loss of carbon

       sinks, they are also deemed as UA as per Joint MDB methodology for Paris-Alignment.



8.      The transition risks and carbon lock-in risks exposed to the proposed IWT corridors are considered low. Although

small amounts of coal and petroleum products are expected to be carried by waterways transport using the project

corridors as part of their overall service, as with all transport modes globally:



    (a) The share of coal and petroleum products to be transported using the proposed IWT corridors are immaterial (see

        Figure A4.2). Based on detailed freight demand baseline survey and forecast under the FS, coal and petroleum

        products accounts for about three percent of total demand in 2019. Sugar, cashew, wood, fertilizer, sand and

        stones together contribute to nearly 85 percent of total demand. Such a commodity structure is expected to

        remain largely the same through 2040, with the share of coal and petroleum products expected to increase

        moderately from three percent in 2019 to about nine percent by 2040, in tandem with increasing industrialization.

        Therefore, transporting of coal and petroleum products does not affect the economic viability of the proposed

        project.



    (b) Viet Nam has committed to phase out coal as a part of its energy transition strategy towards meeting net-zero

        target by 2050. In the Mekong Delta where the project corridors are located, the GoV has announced that no

        more new coal-fired thermal power plants will be developed by 2030, expect for the ones currently under

        construction, namely the Duyen Hai II, Long Phu I, and Song Hau I. These new coal-power plants, once

        operationalized, will not lead to additional demand for coal transportation on the project IWT corridors, nor will

        they affect the economic viability of the project corridors when they are eventually phased-out (Box A4.1).

Paris Alignment - Climate Change Adaptation



Assessment of Risks from Current and Future Climate Hazards



9.       For the project area located in the Mekong Delta Region, the primary physical climate risks exposed to the IWT

infrastructure include increased water level variations and flooding. Sea level rise is expected to increase the risk of tidal

inundation and enlarge the corrosive zone inland for the project area. High water flow velocity from flooding leads to

higher risks of riverbank erosion, thereby undermining structural stability. According to the World Bank’s Climate Change

Knowledge Portal, Viet Nam has extremely high exposure to flooding, ranking as the most vulnerable country globally

along with Bangladesh. Floods and storms are the top two most damaging hazards in Viet Nam historically, and this is

expected to exacerbate due to climate change.



10.     Climate change has significant impacts on IWT infrastructure and services, particularly from water level variations

and flooding risks. Figure A4.3 shows an impact chain from climate change to IWT.58 Climate change-induced variations in

temperature, evaporation, and precipitation levels (volume and distribution) affect water levels in inland waterways and

thereby the navigability for IWT.

11.      Furthermore, climate change induces extreme precipitation, riverine flooding, and flash flooding. The increased

flooding results in higher risks of riverbank erosion, thereby undermining the structural stability of IWT channel

infrastructure. During floods, the huge amount of water flows with higher velocity tears away the top layers of soil at

riverbanks. The occurrence of erosion and landslide events along the river/canal network of the Mekong Delta Region has

been occurring at an accelerated pace.



Risks Mitigated and Climate Adaptation Measures



12.     To adapt to the identified physical climate risks, the full project design applies climate resilient engineering

measures to enhance the resilience of the project infrastructure. To mitigate the risk from water level variation and

disrupted IWT navigability, the designed water level for all project corridors is based on the hourly water level with a

frequency of 98 percent, which represents the lowest water levels of the year in the Mekong Delta Region. Such a design

ensures that the water level of the project corridors would be sufficient for navigation, considering water level variations

under seasonal and tidal changes.



13.      To mitigate the flooding risks from sea level rise and extreme precipitation, the crest elevation of all waterways

embankment and bridges are increased by 30cm based on climate projection through 2050 under RCP 8.5 (high-emission

scenario). For the 6 km of local roads that will be affected by waterways embankment works and restored under the

project, the roads’ elevation will be at least 0.5m higher than flooding water levels to ensure climate resilience.



14.      Furthermore, in order to mitigate the risks of riverbank erosion caused by flooding, the project will use a climate-

resilient/friendly reno mattress and concrete block combination as the embankment structure in most river sections. This

embankment technology allows plants to grow on the surface of the reno mattresses and thereby increases the soil

stability at riverbanks against erosion. Embankments along the project rivers will apply this technology wherever

technically feasible. About 80 percent of the new embankment length will have enhanced resilience to climate change-

induced erosion risks.



15.     With the physical climate risks clearly assessed and the risk mitigated incorporated in the project design, the

residual physical climate risks to the project outcome is considered low. The proposed project is aligned with the Paris

Agreement objectives from an adaptation perspective.
