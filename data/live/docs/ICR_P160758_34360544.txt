     I. PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES



     A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL



 Context



 1.       With a population of an estimated 160 million people, Bangladesh is one of the most densely

 populated countries in the world and located in a fertile low-lying river delta of about 144,415 km2, which

 over the last 40 years has become one of the world’s development success story. At independence in

 1971, Bangladesh had the world’s second-lowest per capita income of around US$100, but it has since

 made exceptionally good development progress to raise the per capita income to an estimated US$1,314

 in 2015, reaching the World Bank’s threshold for lower-middle-income countries. It has achieved this

 through a combination of factors, including consistent economic growth, supported by proactive family

 planning; improved food availability; stronger disaster-coping mechanisms; development of an extensive

 rural road network built in the 1980s; special initiatives to tackle gender inequality and promote women’s

 empowerment; and gains in education and employment, among others. Annual gross domestic product

 (GDP) growth averaged around 6 percent between 2005 and 2015, in spite of the global recession, oil

 price rises, unrest in the Middle East (significant as it affects Bangladeshi remittances and migration), and

 local natural disasters. This was largely due to strong macroeconomic fundamentals, growth in export and

 remittances, improved agricultural productivity, and productive structural shifts in the economy especially

 toward manufacturing, which led to significant job growth over time. Progress on reducing extreme

 poverty and boosting shared prosperity improved with a poverty incidence reduction from 48.9 percent

 in 2000 to 24.5 percent in 2016 and extreme poverty from 34.3 percent in 2000 to 13.0 percent in 2016.1



 2.       Despite these achievements, with constrained public services and comparatively weak

 institutions, significant challenges remain to reduce poverty in Bangladesh to its goal of eliminating

 extreme poverty by 2030. Key challenges identified within the Systematic Country Diagnostic (SCD) 2015

 before project design and at project appraisal included the need to accelerate private sector-led growth

 with an enhanced investment climate—through improved infrastructure and improvement of the

 governance structure in spending public resources for better service delivery, among other things. Public

 procurement is a vital function of the Government to ensure value for money in public spending. It is

 estimated that at the time of appraisal, public procurement amounted to over US$7 billion per year,

 accounting for about 24 percent of the annual national budget and 70 percent of the Government’s

 Annual Development Programme (ADP). By the time of the project’s Additional Financing (AF) in 2021,

 the public procurement figure had increased to around US$24 billion in FY2019, which represented 45.2

 percent of the annual government budget, 85 percent of the ADP,2 and 8 percent of the national GDP.



 3.     Following recommendations of the Country Procurement Assessment Report 2002 (CPAR 2002),

 Bangladesh undertook to implement a sustained reform and improvement of its public procurement

 environment, including the initiation of a comprehensive package of reforms supported by subsequent

 World Bank credits under projects—the Public Procurement Reform Projects (PPRP) I and II and two

 subsequent rounds of AF. Reforms included the introduction of an effective nodal agency—the Central

 Procurement Technical Unit (CPTU), new public procurement laws with associated rules and

 documentation, an extensive capacity-building program, and an electronic government procurement (e-

 GP) system through an internet-based portal (www.eprocure.gov.bd), which is a single portal for the

 entire country with an online performance measurement mechanism built into it.



 4.       With PPRP II closed in June 2017, a follow-on project was proposed to further the reforms,

 strengthen and expand the usage of the e-GP system, and enhance stakeholder capacities. Whereas the

 earlier PPRP II focused on the four selected public sector organizations (SPSOs) that were considered to

 have the highest procurement amounts (Roads and Highways Department [RHD], Local Government

 Engineering Department [LGED], Bangladesh Water Development Board [BWDB], and Bangladesh Rural

 Electrification Board [BREB]), this project aimed to expand to 28 newly selected public service

 organizations (NSPSOs), collectively representing approximately 50 percent of government procurement

 (by value) undertaken in Bangladesh and strengthen the system, capacity building, and civil society

 engagement further. Rather than contributing to a specific sector, this would contribute to the Country

 Partnership Framework (CPF) objective of improving governance, use of public funds, efficiency in project

 implementation, and confidence in the use of government systems. Given the existence of a functioning

 e-GP at project start, the Digitizing Implementation Monitoring and Public Procurement Project

 (DIMAPPP) and subsequent AF focused on scaling up usage, enhancing the functionality of the system(s),

 and improving uptake by users through mass training of stakeholders.



 Theory of Change (Results Chain)



 5.       The appraisal documentation does not outline an explicit Theory of Change but rather describes

 the intended and anticipated causal mechanisms of change in descriptive terms. Improvements

 introduced by an electronic procurement system, rather than using a traditional paper-based system, aim

 to improve transparency, accountability, efficiency, and public trust (intermediate outcomes) in the

 procurement system on several levels concurrently. This aims to lead ultimately to improved public

 procurement results (including value for money), improved governance (decreased opportunities for rent

 seeking), and improved investment climate (via both the outputs of procurement investments and the

 integrity of the procurement system). The higher-level outcomes to which these would contribute would

 be decreased poverty, improved investment climate, expenditure efficiency, and improved public service

 delivery (including via savings of procurement redirected to other government purposes).



 6. The Theory of Change is best understood through describing the mechanisms of the system itself.

 Briefly described, the e-GP aims to create an end-to-end system in which oversight is possible, and the

 goals of improved efficiency, security, and probity are met. Procurement notices are advertised, forms

 are made available, documents are submitted and stored, and approvals workflow is managed via an

 electronic system through a secured public internet portal, in such a way that each step is standardized

 and can be reviewed by authorized managers, with reminders sent at each process step so that undue

 delays are not held up at any one point. Once a procurement is finalized, a contract management system

 would then be used to manage the contract implementation workflow, with submissions of evidence of

 work completed and invoices submitted electronically, payments approved and eventually linked through

 the system to a centralized integrated accounting and payment system (IBAS++). Although the project

 does not have this as an indicator nor is the payment made mandatory as at the project end, this was a

 key reform. The project implementation monitoring process was implemented through the introduction

 of an electronic project management information system (e-PMIS), with monitoring of the physical and

 7. On the governance side, the system works through the mechanisms of internal systems transparency

 to increase accountability, which have the effect of improved system efficiency through decreasing delays.

 On the economic efficiency side, rather than the traditional paper-based system, having an integrated

 electronic system allows for bidders to view, download, fill out, and then submit bid documentation

 without the need for travelling physically to receive bidding information and forms, and can submit them

 wherever they have internet access. On the bid evaluation and approvals side (by government

 authorities), an electronic system eliminates the need for those involved in the procurement appraisal

 process to transport documents between different agencies and locations, all of which incur time costs,

 transport costs, security cost, pollution resulting from transport, and other potential losses. The additional

 features and benefits of the e-PMIS system include the ability of IMED to monitor the physical and

 financial progress of ADP implementation through the system, including the collection of associated data

 by which to assess project performance measures. This data provides the basis for meaningful data

 analytics that can inform procurement decisions and policies. Critical to the system performing properly

 is an informed citizenry that can report on implementation, which was addressed in the project through

 comprehensive behavioral change communication activities, citizen engagement and an information and

 reporting portal. Capacity-building initiatives at all levels of government (officials), the bidding

 community, and citizen stakeholders on the public procurement and e-GP are critical to ensuring that

 people are able to use their respective part of the system. The project focused on developing

 organizations within Bangladesh that could provide ongoing training beyond the life of the project.

 8. In terms of improved access, including for women and other groups who are potentially

 disadvantaged, an internet-based system improves access through lowering the barriers to entry

 potentially traditionally imposed through the need for printing documents, physically delivering those

 documents to the correct place and on time (transportation and logistics costs), the time this takes (not

 flexible), and access to information. This may be specific to Bangladesh, where good internet connectivity

 has been established in decentralized locations, conditions that may need development if they are to be

 replicated in other countries, including with services such as internet cafes where relatively skilled

 individuals can assist in using information technology.



 Project Development Objectives (PDOs)



 9.    The PDO is to improve public procurement performance and enhance capacity for

 implementation monitoring of development programs/projects. This PDO is the same in the Financing

 Agreement and the Project Appraisal Document (PAD).



 Key Expected Outcomes and Outcome Indicators

 10.    The expected outcomes of the project address elements of efficiency, transparency, and

 accountability. They are described as aiming to achieve four specific PDO-level outcomes, expected to be

 achieved by project end (2022), which was extended. These are codified as the four PDO results indicators

 from the original project:



         (a)   NSPSOs procurement lead time reduced in National Competitive Bidding (NCB) using e-GP

               system (invitation to contract award) (Target: 70 days, Baseline: 100 days)



         (b)   NSPSOs invite bids in NCB through e-GP system (Target: 75 percent, Baseline: 2 percent)



         (c)   Selected sub-districts engage citizen monitoring in contract implementation (Target: 45 sub-

               districts, Baseline: 4 districts)



         (d)   SPSOs project implementation monitored on-line by IMED (Target: 45 percent of projects,

               Baseline: 0 projects).



 Components



 11.     The project had four components as detailed in the following paragraphs.



 12.     Component 1: Restructuring CPTU and Institutionalizing e-GP (US$9 million [US$11.50 million].

 Actual cost: US$6.2 million). This component aimed to introduce procurement policy reforms and

 restructure the CPTU, a unit of IMED, under the Ministry of Planning into the Bangladesh Public

 Procurement Authority (BPPA), with the aim of instituting greater degree of regulatory power and

 adequately increasing staff professional resources to effectively carry out its procurement regulatory

 functions, including institutionalizing the e-GP.



 13.     Component 2: Enhancing Digitization of Public Procurement (US$28 million [US$63 million];

 Actual cost: US$54.3 million). This component aimed to enhance the coverage of the e-GP to about 900

 public-sector organizations nationally, under 32 SPSOs (identified 28 NSPSs), which have a high overall

 amount of procurement and enhance the functionality of the e-GP system with more advanced features

 including a robust contract management and payment module, modules for different procurement

 methods, and a national tenderers’ database. Activities for strengthening operations and maintenance of

 the e-GP system were introduced. It also aimed to train over 21,500 procurement practitioners, bidders,

 and other stakeholders on the features and use of the e-GP system. A citizen web portal was developed

 and managed by the CPTU.



 14.      Component 3: Professionalizing Procurement and Citizen Engagement (US$18 million [US$22.5

 million]; Actual cost: US$18.6 million). This component aimed to improve public service delivery by

 improving procurement management capacity in the 32 SPSOs, bidder community, and other

 stakeholders; operationalize and strengthen procurement units within the SPSOs; enhance citizen

 monitoring of contract implementation at the decentralized level (in 48 sub-districts [upazilas]);

 institutionalize a government-tenderers forum (GTF); and institute an open contracting data standard for

 public disclosure of procurement information through the citizen portal. A notable feature of the

 component is the large-scale capacity development and professionalization program that aimed to train

 over 10,700 procurement practitioners, bidders, and other stakeholders.



 15.    Component 4: Digitalizing Project Implementation Monitoring (US$5 million [US$8 million];

 Actual cost: US$5.9 million) aimed to strengthen implementation monitoring of development

 programs/projects at the national level by setting up an e-PMIS.



  B. SIGNIFICANT CHANGES DURING IMPLEMENTATION (IF APPLICABLE)



 Revised PDOs and Outcome Targets



 16.      The PDO was not changed during implementation; however, in 2021 AF, two additional PDO

 indicators were added, two outcome targets were revised, and a second restructuring was processed on

 December 23, 2023, to return unused monies to the World Bank. Given the overall increase in ambition

 of the 2021 AF, a split outcome rating is not needed.



 Revised PDO Indicators



 17.      The second PDO indicator, ‘NSPSOs inviting bids in NCB through the e-GP system’, target was

 increased from 75 percent to 100 percent, reportedly “in response to the COVID-19 pandemic,” in

 particular to ensure broad bidder access given COVID-19 conditions of lockdown. The fourth indicator,

 ‘SPSOs’ projects implementation monitored on-line by IMED’, target was decreased from 45 percent to

 25 percent coverage, reportedly because of delays in completing and implementing the new system. Two

 new PDO indicators that reflect additional features were introduced in the AF and represent additional

 ambition for project outcomes.

 Revised Components



 18.     Components were revised to add features especially to the e-GP in Component 2. A number of

 new indicators were also added to reflect these additions. These included the following:



  •     Develop sustainable procurement policy and implementation roadmap (Yes/No). Rationale: The

        development of a sustainable procurement policy aimed to include targeted development

        opportunities to small and medium enterprises, start-ups, women-led/owned enterprises, and

        aspects of green procurement that encourages energy-efficient/low-carbon emitting/climate-

        related, disaster-resilient goods, works, and services.

  •     Develop mechanism to identify and track procurement performance of the women-led/owned

        enterprises (Yes/No). Before the AF, there was no system to identify the percentage of women-

        led/owned enterprises participating and getting contracts in public procurement. The e-GP system’s

        firm registration process was to collect gender-disaggregated data. Separately, indicators were to

        be included in the procurement management information system to monitor participation and

        receipt of contract award by women-led/owned enterprises. The aim was to track these to later

        target development opportunities for women-led/-owned enterprises to increase their

        participation in public procurement.

  •     Strengthen country’s emergency procurement and contract management framework (Yes/No).

        The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted important procurement and contract management issues

        faced in emergency situations of urgent need, such as timeliness and quality of the deliverables,

        shortage of supplies/materials, volatility of prices, disruptions in supply chains and accessibility to

        project sites, financial constraints of contractors, contract implementation delays, variation

        orders/claims, transparency and integrity, and appropriate government oversight on ex post basis

        keeping in view the urgency considerations. A need was identified for a comprehensive assessment

        of the existing, using lessons learned during the pandemic and previous natural disasters, to Identify

        systemic gaps and recommend improvements for effective implementation.

 Other Changes



 19.      Additional Financing (AF). An AF was completed in January 2021 with an additional IDA credit of

 US$40.0 million, which maintained the original four components and PDO but revised the loan closing

 date, the Results Framework, and components and costs; reallocated between disbursement categories;

 and revised the legal covenants.



 20.      Revised closing dates. The loan was extended from June 30, 2022, to December 31, 2023. The

 main reason for this extension and changes was to complete the remaining activities that were affected

 by lock-down restrictions imposed during the COVID-19 pandemic between March and May 2020 and

 gradual reopening of government processes, services, and procurement activities thereafter, in response

 to some changing conditions that arose in the meantime.



 21.       Return of unspent monies and arrangements by the Government to continue the project

 activities. Toward the end of the project, the World Bank received a letter from the Government of

 Bangladesh (GOB) requesting extension of the project to complete unmet targets using available monies;

 however, the decision was taken by the World Bank management to close the project on time as

 scheduled. This marked the end of a long series of projects supporting the development of a procurement

 reform through to e-GP in Bangladesh, which after 21 years (since 2002) is entering a mature stage of

 development in which it can be self-sustaining. In the final supervision mission of December 2023, it was

 agreed with the CPTU/BPPA that the GOB would aim to achieve PDO-level indicators and intermediatory

 indicators by June 2024 and progressively staff the BPPA by December 31, 2024, using the GOB’s own

 funds. Unspent money (SDR 12,317,000) was cancelled on December 27, 2023, before project closing.

 23.       The rationale for the change of indicators were as follows: the second PDO indicator (b) ‘NSPSOs

 during the contract implementation aimed to ensure better efficiency in contract management. Among

 certification, and virtual work monitoring through geotagging and electronic payment. The rationale for

 value procurements. Processing of ICB though e-GP aimed to mitigate this issue and increase efficiency

 and transparency for high-value procurements under both the GOB and development partner-funded

 projects.

 not fully met, based on the following rationale: (i) The BPPA has committed to completing the pending

 activities and achieving the pending results within the next 12 months; (ii) this closure provides a

 necessary pause after continuous World Bank support spanning over two decades, offering a period to

 evaluate the sustainability of the public procurement reforms implemented; and (iii) it allows both the

 BPPA and the Bank an opportunity to reassess and strategize the implementation of the next generation

 of public procurement reforms. This strategic pause is essential for integrating lessons learned and to

 design innovative apaproach for the next generation of public procurement reforms.



 26.       Impact on the Theory of Change. The original Theory of Change was relevant throughout the

 project irrespective of changes in the external conditions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. If anything,

 the underpinning overall rationale and efficacy theory of an e-GP became even stronger due to COVID,

 because of the difficulties related to travel and interpersonal communication in a context of needing to

 isolate from each other.

 37.       Indicator 5 not achieving its target outcome has a few possible explanations: (a) insufficient

 focus of SPSOs in the final months of the project to push procuring entities to use the system, (b) not

 enough/sufficient training for stakeholders on how to use the system, and (c) possible preference of users

 accountability. The system is not yet mandatory, and it may be that the environment is not sufficient to

 encourage or require SPSO users to use the system without a strong push from SPSO and BPPA leadership.

 strengthening of e-GP system may divert rent seeking focus of actors to other, higher potential areas,

 questions and the slow uptake of the e-CMS in the final twelve months of implementation, it is not assured

 that this uptake and indicator will be achieved within the next six to twelve months. At a minimum, the

 same positive reinforcement mechanisms that enabled successful implementation of e-GP systems, such

 as concert direction by the BPPA management, implement change management ,capacity building and

 communication will be required for successful implementation of e-CMS. A formal mandate would be

 even more powerful, possibly even necessary given the incentives against widespread adoption.

 52.      The project lowers the barriers to entry for women-owned businesses by making the overall

 process of procurement more accessible to a wider cross section of bidders through being online (no need

 businesses ineligible according to standard criteria.

  III. KEY FACTORS THAT AFFECTED IMPLEMENTATION AND OUTCOME



  A. KEY FACTORS DURING PREPARATION



 57.      Project design. The project was designed based on previous reforms and an e-GP system that had

 been in gradual development since 2002, built upon a vision that the GOB itself had expressed. Drawing

 on an established, competent, technical unit (CPTU) for implementation, there was both capacity and

 significant prior lessons learned upon which to build the project design. An established commitment from

 the Government at both a technical level and the support of the Prime Minister’s office, helped maintain

 momentum and commitment among stakeholders. A simple design was established, which aimed to build

 system capacity into the e-GP platform itself, by adding features to the system through modules, train the

 different stakeholders to use the systems, provide a helpdesk to address questions, and establish a civil

 society engagement function.



 58.     The design was comparatively simple but with many parts, focusing on empowering the central

 procurement technical unit through institutionalizing it as an independent authority; increasing the

 functionality of the e-GP systems, including expanding the types of procurement possible through the

 system (to ICB); increasing stakeholder capacities; and instituting an end-to-end system that aims to

 eradicates loopholes and thereby discourages rent seeking. The AF drew upon the 2020 Assessment of

 Bangladesh Public Procurement System, a comprehensive assessment of the system. One aspect that

 could have been strengthened in design was the inclusion of an assessment of IT security design needs

 ahead of time, particularly for the ICB module. When it was determined, during implementation, that a

 stronger IT software infrastructure would be required than originally anticipated, this presented as a delay

 in implementing the module, whereas an earlier identification could have anticipated this and been

 planned. As it turned out, this delay did not affect the outcomes negatively, but the increased systems

 security can be seen as a strong positive feature.



 59.    Results Framework and risks. The Results Framework was basically sound, focusing on the

 improvement of the e-GP system, training, and key policies or reform items. Risks identified during design

 were mostly mitigated well, the most significant of which being the need to provide capacity-building



                                                                                                        Page 21 of 74

The World Bank

Digitizing Implementation Monitoring and Public Procurement Project (P160758)







 training to a wide range of stakeholders to use and administer the new e-GP system. One risk that was

 not explicitly identified and mitigated was the political economy issue of possible resistance to systems

 update of the e-CMS (including potentially resulting from limiting opportunities for rent seeking), not

 mentioned in the AF (pp 18−19) at all. Capacity for implementation is mentioned; however, incentives for

 full implementation are not. In the absence of a mandated (legislative) requirement to use the system,

 the measures would incentivize stakeholders to use a contract management system (e-CMS) that would

 hold them to greater account are less clear. More could have been done to identify this risk and mitigation

 measures.



  B. KEY FACTORS DURING IMPLEMENTATION

 60.     There were four main factors during implementation: commitment of government was very

 strong at the outset, the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic had a substantial impact on implementation of

 the project, large amounts of training led to high adoption rates once the system was in place for the

 NSPSOs, and there was a slowdown in progress toward the new PDOs in the last six months of the project.

 It was discovered, or realized, that a significant upgrade to system security would be needed for

 developing the ICB module. This took time to implement, causing a delay in the completion of the ICB

 module; however, the overall system was strengthened. There was a delay in institutionalizing the CPTU

 to the BPPA by the National Parliament, a key condition of effectiveness.



 61.    There was uncertainty of project extension during the last year of project. Otherwise, the

 DIMAPPP was implemented with excellent direction and support, based on a solid, sustained series of

 procurement reforms since 2002. It achieved most of its development objectives along a continuum

 toward a comprehensively improved public procurement system.



  IV. BANK PERFORMANCE, COMPLIANCE ISSUES, AND RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME



  A. QUALITY OF MONITORING AND EVALUATION (M&E)



 M&E Design



 62.      The quality of the M&E design was overall excellent, with key indicators used to measure

 important aspects of e-GP feature delivery and project implementation progress, with a couple of caveats.

 The Theory of Change is not systematically explained in 2017 within the project document, relying instead

 on an implied understanding by readers of the importance and relevance of features of the proposed

 DIMAPPP and DIMAPPP-AF based on earlier and other diagnostic work, such as the 2020 Assessment of

 Bangladesh Public Procurement System, upon which the DIMAPPP-AF draws its list of priority additional

 features. Indicators are specific, measurable, relevant, and time bound. They all have baselines and

 targets. Most indicators are output based and therefore easy to track. For the most part they are

 achievable, with the caveat that PDO Indicator 5 relies on bid preparation activities that falls outside the

 full control of the project, and PDO Indicator 6 relies similarly on system uptake by staff who are directed

 by management other than the CPTU Director General (BPPA CEO), and this management

 direction/mandate also is outside the direct control of the project. In the case of Indicator 5, the project

 might better have selected an indicator that measured percentage of readied bids advertised rather than

 a defined absolute number.





                                                                                                        Page 22 of 74

The World Bank

Digitizing Implementation Monitoring and Public Procurement Project (P160758)







 63.     Under the Results Framework, procurement performance is primarily indicated by the measure

 of time to NCB contract award as a measure of efficiency (Indicator 1); while PDO Indicators 2−6 are

 intermediate (outcome) indicators, in that they relate to system usage rather than as a direct

 measurement of improved performance. As they are not inherently performance indicators, this places a

 strong reliance on the robustness or efficacy of the system design and its links to performance through

 the Theory of Change. From this perspective, while it may be ideal to include performance indicators such

 as ‘reduction in ICB contracting time’ (for the e-GP ICB module) or ‘reduction in variance between agreed

 contract implementation delivery schedule and actual’ (for e-CMS), these kinds of indicators cannot be

 used unless the module is physically available and the procuring entities use these modules.



 64.      One can reasonably ask “is the e-GP system mature enough to deliver performance data?” If so,

 it can be asked, “did the e-GP improve procurement outcomes,” by which we might mean not just faster

 procurement but successful completion of projects procured through the system (that is, in less time, with

 higher quality, with fewer issues). As a stand-alone systems-building project focused on procurement, this

 M&E framework makes sense for its stage of development; however, it does not link all the way to broader

 development effectiveness. Other than Indicator 1 and likely large cost savings, it is not clear from this

 M&E framework whether implementation of projects on the ground was improved as a result of e-GP. An

 agency does not need an e-GP or e-CMS to track performance data (for example, time to procurement

 award or contract completion)—it just makes it easier and automated to collect it. For a broader M&E

 design more integrated with development outcomes, an end-to-end e-GP that more fully develops

 performance indicators would be needed, especially for e-CMS. This would be part of a next-stage

 development of the system.



 M&E Implementation

 65.     A large amount of the needed M&E data was collected using the e-GP system itself, by the CPTU

 and the implementing agencies, allowing for and providing a rich, detailed, and comprehensive set of data

 of various aspects of e-GP implementation. Baseline data were compiled at project start, with subsequent

 changes in system registration, time taken for procurements, numbers of helpdesk assistance calls, and

 other data routinely compiled from developed systems. Data were adequate to report in Implementation

 Status and Results Reports. Training data are largely output based and provided by training organizations.

 M&E functions and processes are integrated into e-GP systems and highly likely to be sustained after

 project closing.



 M&E Utilization

 66.      The project effectively used the original and AF Results Frameworks to guide implementation and

 target setting, up until the last six months, when targets for some intermediate and PDO indicators were

 not met as expected but not reported as being deficient. The AF was flexible in amending targets to take

 account of implementation progress considering COVID-19 conditions, increasing the ambition for one

 indicator where progress had been better than expected, and downgrading one where systems’

 development had been slow. An overreliance on output-type indicators means that the internal quality of

 outcomes is largely unknown, despite reaching numerical targets in training outputs, numbers of staff

 accredited, systems being used, and even contracts being implemented. A more performance-based style

 of indicator will be needed in future to continue improving quality of system development, in addition to

 scope and usage.

 M&E Quality rating: Substantial



 67.     Overall, the quality of the M&E is Substantial. The M&E system is designed in such a way that it is

 terms of choices of indicator, and reliance on output indicators, the indicators chosen were largely

 appropriate given the stage of the e-GP systems’ development. Candor was lacking in reporting on some

 indicators in the last year of implementation, which most likely negatively affected urgency toward

  B. ENVIRONMENTAL, SOCIAL, AND FIDUCIARY COMPLIANCE



 68.      The project was assigned a Category ‘C’ rating at preparation, indicating that no environmental

 impacts were expected during project implementation. This held for the AF in 2021, where a waiver was

 given. A robust social safeguards policy was introduced within the project for grievances and workplace

 non-discrimination. The social risk is low for the project. The CPTU carried out stakeholder consultations,

 including the implementation of the specific subcomponent on citizen engagement in public procurement

 and contract implementation monitoring. The Bangladesh Citizen Portal for Procurement

 (https://citizen.cptu.gov.bd/) has forms for citizens to provide feedback and the CPTU conducted an

 awareness program on behavioral change and communication with the assistance of a firm. A mobile

 phone app was also developed. An annual audit was a requirement, and there were no unresolved audit

 issues at project end. No fiduciary issues were recorded during project implementation.



  C. BANK PERFORMANCE

 Quality at Entry

 69.       Project quality at entry was moderately high, with clear, realistic objectives, a clear Results

 Framework with baseline targets, measurable goals, and institutional arrangements in place to measure

 them. Based on previous projects’ experience and lessons learned, project design seemed appropriate in

 that it focused on next steps to strengthen the institutional, policy, and systems of procurement. The 2021

 Assessment of Bangladesh’s Procurement System found, however, that procurement assessments of the

 Bangladeshi system up to that point had been either out of date (the World Bank’s CPAR of 2002) or not

 comprehensive enough (PEFA16 of 2010 and 2016; e-GP assessment of 2017) by not comparing the entire

 spectrum of the public procurement system with international good practices and standards. This

 suggests that there was a deficiency of technical studies to inform the original design, a deficiency that

 was ameliorated through the 2020 assessment that was used to inform the AF.



 70.      A strength of the program design and implementation was that it focused on fundamental next

 step elements of procurement institutional, policy, and systems reform, which were completed before

 taking on additional iterative steps, rather than attempting to do many things at once and not achieving

 any fully before moving to a next thing. The revised AF added additional elements based on important

 next step improvements. Two aspects were not adequately incorporated in the revised AF Results

 Framework: (a) the project was not directly responsible for preparing ICBs, so it should have selected a

 PDO indicator to reflect that the e-GP system would advertise those ICBs prepared, not a set number of

                                                                                                                    Page 24 of 74

The World Bank

 ICBs, which was out of its control (b) there are factors that affect whether a system will be implemented

 or not, both the capacity to implement and incentives to implement, which represent risks that the project

 did not sufficiently analyze these political economy/incentive/behavioral factors in either entry or AF

 preparation or mitigate them in its design. Stakeholders identified insufficient training as a reason that

 the PDO target was not met; however, the issue and risk of incentives to (not) use the system was not

 recognized or mitigated in design. A third issue was that there were insufficient technical studies done on

 the IT system requirements for an ICB contracting module for the AF, which led to significant delays during

 implementation. Lastly, the calculation of the ERR in the AF does not explain its assumption for cost

 savings, and its ERR estimates over US$ 9 billion without linking the e-CMS in the calculation. For an ERR

 to be credible, able to be validated, and replicable across projects the methodologies and assumptions

 need to be explicit, clear, and realistic.



 Quality of Supervision

 71.      There was a high capacity in the World Bank team, located in Dhaka, which was helpful to the

 CPTU. The World Bank team in place in-country was able to oversee the project and support it in real

 time. Many team members had long experience with the e-GP, which helped resolve issues as they arose.

 Support and supervision were demonstrably excellent under tough conditions during the COVID-19

 pandemic, and the GOB and World Bank mobilized to take the opportunity of building on gains to expand

 the e-GP system with increased ambition in the AF. High-quality supervision support early on is offset

 somewhat by a lack of attention to reporting candor or realism in the last 12 to 18 months of

 implementation. Clear messaging that PDO indicators 5 and 6 were not on track might have prompted

 management action to push for those results, actions lacking in the absence of candid reporting. Similarly,

 if it was known by GOB that there would be no project extension, this could have been communicated by

 the Bank as early as it was known may have allowed the GOB to push for project results in the final six

 months. Actions could have included additional training in e-CMS to NSPOs and a concerted push from

 GOB management for staff to use the e-CMS module (indicator 5), and potentially additionally support

 given to contracting agencies to expedite the preparation of ICB packages (indicator 6). A contributing

 factor to low candor and the timing of communications to the client may have been that there was also

 a high turnover of Task Team Leaders in the last year of implementation. Another key factor in GOB’s

 ability to push for results may have been that right until the last quarter, a key condition of effectiveness

 had still not been met, one expected in 2021—the institutionalization by Parliament of the BPPA.



 Justification of Overall Rating of Bank Performance

 72.     Moderately Satisfactory. Overall, the World Bank team had close coordination with the GOB,

 CPTU, and communications and technical assistance was good. A higher degree of risk identification,

 mitigation, and candor in reporting could have improved the World Bank’s response to components

 where the PDO indicators were not ultimately achieved, particularly in revising PDO Indicator 5 to one

 that was achievable and using available funds to increase training in the use of e-CMS to increase uptake.



  D. RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME

 73.     Medium. There are two primary risks to development outcome, specifically to increasing and

 maintaining appropriate staffing levels for the BPPA and, second, to continued government commitment

 to implementing reforms and institutionalization of using the e-GP and its contract management modules.

 Ensuring adequate staffing of the BPPA was one of the indicators not yet met by project closing; this will





                                                                                                         Page 25 of 74

The World Bank

Digitizing Implementation Monitoring and Public Procurement Project (P160758)







 require increased staffing to handle the increase in volume of procurement through the system, calls for

 assistance to the helpdesk, and systems maintenance. This risk ought to be mitigated by the existence of

 surplus revenues that the BPPA earns from e-GP fees to users, compared to operating costs.



 74.      The second risk is the maintenance of government commitment to push through in the reforms

 and maintain them. On the specific PDO goal of instituting contracts management, it remains to be seen

 how difficult it might be to fully implement a contract management system in which 100 percent of

 contracts are processed through electronic means. With the reelection of the Prime Minister for a historic

 fourth term and her emphasis on promoting a Digital Bangladesh, the timing is optimal to push forward

 for the completion of regulations requiring this and/or the department heads to require it of their staff.

 However, there will always be strong incentives for individuals to seek rents where there are weak

 enforcements and/or transparency safeguards and sanction measures. Strong requirements such as

 mandatory use will be needed for the e-CMS to be successfully implemented, and more broadly, high-

 level government support for a system will be needed to maintain it over time.



 75.     Once established, as it has been to date, there is low risk of the e-GP procurement system, as

 currently implemented, devolving into ineffectiveness and inefficiency. Having established the protocols,

 rules, and funding mechanisms that are self-sustaining sets it up well for the future. Nonetheless,

 adequate staffing levels and training to support both users and PEs and other stakeholders will remain an

 ongoing need.



  V. LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS



 76.     Lessons learned are clustered around three points.



 77.    First, strong and consistent signaling from political leadership is crucially important for difficult

 systems reforms like this one where there is potential for rent seeking. This is a lesson learned from

 previous iterations of the project and which tells a consistent story. The project was fortunate to have

 strong high-level support at the outset and throughout the project, as well as from mid-level

 administrators, with only some uncertainty toward the end of the project. In difficult reforms, timing is

 important. Given the fresh new term of the current political leadership, it is recommended to take

 advantage at the beginning of this new term by pushing for the next set of reforms and the completion of

 the e-CMS institutionalization.



 78.     Institutionalization in this context means that mandates be put in place that require officials to

 use the e-GP systems, including the contract management system and linked iBAS++ payment system. It

 may not be possible to eradicate all loopholes and opportunities for rent seeking from within a system;

 however, the full use of an integrated system will, by design, cut down on the entry points (opportunities)

 even if an incentive remains. As of project end, there was still the option to not use the contract

 management system, and payments for contracts were reportedly still being issued by direct check from

 departments, linked to treasury through the iBAS++ without routing through e-CMS.



 79.      Second, project reporting with candor is more likely to lead to course adjustment that improves

 results. This point related to the first lesson and specifically refers to the slowing down of progress in the

 last six months to a year of the project on Indicators 5 and 6—e-CMS and ICB notices. A main reason for

 the low achievement of the uptake of the e-CMS contract management system was reported—by project



                                                                                                          Page 26 of 74

The World Bank

Digitizing Implementation Monitoring and Public Procurement Project (P160758)







 stakeholders—as insufficient training for users of the system to become comfortable with it. Taken at face

 value, if this is the case, this could have been addressed by using available funds to provide that training

 in the final year. However, signaling from World Bank ISRs was that the project indicators were all on track

 and Satisfactory and did not trigger any urgent response from the Government or the World Bank. A less

 rosy picture would more likely have prompted a more urgent set of follow-up actions on PDO Indicators

 5 and 6. Also, a more candid assessment may have led to a closer look at the indicators themselves and

 possibly a restructuring to bring them in line with what was more likely possible. Indicators can mislead a

 team in the assessment of results. Teams should remain vigilant as to what the project can reasonably

 control, design indicators accordingly, report candidly, and scale up and/or adjust activities to meet the

 target. In the case of PDO Indicator 6, a percentage of ready bids would have been a better indicator. In

 the case of Indicator 5, more candid reporting might have led to more training and a closer focus that

 could have increased the percentage uptake of using the e-CMS.



 80.     Third, if the Theory of Change is outlined clearly, comprehensively, and explicitly, it can be

 understood and applied more rapidly to other country situations. A project like this has application to

 many countries around the world. One of many admirable things about the DIMAPPP-AF was the clarity

 of those involved as to what they were doing and trying to achieve. The result of it being a long process

 of gradual improvement was that everyone seemed to know what it was about. This singular clarity in

 purpose and goals is helpful and can create a virtuous cycle as the word spreads from the center to many

 more people. This clarity has an implication for the Results Framework and Theory of Change, for which

 there is a specific recommendation: a systems improvement project can move more clearly to

 performance-type indicators when the steps in systems improvement are clearly articulated in a Theory

 of Change that is larger than the project itself. In this project, most PDO indicators are systems usage

 based—whether a system is used or not—rather than how well the system itself performs, with the

 exception of Indicator 1. In a fully developed and advanced system, those indicators would be considered

 intermediate outcome indicators and the outcomes would be in terms of speed and efficiency. This project

 represents a part of a continuum towards an ‘ideal’ e-procurement system, which is not quite complete

 and not quite comprehensive, yet. A diagrammatic and explanatory Theory of Change that places this

 project and its aims on an idealized continuum would help in determining where to focus and iterate next

 steps.



 81.      A fourth lesson learned, not substantially different from those in previous projects, is a part of

 design that is worth repeating. The type and extent of training is important to a systems development

 project, for effective uptake and usage. Extensive training is a key to success. Supporting this broad

 training, a helpdesk set up by the project was important for real-time assistance. This must be adequately

 staffed, with specialists for tricky issues and continue even after the project closing. In the DIMAPPP-AF,

 there was a focus on developing not just trainers to deliver future trainings but also institutions and

 training centers that could provide training in the future.



 82.      Public Procurement is vulnerable to rent seeking behavior (corruption) and IT can be

 productively used to address this issue through increasing public transparency (publication of bidding

 documents, awards); decreasing opportunities for rent seeking (monitoring of process steps); and

 improving service delivery at the community recipient level (community monitoring and reporting). e-GP

 is a disruptive technology and successful implementation in Bangladesh provides lessons for other

 countries who are embarking on this journey. Government representatives from more than 18 countries

 visited Bangladesh to learn e-GP implementation, which is a testimony to the work Bangladesh has done



                                                                                                        Page 27 of 74

The World Bank

Digitizing Implementation Monitoring and Public Procurement Project (P160758)







 and the model it is pursuing. The key success factors in this project are good communication,

 implementation of change management and a capacity building component with adequate budget - this

 approach made the difference. Another aspect is citizen engagement, a transformative tool to empower

 citizens to participate in project implementation and question the government on issues they observe in

 real time.



 83.      Reform processes take time, and they must be completed in good order if they are to succeed.

 A strength of the DIMAPPP-AF project, and the preceding projects, is that they were focused on

 completing institutional, policy, and systemic reforms in a stepwise fashion, and did not engage in ‘scope

 creep’ by attempting to do more than was possible at the time. The reform process culminating in the

 DIMAPPP-AF project has been ongoing since 2002, with limited demonstrable direct impacts beyond its

 specific functional systems, private sector and public trust, and procurement efficiency improvements.

 Beyond metrics of procurement speed and the use of specific systems, a truly improved system will

 demonstrate improved goods, service, and infrastructure quality and value-for-money over time,

 something that is part of a holistic view of system’s improvement and a mature advanced procurement

 system. Without such metrics it is difficult to know what effects on-the-ground an e-GP is having.

 Integration with sectoral outcome performance metrics have not been a focus of this project and

 represent a next step in the evolution of this kind of system as it gradually advanced and developed to

 maturity.



 84.      As part of an Economic Analysis, a project should detail how each component adds value to the

 outcomes, rather than quantify only what is straightforward in order to justify an investment. The

 analysis provides not just a minimum bar to be cleared, but basis for comparative strategic decision

 making in the future. That is, if it is found that some kinds of projects yield better social and economic

 returns than others, they can be pursued with greater clarity on a more solid evidence base if that analysis

 is done up-front and ex post validation of projected outcomes are confirmed. In the case of the DIMAPPP-

 AF, the key PDO indicator that was not achieved was the utilization of a completed e-CMS module (PDO

 indicator 5), a component which has the potential and the promise to improve on-the-ground outcomes

 in terms of implementation quality, timeliness, and accountability across the categories of goods, works,

 and consulting services in all sectors. For a systems transformation project to show its worth it must go

 beyond internal utilization and process measures to the next step of full integration with measurements

 of results on-the-ground.

                                            ANNEX 4. EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS



 1.       Efficiency of the project is assessed in terms of how economically resources and inputs were

 converted to results and whether the project realized an appropriate return on the opportunity cost of

 its investment. The analysis includes aspects of costs versus benefits and non-economic efficiencies that

 arise out of design and implementation. The efficiency measure for the project is rated based on

 efficiencies expected of the project compared to those achieved, including the question as to whether the

 project delivered as much, more, or less than expected for its inputs. The scale of achievements of the

 project outcomes, compared to the inputs of the project, is also considered. The assessment is organized

 around economic and non-economic considerations of efficiency.



 2.      The choice of components and activities were efficient in that each was directly appropriate to

 achieving the overall development objectives. The aim of the project was to improve system efficiency,

 sustainability, access, and accountability of the Bangladeshi public procurement system. It was designed

 to strengthen the system through improving e-GP system functionality, through the training of

 procurement entity staff and other stakeholders, and strengthen monitoring mechanisms including civil

 engagement. For a project such as this one that itself focuses on improving efficiency in a system, the

 focus of this analysis is on processes and resources used to effect those changes rather than the

 improvements themselves (which are efficacy).



 3.       Economic efficiency of the Bangladesh procurement system itself is believed to have been

 significantly improved through the project, for a relatively small investment. With an estimated US$24

 billion per year in public procurements, the savings of using the e-GP system to the GOB was cited at the

 AF stage as up to US$1.1 billion per year and US$8 billion throughout the life of the DIMAPPP-AF.17 The

 ICR was not able to verify the modelling for this estimate, partly as the number reference is not found

 within the Assessment of Bangladesh Public Procurement System (MAPS) paper as cited. Nonetheless, an

 independent impact evaluation of the e-GP in Bangladesh, as it was then, found “overwhelmingly positive

 impacts of e-procurement on a wide range of outcomes tracked” from both direct income effects, savings

 to government and bidders, and spillover indirect economic effects.18



 4.      A separate evaluation found that electronic procurement led to cost savings of around US$600

 million between FY12 and FY18 for the GOB,19 allowing the Government to increase provision of basic

 services. Including multiplier effects, the total impact on Bangladesh’s GDP is likely to have been between

 0.48 percent and 0.54 percent in 2019 (the last year of data available), or in absolute terms, between

 US$0.94 billion and US$1.07 billion (in 2011 prices).20 The e-GP is also likely to have led to employment

 increases in Bangladesh, at approximately 0.15 percent to 0.17 percent, or around 93,000 to 105,000

 additional jobs in total. The largest economic impacts of e-GP are from cost savings accruing to the GOB.

 This productivity increase in government tendering would allow it to increase output in other areas, and

 5.       An ERR was not calculated at original project design; however, at the AF stage an ERR was

 calculated, with ERR for direct revenue generation from the e-GP system: 44 percent, and ERR for all

 economic benefits of the e-GP system: 180 percent. This is considered a very high rate of return, with

 typical and acceptable ERRs in the private sector around 10-15 percent and sometimes less in the public

 sector when there’s a strong public good component.



 6.       Economic Analysis at the ICR aimed at confirming achievement of the estimated ERR based on

 the same assumptions made during the AF, however the ICR team found that a key assumption of cost

 savings in the original AF ERR was not able to be validated as realistic. Benefit streams include direct

 revenue generated from fees and registrations, cost savings by government using the e-GP platform,

 benefits from reduced greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and cost savings by the private sector through

 using the e-GP.22 Cost streams include direct costs (original IDA, AF, and GOB costs) and indirect costs

 including staff time spent on training and management. Direct revenue estimates used in the 2021 AF

 projected a direct revenue to the CPTU/BPPA from the e-GP of a total of US$333.5 million, whereas the

 actual revenue generated was US$261 million, or 22 percent less, lower than projected at AF but still very

 high. By project end the project spent 81 percent of IDA and GOB financing, and the total number of staff

 trained achieved the target numbers. The intermediate indicators not achieved are at the policy level and

 there is no link with project expenditure; thus, there was no significant change in terms of the cost

 estimates. It is assumed that no additional cost was required for the benefit to continue for the project

 period. By ICR, only one PDO indicator 5 was not achieved - on utilization of the contract management

 system – which is key indicator and a component that impacts quality of procured goods, works and

 consultancy implementation outcomes but which appears not to be included in ERR model estimations.

 Not including an explicit analysis of the economic benefits of e-CMS utilization on implementation cost,

 quality, timeliness and accountability is a missed opportunity to test and demonstrate the efficacy of an

 important aspect of the e-GP approach.



 7.       The ERR calculation on indirect benefits in the AF is heavily biased by the number of bids, and

 as this is multiplied by a savings per bid assumption (i.e. rebate rate), the actual ERR value is highly

 dependent on the assumed level of savings per bid. At AF appraisal, it was projected that a total of 420,000

 bids would use the e-GP in its 5 years of implementation (average 84,000 per year); by the end of the

 project an estimated 674,500 bids used the e-GP after 6.5 years (average 103,800 per year).23 The number

 of average bids using the e-GP during the project period is about 24 percent higher compared to the

 baseline projection. Of note is that in the AF economic analysis the total economic cost savings is

 estimated at US$ 8,176.6 million,24 more than 13 times the total cost savings of the system between FY12

 and FY18 found in the 2020 MAPS assessment, and 18 percent higher than the upper range of the

 economy-wide effect estimated in the Cambridge Econometrics impact evaluation for the time-period.

 This gives pause to reconsider that assumption which makes the estimated cost savings rebate rate likely

 unnaturally high. The ERR calculation at AF was done in BDT. Therefore, exchange rate fluctuation is

 8.      Summarizing the above, based on the fact that the project disbursed 81 percent of the available

 funding envelope to achieve the results, the slightly lower revenue income, and the slightly higher bid

 volume, the actual ERR is likely to be around the estimate if assumptions are maintained, except that the

 rebate rate should be re-examined for realism. In real terms the ERR remains very high, and will be likely

 even higher in the near future when factoring in the throughput of the ADB portfolio’s procurement (of

 US$1+ billion per year) now that the ICB module is complete, something not factored into ERR projections.

 For future ERRs the economic impact of an e-CMS should be explicitly examined.



 9.      The business model developed for the e-GP aimed to be self-sufficient in operating costs

 through the charging of fees for services (tenderers’ registration fee, renewal of registration, and sale of

 tender documents). It earned US$261 million in total fees from 2017 to the end of December 2023 (BDT

 2,198.02 crore), which more than covers the cost of operations and maintenance (estimated at US$4.2

 million per year) and contributes to financial sustainability. This is largely achieved through a large

 increase in the number of subscribers, who each pay a marginal fee to register and access online bidding

 documents.



 10.     The cost to bidders is considered to be marginally lower per entity than the cost of a paper-

 based system; however, the real benefit to bidders is in market access, which is indicated by the enormous

 increase in registered bidders from 23,000 in FY17 to 75,000 in FY20, 111,177 by the end of project,

 December 2023. This can be considered a high increase in efficiency of access and 11 percent above

 expectations set at project design.



 11.    In design, the main project measure of efficiency was framed in terms of time expected to

 process bids to award (PDO Indicator 1), measured in days. The expected result of that improvement

 was reduced processing time from 100 days to 70 days; the project achieved 54 days by project end, above

 expectation.



 12.      The onset of the COVID 19 pandemic reduced the implementation efficiency of the project, but

 was outside the control of the project. These delays directly led to the AF restructuring in 2021, which

 introduced an increased ambition to strengthen certain modules including the contract management

 system (e-CMS) and the ambition to develop and roll out the ICB module, along with ambitious targets.

 Completion of both the e-CMS and ICB modules was delayed, placing pressure on especially the e-CMS

 training and implementation rollout. The adoption of the BPPA Act was also delayed, from an anticipated

 2021 adoption to the end of 2023, which delayed the implementation of an organogram and staff hiring

 to beyond the project end date.



 13.      The project achieved most of its main PDO goals; however, there were some that were not

 achieved, albeit with some funds unspent at project end. If funds had all been spent and some goals not

 met, it could be said that this was inefficient or vice versa that if all goals were met and with funds left

 over it would be considered efficient. In this case, with funds left over and not all goals met, there might

 have been a project extension, and in the penultimate ISR, it was noted that the project could meet all

 targets with the available funding envelope if given an extension of time. Prima facie, this suggests a parity

 of efficiency and ‘on track’ in terms of funding; however, the need for additional time can itself be said to

 be inefficient assessment of time relative to the AF project design, especially as time extensions impose

 staff costs and other opportunity costs not always accounted formally. In project management, time

 overrun is generally considered an indicator of inefficiency.



 14.      Two of the PDO targets introduced at the AF stage ultimately not being met can potentially be

 framed in efficiency terms; the project may be said to have not allocated enough resources to ramp up

 activities toward the achievement of those targets. Noting this as a counterfactual, improved results could

 have been achieved through the project providing additional trainings (in how to use the e-CMS),

 additional hands-on support to prepare bidding documents for international tender (ICB), and so on, to

 spend the available funds to reach the goals on time and on budget. While in principle this might sound

 possible and appealing, in practical terms, this was not feasible. Nonetheless, the project cannot be said

 to have been superlatively efficient (High rating) if it did not meet or exceed its targets on time or on

 budget.



 15.     Another factor that may have contributed to implementation outcomes was that there were

 three different World Bank supervision Task Team Leaders (TTLs) in the final year. Leadership change

 adds an element of inefficiency as it takes some time for each new person to become familiar with the

 project and be able to effectively push for results, and may create incentives for a rosy implementation

 record to be maintained.



 16.     In conclusion, the project introduced procurement system reforms, and extensive training

 across stakeholders that likely massively increased the efficiency of the Bangladesh procurement

 system (efficacy), with a very reasonable allocation of resources (efficiency), however did not achieve

 all goals within the target time frame. The processing time for national procurements in particular

 succeeded above expectations. Because there were delays in achieving targets in certain aspects of the

 AF components (e-CMS and international bidding platform in particular), available resources were not

 used in a way that adequately accelerated e-CMS training and implementation support for finalization of

 international bidding documents in the final year, which led to a downgrading of efficiency from ‘High’ to

 ‘Substantial’. According to the ICR guidelines (2021), this indicates that efficiency was ‘as expected’ rather

 than being ‘above expectations’, on balance. Rather than the goals being completed on time and within

 budget, the goals were not achieved and without available budget spent. Both World Bank staff and

 counterparts seemed to rely on the hope that the project would be extended. This was not an ‘efficient’

 use of resources within the time frame, even though the project itself has been massively impactful and

 has strong prospects for continued success as it is further developed in the future.

                                      F. DESCRIPTIVE REPORT

 1. General Observations/Remarks of the Project on

 1.1. Background

 1.      Bangladesh has achieved a milestone in efficient public procurement management through

 introducing electronic Government Procurement (e-GP) system in 2011. After successful implementation

 of e-GP system through Public Procurement Reform Project II (PPRP II) in the four key target agencies

 (Roads and Highways Department, Local Government Engineering Department, Bangladesh Water

 Development Board and Bangladesh Rural Electrification Board), the Government planned to expand the

 e-GP to all public sector agencies as a priority basis. Hence, 28 Public Service Organizations have been

 selected for further implementation of e-GP system. The existing resources, technology and infrastructure

 of Bangladesh Public Procurement Authority (BPPA), former Central Procurement Technical Unit (CPTU),

 were not sufficient to support this mandate of the Government. With the view to expanding e-GP system

 and restructuring CPTU for further capacity enhancement Implementation Monitoring and Evaluation





                                                                                                     Page 63 of 74

The World Bank

Digitizing Implementation Monitoring and Public Procurement Project (P160758)







 Division (IMED) undertook the Digitization Implementation Monitoring and Public Procurement Project

 (DIMAPPP) funded by International Development Association (IDA) and Government of Bangladesh. The

 project has four components including restructuring CPTU and institutionalizing e-GP, enhancing

 digitization of public procurement, professionalizing procurement and citizens engagement, digitizing

 project implementation monitoring.



 1.2. Justification/Adequacy



 2.      In first phase, the e-GP system was fully functional up to the contract award step by the four key

 target agencies (RHD, LGED, BWDB, BREB), covering about 30% of the ADP allocation. The e-GP system is

 an end to end solution from procurement planning to payment and closing of contract. For taking

 advantage of the complete e-GP system, implementation of contract management module was

 mandatory. This project focused on implementing further steps of e-GP system. This Project has been

 carried out for further implementation of e-GP system in 28 Newly Selected Public Sector Organizations

 (NSPSOs) as well as increasing procurement efficiency through capacity building of procuring entities and

 restructuring of CPTU.



 1.3. Objectives

 targets are:

         •   to digitize project implementation monitoring;

         •   to engage Citizen Monitoring of 45 Selected Upazilas in contract implementation (baseline 4);

         •   to do online implementation monitoring 45 percent projects of SPSOs’ by IMED (baseline 0).

 6. Remarks & Recommendations of the Project Director

 42.     Successful and sustainable procurement reforms require ongoing high-level commitment and

 ownership of the key stakeholders, especially the implementing agencies and the tenderer community,

 with the government political commitment at the higher level. Such combined ownership, enhances the

 probability of sustainable reform. The actual implementation of crucial reform like e-GP is a bottom-up

 approach, mainly owned by the mid-level public officials of the agencies and relatively young tenderers





                                                                                                         Page 72 of 74

The World Bank

Digitizing Implementation Monitoring and Public Procurement Project (P160758)







 community, both with IT knowledge and skills. The project implementers, including BPPA (Former CPTU)

 and the 28 target SPSOs and other public agencies key officials leveraged the support of its mid-level

 enthusiastic officers and young community of tenderers and banks by widening the ownership circle. As

 a result, e-GP has been expanded rapidly within a short period of time.



 43.      To sustain the benefit of the project the government should enhance the implementation of e-GP

 with its continuity, necessary policy reform in order to ensure the sustainable procurement and ensure

 the best practice through capacity building of government officials, tenderers, bank officials on

 management & IT aspects of public procurement. Moreover, the engagement of civil society, private

 sector and the citizens at large in the monitoring of public procurement should be ensured to promote

 transparency, accountability & efficiency in public procurement. To make the BPPA more robust capacity

 development and infrastructure development is mandatory. Hence, it is recommended to initiate a new

 project to fulfill the above objectives.
