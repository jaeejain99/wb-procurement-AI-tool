I. PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES



 A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL



   Context



   1. The Ghana Economic Management Strengthening Project (P152171) (the Project) was an Investment Project

   Financing (IPF) in the amount of US$15 million (IDA-58960). In its original design, the Project aimed to strengthen

   the institutional capacity of the Government of Ghana (GoG) for revenue and expenditure management. The Project

   was approved on August 30, 2016 and became effective on November 1, 2016. The first restructuring took place in

   March 2020 to provide for a no cost extension of the Project closing date by 24 months from its original date March

   31, 2020 to March 31, 2022. In March 2022 an additional no cost extension of 3 months was provided to extend the

   closing date to June 30, 2022. The Project closed on June 30, 2022, fully disbursed (99 percent disbursement).



   2. At the time of appraisal in 2016, Ghana was amid a challenging period with slower growth, twin deficits,

   growing public debt, and higher inflation combined with sharp currency depreciation. The period followed Ghana’s

   stable economic growth since the early 2000s, pushed by favoring factors such as high gold and cocoa prices, new

   oil and gas discoveries, substantial capital inflows, and political stability; this remarkable growth successfully

   catapulted Ghana to its Lower Middle-Income Country (LMIC) status. The period of stable economic growth was

   accompanied by significant progress in key social indicators including poverty rates, school enrollment and maternal

   mortality. However, Ghana’s growth peaked in 2011 as the GoG’s fiscal balance deteriorated with the expansion of

   the wage bill following the implementation of the single spine salary structure in 2010, and sharp increases in energy

   subsidies and transfers. Consequently, Ghana’s economic growth fell from 14 percent in 2011 to 4 percent in 2014.



   3. The Project was approved to support the GoG’s efforts to address the macroeconomic challenges, and

   complemented the supports provided by development partners (DPs). The World Bank provided Macroeconomic

   Stability for Competitiveness Development Policy Operation (DPO) Series including measures to improve efficiency

   of public spending, governance reform of state-owned enterprises (SOEs), and debt management strategy among

   others. The International Monetary Fund (IMF), under its three-year Extended Credit Facility (ECF) of US$918 million,

   supported the GoG with focuses on the primary fiscal balance, wage bills, net international reserves, stock of arrears

   and others. The technical assistance provided under the Project was designed to complement and support the GoG

   during the implementation of these reform efforts.



   4. The Project was aligned to the Ghana Shared Growth and Development Agenda II (GSGDA II) 2014-2017, which

   sought to support growth and to restore macroeconomic stability. The Project supported the GoG in four of the

   seven medium-term priority policy areas anchoring GSGDA II including: a) ensuring and maintaining macroeconomic

   stability; b) enhancing competitiveness of Ghana’s private sector; c) enabling infrastructure and human settlements

   development; and d) enabling transparent, responsive, and accountable governance. It was also closely aligned to

   the Country Partnership Strategy (CPS) for Ghana (2013-2016) whose three strategic pillars to support the GoG were:

   1) improve economic institutions; 2) improve competitiveness and job creation; and 3) protect the poor and

   vulnerable.

Components



8. The Project consisted of five components. The details of each component and its subcomponents, as described

in the Project Appraisal Document (PAD), are summarized below.



   Component 1: Strengthening Ghana Revenue Authority’s (GRA) Business Intelligence (BI) System (US$4.0

   million equivalent)

   The overall objective of this component is to improve tax compliance, integrate income assessment regardless

   of source and location, simplify the processes, and align with international tax rules.



           Subcomponent 1.1: Improving the BI System (US$3.2 million)

           The objective of this subcomponent is to implement the Data Warehouse (DW), with BI, an information

           technology (IT) toolset for integration of data and analytical reporting. This tool set will extract and pool

           data from multiple underlying source systems and integrate the taxpayer information, both individuals

           and firms, into analytical reports. Although the first phase of integration falling within the scope of this

           project is on integrating tax revenue—Total Revenue Integrated Processing System (TRIPS) with customs

           information (Ghana Customs Management System (GCMS))—through the DW, the potential for further

           integration with third-party systems will be explored after work on information-sharing agreements with

           the owners of the third-party systems is completed. This work is already ongoing under a separate

           project of the GRA. The tool set will enable tax officials to capture potential taxpayers in the informal

           sector who deliberately avoid taxes, improve risk management mechanisms, and influence the design of

           tax policies.



           Subcomponent 1.2: Implementation of Common Reporting Standards (CRS) and Foreign Account Tax

           Compliance Act (FATCA) Software (US$0.8 million)

           This subcomponent aims to support the implementation of the software to enable the GRA to comply

           with CRS and FATCA requirements. The software will receive, collate, store, and transmit domestic tax

           information of European Union (EU) and US citizens, respectively, holding assets in Ghana, through

           automatic data exchange mechanisms. This will improve tax revenue mobilization and higher voluntary

           tax compliance by taxpayers and will also support GRA alignment with international tax regimes to

           increase its tax revenues from Ghanaians living abroad.



   Component 2: Strengthening Debt Management (US$1.2 million equivalent)

   The objective of this component is to strengthen the Ministry of Finance’s (MoF) capacity to manage public debt

   with a high degree of transparency as well as to improve treasury management and forecasting.



           Subcomponent 2.1: Improved Debt Management with Credible Annual Borrowing Plan (ABP) (US$0.6

           million)

           The first objective was to operationalize the MTDS through a detailed ABP. The ABP should consider the

           likely timing of Government cash flows through the year to determine the monthly net financing needs

           and forecast redemptions. These key inputs break down the annual financing needs into a quarterly

           profile and convert them into an auction calendar and thus transform the MTDS for three to five years

        into implementable annual operational plans. The second objective is to foster development of a robust

        DSA (Debt Sustainability Analysis), because fiscal slippages in the last few years have been predominantly

        responsible for rendering the debt management strategy ineffective. The third objective is to develop

        national borrowing procedures and guidelines to regulate the acquisition of loans/credit both

        concessional and non-concessional by Ministries, Departments, and Agencies (MDAs) and SOEs within

        the limit of the annual borrowing plan. The national borrowing guidelines would also highlight conditions

        under which guarantees can be issued by government and or loans that can be on-lent to SOEs taking

        into consideration the credit risk assessment of these institutions.



        Subcomponent 2.2: Strengthened Treasury Management and Forecasting (US$0.3 million)

        The objective of this subcomponent was to improve the existing treasury management and cash flow

        forecasting, and to link it with the debt auction calendar as well as to build the capacity in the Debt

        Management Division’s (DMD) front office to carry out the auctions. This effort to strengthen the existing

        treasury management and cash flow forecasting to make it more robust was a priority for two reasons.

        First, the Bank of Ghana (BoG) has a zero limit on gross credit to the Government, and second, arrears

        cannot be accumulated. Effective treasury management and cash flow forecasting have, therefore,

        become essential to the effective use of resources and development of the domestic debt market, with

        reliable and accurate cash forecasts allowing for closer adherence to an auction calendar. The current

        arrangements in Ghana fall short of international good practice given that the Treasury Single Account,

        cash flow forecasting, and cash investment practices are all underdeveloped and could benefit from

        more robust analytical tools.



        Subcomponent 2.3: Improved Debt Reporting and Transparency (US$0.2 million)

        The objective of this subcomponent was to build the necessary capacity in the DMD to fulfill reporting

        and transparency requirements mandated under the Public Financial Management (PFM) Law as well as

        to be able to periodically publish a debt statistical bulletin (and/or its equivalent). The MoF should

        regularly publish debt data on the central government, total nonfinancial public sector debt, and

        outstanding loan guarantees in a transparent and timely manner. These data should not be older than

        three months at the time of publication. These data should be published regularly through (a) a debt

        statistical bulletin containing debt data and basic risks indicators; (b) an annual report to the parliament

        under the PFM Law; and (c) the auction calendar and results in the domestic primary market. In addition,

        the MoF website should contain up-to-date and complete debt information. The achievement of these

        objectives was essential for ensuring transparency of the debt and loan portfolio and a vital information

        source for the rating agencies and the investors in government bonds.



        Subcomponent 2.4: Improved Operational Risk Management (US$0.1 million)

        The objective of this subcomponent was to support the MoF in its commitment to develop and

        implement a plan to strengthen the operational risk management framework. This will include

        operational risk management guidelines and a code of conduct along with risk monitoring and

        compliance functions that need to be developed.

 Component 3: Strengthening Capacity in Public Investment Management (PIM) (US$4.0 million equivalent)

 The overall objective was to develop and institutionalize a functional, transparent process for public investment

 programming that was applied across the Government. Such a process will help to improve the contribution of

 public investment to growth and economic development in Ghana. It seeks to achieve this by supporting the

 establishment and implementation of a functional PIM system within the GoG in accordance with the cabinet-

 approved policy. The proposed project, by its very nature (dealing with economic management at the level of

 the MoF), focuses mainly on the upstream decision-making process. The component also provides support to

 the National Development Planning Commission (NDPC) to enhance its capacity for long-term planning of

 infrastructure and other investment projects.



         Subcomponent 3.1: Development of a Comprehensive PIM Reform Strategy and Action Plan (US$0.25

         million)

         This subcomponent was to support a PIM capacity assessment including (a) a comprehensive review of

         existing institutional arrangements and PIM functions within the GoG; (b) a PIM-specific organizational

         review of the MoF; (c) capacity assessment of existing PIM resources; and (d) preparation of a

         comprehensive strategy and detailed action plan for PIM reform, including recommendations on the

         optimal staffing, skills, and competencies required to make the Public Investment Division (PID) and the

         PIM system function effectively in Ghana.



         Subcomponent 3.2: Improvement of the Regulatory and Institutional Setting for PIM and Creation of

         the Required PIM Instruments (US$1.25 million)

         The objective of this subcomponent was to operationalize the PIM policy and support the

         implementation of the reform strategy and action plan. Apart from the lack of technical expertise to

         perform the necessary analysis and appraisal of proposed investment projects, there was also a lack of

         institutional capacity to ensure that prescribed processes are followed and not circumvented. This was

         especially true about project prioritization, selection, and inclusion in the Public Investment Program

         (PIP). The draft PFM bill to be presented in parliament contains general provisions on PIM but leaves

         specific guidance for subsequent regulations to be developed. The proposed subcomponent will support

         the development of the regulations as well as strengthening of the institutional guidelines and processes

         (rules) for PIM, which will be publicly disclosed. The PID will be the single point of entry and filter for

         MDAs submitting projects for inclusion in the national budget, for consideration as possible PPPs, or for

         borrowing.



         Subcomponent 3.3: Implementing the Capacity-building Strategy for PIM in the PID, MDAs, and SOEs

         (US$1.50 million)

         The objective of this subcomponent was to develop, on a sustainable basis, the human capacity to

         operationalize the PIM system through training and technical assistance at all stages of the project cycle.

         Capacity needs to be quickly built up in the PID, given its central gatekeeper role, for carrying out

         preliminary reviews and appraisal of projects. It was equally important that MDAs and SOEs primarily

         tasked with project preparation have sound knowledge of modern assessment tools and techniques,

         procedures for analyzing projects, and assessing and managing risks. This will raise the quality of project

         preparation and reduce the time and resources required for assessment. It will also ensure that projects

         being funded by the GoG are well prepared. However, it was important that this be done in a sustainable

         manner, which ensures that capacity was not only built, entrenched, and retained in the GoG, but also

         can be replenished at minimum expense given normal staff movements and turnover. Hence, the project

         will support the GoG to build enduring capacity at suitable national institutions in Ghana where PIM-

         related training can be strengthened and offered on a continuous basis, independent of the World Bank

         project.



         Subcomponent 3.4: Strengthening of the NDPC (US$1.0 million)

         The objective of this subcomponent was to strengthen the NDPC to enable it to play its role as the apex

         of Ghana’s PIM system. The NDPC is established by law with the responsibility for medium - and long-

         term planning. The most recent such plan was the GSGDA II 2014–2017. The NDPC’s national plan is the

         foundation of the sectoral and district-level medium-term development plans (MTDPs) which set out all

         major capital investment priorities. Sector budgets are then expected to be based on and aligned with

         the sector- and district-level MTDPs as well as GSGDA II. Significantly, GSGDA II refers to a National

         Infrastructure Plan (NIP) as well as PIP, both of which are still to be completed. GSGDA II outlines policies

         and strategies for accelerated infrastructure development but defers the more detailed elaboration to

         NIP and PIP. Both are fundamental to the PIM function within Ghana in general, and the GoG in

         particular, as it was important to ensure that strategic planning, project preparation and appraisal, and

         budget execution are linked effectively. This will help the GoG to be able to provide the right

         infrastructure at the right time and at the right price. The PIM policy also assigns a role to NDPC for

         Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) of projects against GSGDA II, especially focusing on the longer-term

         economic and social impacts of major infrastructure projects.



 Component 4: Improving the Governance of SOEs (US$5.25 million equivalent)

 The objective was to improve SOE governance, the project will support reforms aimed at (a) consolidating the

 state’s ownership role through an equity study and preparatory work for setting up a single entity responsible

 for overseeing SOEs; (b) establishing the SOE single entity; and (c) piloting corporate governance improvements

 in five SOEs. A brief description of each of the subcomponents was provided below, including the activities to be

 supported by the proposed project.



         Subcomponent 4.1: Consolidating the State’s Ownership Role (US$1.5 million)

         The objective of this sub-component was to support the GoG in undertaking a comprehensive review of

         its portfolio of SOEs and carrying out the preparatory work for setting up a single entity, with a view to

         consolidating state ownership to improve SOE performance and ensure effective and efficient service

         delivery.



         Subcomponent 4.2: Establishing the Single Entity (US$1.25 million)

         The objective was to establish the single entity based on the preparatory work supported by Sub-

         component 4.1 to strengthen the Government’s capacity to oversee SOEs and improve corporate

         governance practices at the entity and SOE levels. The single entity will be responsible for the

         governance and financial oversight of its equity investments in SOEs. The main objective for establishing

         the single entity was to shift from the current highly decentralized state asset management model to a

         centralized state capital management model, where SOEs are both better governed and are financially

         independent and sustainable.

              Subcomponent 4.3: Piloting Corporate Governance Improvements in Selected SOEs (US$2.5 million)

              The objective of this subcomponent was to support corporate governance improvements in the five

              selected SOEs for which corporate governance assessments and action plans have been prepared. In

              particular, the project will support the implementation of three-year company-level corporate

              governance action plans approved by the cabinet; and capacity building of boards and senior

              management through training, peer-to-peer learning, and other means. This subcomponent will build

              the capacity of the boards and senior management and improve management protocols and internal

              corporate governance systems and procedures. To place this capacity building on a more sustainable

              footing, national training institutions such as the Ghana Institute of Management and Public

              Administration (GIMPA) and the University of Ghana Business School will be supported to develop the

              capacity to deliver such trainings on an ongoing basis. The project will also support citizen-led M&E and

              oversight of SOEs’ compliance with corporate governance standards.



      Component 5: Project Management (US$0.55 million equivalent)

      The key objective of this component is to provide resources, equivalent to approximately 3 percent of the overall

III. KEY FACTORS THAT AFFECTED IMPLEMENTATION AND OUTCOME



 A. KEY FACTORS DURING PREPARATION



    41. The Project was prepared in response to the government request, in line with the government priorities,

    and in close consultation with the government. The government had clear priorities under GSGDA II which served

    as the spine for discussions with relevant stakeholders in identifying the components and activities to be

    undertaken supported by the Project. The Project was directly aligned with the programmatic DPO series, and its

    design was also supported by existing plans, technical assessments and analytical works, including the GRA

    Modernization Plan, PIM assessment, Public Debt Management Reform Plan and SOE diagnostic study.



 B. KEY FACTORS DURING IMPLEMENTATION



    42. The COVID-19 pandemic significantly delayed project implementation. Travel restrictions, lockdowns and

    social distancing measures forced some activities to be conducted virtually, proving to be less effective than face-

    to-face activities. Most notably, IR Indicator 4.3 “CSOs participating in the annual stakeholder workshop on SOE

    governance” was not achievable during the height of the pandemic but was later achieved in 2022 after the

    government’s policy to restrict physical workshops was relaxed. Despite these challenges, the Project had achieved

    99 percent disbursement prior to the second extension.



    43. Procurement of a complex system requiring inter-departmental collaboration was a challenge. The

    responsibility for the management of the DW/BI within GRA was transferred to different department multiple time,

    which delayed the overall implementation. Furthermore, there was insufficient communication and collaboration

    between those responsible for detailing the specifications and the final beneficiaries of the system; this could have

    been avoided by assigning either a single department or a group consisting of key departments in charge or

    operating such systems to be responsible for the entire process from planning, procurement and implementation.







IV. BANK PERFORMANCE, COMPLIANCE ISSUES, AND RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME



 A. QUALITY OF MONITORING AND EVALUATION (M&E)



   M&E Design



   44. The M&E function under the Project was assumed by the M&E Unit under the Policy Coordination Monitoring

 D. RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME



    58. Risk to development outcome is assessed as Low. The results achieved are sustainable and capable of

        supporting subsequent interventions, minimizing institutional capacity risk. The government is committed to

        continuing to sustain the development outcomes and to further implement reforms for better service delivery.

        The departments and institutions of the Project – MoF, GRA, CAGD and SIGA – will continue to implement

        reform activities under the government’s new PFM Strategy (2022-2026). The PFM Strategy was approved by

        the government in May 2022, presenting the government’s reform strategy and priorities in PFM, including

        those overlapping with the Project – domestic revenue mobilization, public investment management, SOEs

        and audit. The World Bank Board of Directors approved, in June 2022, a new PforR operation PFM for Service

        Delivery Program which will support the implementation of the PFM Strategy. The challenge for the

        government going forward is to ensure the momentum is kept as the fiscal and debt vulnerabilities worsen

        (inflation rate of 40.4 percent year on year in October 2022) and an Extended Credit Facility of USD 3 billion

        from the IMF is imminent. The Bank is working closely with the IMF and expected to provide adequate support,

        in the form of a DPO, which may cover some of the key subcomponents of the Project including debt statistics,

        extrabudgetary funds among others. On the political aspect, the General Election, to be held in 2024, may also

        pose a challenge as it may add electoral pressure on government spending; however, Ghana has a track record

        of managing elections peacefully with relatively limited turbulence before and after the elections.







V. LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS



    59. Using readily available indicators, where possible, could reduce administrative costs and might be a more

        suitable option. One of the PDO indicators required revision due to difficulties in data collection by GRA and

        in measuring the target. The formula for the indicator was not clear and at times was a source of confusion;

        the administrative cost associated with checking the definition and collecting the relevant data could have

        been minimized by selecting indicators that had regularly been collected and used by GRA or MoF provided

        they serve as adequate proxies for the expected outcomes.



    60. The Project was designed to, and successfully did complement the programmatic DPO series. The

        programmatic DPO series had two primary pillars: a) strengthen institutions for more predictable fiscal

        outcomes, and b) enhance the productivity of public spending. The achievements under Projects proved that

        DPOs and IPFs, when designed effectively with well aligned pillars and components, complement each other

        well and produce successful results. This is especially true when dealing with macro level issues such as debt

        management which are dependent on policies and other external factors. It may also have helped the Project

        to implement a relatively large number of components and activities despite the modest amount of credit.



    61. Inter-departmental collaboration is key to ensuring delivery of outcomes. During a procurement process, the

        beneficiary entity can play a proactive role and provide support to the procuring entity to ensure timely

        delivery. The procurement of the DW/BI required more time than it had originally planned due to the changes









                                                                                                                Page 22

        The World Bank

        Economic Management Strengthening (P152171)







           to the departments within GRA with the responsibility for the management of the DW/BI, and in turn required

           additional inter-departmental collaboration to ensure the specifications of the systems were aligned with

           those required by the beneficiary entities. The Project could have benefitted from more ownership and

           proactive engagement from the final beneficiaries – including departments and relevant individuals – to foster

           better collaboration. Task teams may also consider internalizing this during project designing and make sure

           the procuring entity and the beneficiary entities are not disconnected.



       62. The chain of command can be more efficient with fewer levels. The Project Steering Committee (PSC), at

           appraisal, was set up as an overarching body and a means of ensuring efficient project implementation

           considering the number of different executing units and the complexity involved. However, the directors and

           heads of the relevant executing units and divisions, who had originally been named as members of PSC,

           continued to take part in key meetings and missions, and provided support. Many of these key interventions

           were not in the form of an official PSC and showed that the same results could be achieved not necessarily

           through formal committees.



       63. Overlapping institutional and departmental mandates hindered effective project implementation. NaMEIS

           was launched by NDPC whose roles and responsibilities overlaps with another public institutions, namely the

           Monitoring and Evaluation Secretariat. The seemingly overlapping inter-institutional roles and responsibilities

           combined with insufficient coordination with other institutions and departments may have resulted in

           suboptimal specifications of the system. Furthermore, there is another system owned and operated by NDPC

           with similar functions with NaMEIS. Streamlining of roles and responsibilities would allow project resources to

           be used efficiently and avoid duplications of outputs.



       64. The Project could have had more ambitious targets, especially after restructuring. While both the Bank team

           and the government were – understandably – cautious about the uncertainties surrounding the COVID-19

           pandemic and therefore focused primarily on achieving the original PDO rather than adding new activities or

           new target, the targets could have been more ambitious considering how satisfactorily the Project had been

           progressing at the time of restructuring. One approach that the Team may have opted for, while balancing the

           reluctance to be overly ambitious during time of uncertainties and willingness to deliver higher outcomes, is

           to not make changes to PDO indicators to ensure the achievements are fully recognized but add or edit other

           indicators in the results framework.

                                                   ANNEX 5. EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS







1. The efficiency is evaluated based on financial analysis of two components of the Project: enhanced mobilization of

domestic revenues and SOEs.4



2. Enhancement to mobilization of domestic revenues brought overwhelming financial returns. In 2021, tax revenue

as a percentage of GDP amounted to 13.4 percent, equivalent to GHC 57,433.38 million. In a without-project-scenario

with a baseline of 9 percent of GDP, the tax revenue is estimated at GHC 38,574.66 million. The difference between the

actual and the without-project-scenario is GHC 18,858.72 million, or USD 3,139.93 million. A conservative assumption

that GRA’s efforts contribute to 0.5 percent of the difference still estimates the financial returns from enhanced

mobilization of domestic revenues in 2021 alone, overweighs the Project cost.

                  ANNEX 6. BORROWER, CO-FINANCIER AND OTHER PARTNER/STAKEHOLDER COMMENTS



The draft ICR was shared with the Government, and comments were received on February 23, 2023. Comments were

primarily editorial and minor changes have been incorporated in the final ICR document, including those provided in

tracked changes.



The Borrower’s ICR was also shared to the World Bank as a supporting document. A summary is presented below:



                                              Executive Summary of the Borrower’s ICR



1.    The Government of the Republic of Ghana through the Ministry of Finance received US$15 million credit from the World Bank to

      implement the Ghana Economic Management Strengthening Technical Assistance Project (GEMS-TA). The project aimed at

      strengthening the Government of Ghana’s institutional capacity for revenue and expenditure management from November 2016 -

      June 2022. This was a complex and overly ambitious project, initially designed for a period of three years, and later extended by

      two (2) years, three (3) months.



2.    GEMS-TA Project had five components focused on strengthening Ghana Revenue Authority’s (GRA) Business Intelligence Systems;

      improving debt management capacity of the Ministry of Finance; strengthening public investment management capacity;

      improving the governance of State-Owned Enterprises and project management.



3.    In terms of achievements, the GEMS-TA project progressed relatively well in most of its components. All the components are rated

      satisfactory even though some broad activities were delayed due to the COVID-19 pandemic and initial delays. Some

      outcomes/outputs were achieved through effective collaboration and proactive actions of the component coordinators.



4.    Notable achievements of the GEMS-TA have been the improvement of debt management with credible annual borrowing plan

      (ABP), development of a Credit Risk Assessment Framework (CRAF), publication of the National Borrowing Guidelines (NBG),

      digitization of loan agreements and related documentations, as well the establishment of the State Interests and Governance

      Authority (SIGA)1, passage of PFM (PIM) Regulations 2020 (L.I.2411), institutionalization of the PIM capacity-building programme

      at the University of Ghana Business School (UGBS) and enhancement in delivery of annual public debt reports and other statutory

      reports. Prior to the implementation of GRA’s Enterprise Data Warehouse solution, a temporal Mini -Data Warehouse environment

      was implemented to streamline GRA’s revenue reporting process and provide data support for tax audits. Despite the performance

      of the project, there were delays in the implementation of some activities, which made it impossible for the Ministry to fully achieve

      the PDO by March 2022. This necessitated a three-month extension to June 2022



5.    The GEMS-TA Project Completion Report (PCR)is intended to evaluate the performance and results of the operation and to capture

      and disseminate experience from operational design and implementation, to improve the design and implementation of future

      projects. This report covers the period from November 2016 to June 2022 and provides a project completion report which includes

      the lessons leant and recommendations.



PDO

      taxpayer database and enhancing capacity for the management of public debt, public investments, and SOEs. The PDO indicators

           to GDP.

      b. PDO Indicator 2: Deviation of the share of short-term refixing in total debt at the end of the year from the level set out in the

           medium-term debt management strategy (MTDS). (Baseline: 2%, End-line target: 0.5%)

Relevance of PDO



8.   The development objectives of the GEMS-TA project have remained highly relevant to the strategic priorities outlined in key

     strategic policy documents of the government of Ghana throughout the implementation. The Project Development Objective “to

     strengthen the Government of Ghana’s institutional capacity for revenue and expenditure management” is consistent with the

     government’s medium-term development strategy for Ghana. The PDO advances the vision and medium-term policy objective as

     well as the key strategic anchors and specific goals, broad policy areas, strategies, programmes and initiatives being implemented

     under CPESDP; Medium-Term National Development Policy Framework, 2018-2021 (MTNDPF); and subsequent Budget Statement

     and Economic Policies of Government.



9.   The interventions of the project reflect and complement some key objectives, strategies and measures implemented within the

     context of the CPESDP and MTNDPF to ensuring improved fiscal performance and sustainability through: (a) strengthening of on-

     going revenue administration reforms; (b) improving public financial management and expenditure rationalization to enhance the

     efficiency of public spending; and (c) implementation of new debt-management strategies.



10. The project and its interventions directly address some of the key development issues identified under the MTNDPF including

    heavy public debt burden and high debt-service payments; expenditure overruns and accumulated arrears; weak expenditure

    management and budgetary controls, revenue under-performance; and weak revenue management capacity. Finally, there are

    several policy initiatives and measures outlined in the CPESDP, MTNDPF, and 2021 Budget which have been implemented with the

    project support or are currently being implemented under the project.



11. Rating: Based on the findings, the Relevance of the PDO is rated as HIGH

Efficiency



17. The project was fully mainstream into the institutional structure of the Ministry of Finance without the need for a separate project

    implementation unit. The project built on and fully utilized the existing capacity of MoF. To implement this Project MoF has

    previously and is currently implementing complex reforms supported by World Bank projects such as the GIFMIS, PFMR, and PPP

    projects. On the side of GRA, the Office of Modernization, which was established to drive the reform programmes within GRA, was

    also utilized. The NDPC also had demonstrated capacity to implement technical components of the project that relate directly to

    their mandates. The project was however overseen by a multi-agency project steering committee made up of director level officials

    of the MoF, and Head of Executing Divisions the project steering committee is chaired by a Deputy Minister.



18. There has been recent improvement in activity implementation; overall project expenditure has been consistent with the approved

    forecast disbursement. The project budgeted about US$15million for the implementation of planned activities up to 31st March,

    2022 of whichUS$11.84 million has been disbursed which is about 78%.



19. There was a cost-savings in respect of some of the activities. The savings made by the project following a shift in strategy from the

    use of external consultants to the placement of internal expertise and task force for certain activities positively contributed to the

    low expenditure. The use of mainstream staff was also a contributing factor to the low expenditure of the project.



20. Based on the observed performance of the project, efficiency is rated as substantial.

21. Gender. The project has contributed to gender mainstreaming. Women have participated in achieving the project development

    objectives and the project implementation team made conscious efforts to develop appropriate data collection forms to track the

    participation of women relative to men in all PIM-related events. The gender breakdown of trainees for females was 621. This was

    not the practice prior to project implementation. This would help to promote active women participation and capacity

    strengthening to facilitate gender equality.

Conclusions and Recommendations



25. Over the past six years, the GEMS-TA project has contributed to strengthening the Government of Ghana’s institutional capacity

    for revenue and expenditure management. Through its five components, the project has contributed to improving the

    government’s tax database, enhanced capacity for public debt management, public sector investment and state-owned

    enterprises.



26. The project has contributed to a large degree, the achievements of its PDO and Intermediate results as well as its overall impact

    on public revenue and expenditure management in Ghana.



27. The project experienced several delays in implementation of some components for several reasons including capacity challenges

    of component implementers, lack of comprehensive anticipation of the scope of activities beyond those outlined in the PAD;

    extensive and bureaucratic procurement processes of both the World Bank and PPA as well as an ambitious delivery period for the

    scope of activities being implemented especially for the IT related deliverables.



28. Nonetheless, as a mainstreamed project of MoF and implemented with other institutions, the project was able to achieve

    significant collaboration and coordination with both internal and external stakeholders to implement its activities. It reinforced the

    coordination role of the ministry and strengthened the ongoing partnerships between the ministry and relevant agencies of the

    state. The project contributed to the birth of the SIGA, which will go a long way in improving public sector governance and reforms

    as well as protecting state interests in SOEs. Again, the project strengthened the National Development Planning Commission

    (NDPC).



29. Future potential public sector reform and capacity building technical assistance like GEMS-TA could consider the following

    recommendations:

        a. A multi-institutional steering committee made up of the highest decision makers constituted by the project could meet

            frequently (semi-annually or annually) to provide strategic oversight and fast-track implementation challenges. This would

            foster accountability of the beneficiary institutions and provide a platform for addressing common implementation

            challenges where more than one institution is involved.

        b. The project had a strong capacity building component which was very helpful. It will be helpful to continue to involve

            institutions of higher learning in developing, and delivery of future training on the topics to enhance local capacity and

            sustainability. This implies that capacity building delivered by the bank could be developed and delivered in conjunction

            with resources persons from a selected universities in Ghana that will play host to future training and research.

  c.   It seemed almost every IT related delivery faced delays in implementation. Reasons for this could include the evolving

       scope of IT related services and infrastructure, appropriate scope, cost and time for delivery and the inability to

       forecast/predict the possible trends in IT developments/solutions. That is, at the time of design, a solution may be suitable,

       however, two to three years later, that solution may not be suitable or may become obsolete. To address this challenge,

       it is recommended that:

               i. IT related deliverables should have extensive consultation with internal IT departments and external IT experts

                  to consider both the institutional implementation capacity as well expertise from outside the organization on the

                  potential and emerging trends to the proposed solution. These external consultations could include the Ministry

                  of Communications and Digitalization, Data Protection Commission and National Information Technology

                  Agency.

              ii. IT deliverables should balance scope, cost and time viz-a-viz the project duration. This could imply that scope

                  could be defined in modular form to enable gradual development of the solution instead of an outright complete

                  development of the solution. The modular format will allow for ongoing development, testing and usage of the

                  solution while additional components are added based on user feedback and needs.

  d.   The project had a good number of procurement activities supported by the procurement team of the project. This is

       commendable. In addition, the components also made contributions to the procurement process through development

       of TORs for consulting and non-consulting services. It seemed capacity (knowledge, skills and time) were lacking within

       the components to draft appropriate TORs within the required timelines. Moreover, internal reviews of the TORs

       alongside processes at the Bank and PPA affected the timelines for deliverables. It is recommended that component leads,

       and team members be equally trained in aspects of WB procurement processes to improve capacity. Where possible

       short-term expertise in procurement could be utilized to speed up the procurement processes.

  e.   As indicated earlier, staff working on the project had additional responsibilities in their institutions. It is recommended

       that additional financial incentives be considered for such staff and more so for those who meet key deliverables of their

       components.
