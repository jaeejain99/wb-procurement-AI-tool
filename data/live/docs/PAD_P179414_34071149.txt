I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT

A. Country Context



1. Kenya’s economy has demonstrated resilience to shocks, but the challenges are vast. Kenya’s economy has

   achieved robust growth, averaging 5.0 percent per year from 2010-2019, in line with the average in lower-middle-

   income countries (LMICs).1 Kenya is recovering well following the impacts of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-

   19) pandemic, with real Gross Domestic Product (GDP) increasing by 7.5 percent in 2021 and by an estimated 5.2

   percent in 2022, following a 0.3 percent contraction in 2020.2 However, successive shocks from the global pandemic,

   regional drought, commodity price volatility, and tightening of credit markets have both exposed and exacerbated

   underlying vulnerabilities in the Kenyan economy.3



2. Sustained economic growth translated into improved living standards and a steady reduction in poverty; however

   a series of successive shocks has reversed this trend. Between 2005 and 2019, GDP per capita increased; living

   standards increased; and people were lifted out of poverty, primarily in rural areas. The share of the population

   below the international poverty line decreased from 43.9 percent in 2005 to 33.4 percent in 2019, but the poverty

   elasticity of growth, already trailing other LMICs, also declined from 0.43 (2005-15) to 0.27 (2015-19).4,5 In 2020,

   poverty increased to an estimated 35.7 percent due to the impacts of COVID-19, before going back to the pre-

   pandemic level of 33.4 percent in 2022.6 Despite poverty being lower than the Sub-Saharan Africa average, it remains

   well above the LMICs average. The Gini index fell between 2005 and 2015 and appears to have stagnated since then.



3. Kenya continues to be highly vulnerable to adverse impacts of climate change and natural hazards. As per the 2020

   Notre Dame Global Adaptation Index, Kenya is ranked 149 of 182 countries in terms of climate change vulnerability.7

   The country has experienced a distinct warming trend with an estimated average temperature increase of 0.21°C

   per decade since the 1960s.8 It is projected that mean annual temperature may increase by between 0.8°C and 1.5°C

   by the 2030s and up to 3°C by 2100.9 Increased temperatures in the future could lead to further increases in drought

   conditions and have a significant impact on water and food security. This is especially problematic in the arid and

   semi-arid lands (ASAL) which cover over 85 percent of Kenya. Droughts have negative economic impact (at 8 percent

   of GDP, on average every 5 years) that worsen poverty and vulnerability.10 The country also remains highly vulnerable

   to floods and crop diseases. The country’s vulnerability to climate variability and change is compounded by weak

   adaptive capacity, poor infrastructure, and limited institutions.

4. Poverty and vulnerability to climate change are closely intertwined in Kenya . Globally, the poorest are

   predominantly the most vulnerable to, and the most severely affected by, climate-related natural disasters.11 Kenya

   is no exception, with populations with lower levels of income residing in more climate hazard prone locations. 12 In

   2019, poverty rates in rural areas were more than double those in urban areas.13 Poverty is particularly acute in the

   ASAL counties of northern and eastern Kenya, where droughts and other climate-related disasters are common.

   These regions rely heavily on agriculture and pastoralism as the primary livelihoods, making them vulnerable to the

   impacts of climate variability on food security and income. Out of the 17.8 million Kenyans employed in 2019, some

   7.9 million (45 percent) are employed in agriculture, of which 5.2 million are engaged in subsistence agriculture,

   accounting for just under 30 percent of all employment.14,15 There is large heterogeneity across counties; for example,

   in some counties (Busia, Kisii, West Pokot) subsistence agricultural activities account for over two-thirds of all

   employment. In 2016, 25 percent of agricultural households reported experiencing a drought or flood, some form of

   crop failure, or livestock loss.16 There is regional variance, with some counties having over 80 percent of households

   reporting experiencing climate related shocks. The relative severity of the shocks is also much larger for poor

   households – the poorest quintile experienced losses that were almost 6.5 times greater than the richest quintile.17



5. Kenya has seen substantial inflows of refugees over the last few decades, with the country currently hosting

   588,724 refugees and asylum seekers.18 A majority of the 511,467 refugees and 77,257 asylum seekers reside in

   Garissa and Turkana counties within the Dadaab refugee complex (41 percent), and the Kakuma refugee camp and

   Kalobeyei settlement (43 percent). The remaining 16 percent reside in the country’s urban areas, mainly Nairobi.

   About 55 percent of Kenya’s refugee population originates from Somalia, 31 percent from South Sudan, and the

   remainder from other neighboring countries.19 The inflow of refugees into the ASAL counties of Garissa, Turkana, and

   Wajir due to their proximity to the refugee camps in Dadaab has significantly changed the population, employment

   environment, and social dynamics of these host counties, which are already among the poorest in Kenya.



B. Sectoral and Institutional Context



6. Kenya’s social protection system has developed significantly over the last decade, however, coverage remains low.

   Although 33.4 percent of the population lives below the international poverty line in 2019, coverage of social safety

   nets stands at only 8.7 percent.20 Further, among those employed, the quality of employment is not very high – with

   some 70 percent of those employed (some 12.5 million) working in lower-tier informal employment. A further 16

   percent work in upper-tier informal employment, with just 14 percent in formal employment.21 Currently only those

   in formal employment get some form of social insurance leaving a large missing middle in Kenya that has no social

 7. Improving employment outcomes and providing savings opportunities for youth and refugees in Kenya

    necessitates understanding and addressing constraints faced by individuals as well as firms/entrepreneurs. Better

    employment outcomes are realized when there are more jobs, better jobs,22 and more inclusive jobs for

    disadvantaged groups among youth, such as the vulnerable, women, refugees, and people with disabilities.

    Integrated solutions that address constraints in labor supply, labor demand as well as during intermediation – i.e., in

    helping workers find the best possible match and supporting firms in finding appropriately skilled workers – are

    important. The sectoral context identifies the constraints in each of these areas. In addition, it discusses the linking

    of saving opportunities with employment and short-term incentives, such as maternity insurance, helping Kenya

    move closer to a systematic approach to ‘protect and promote all’.23



         Labor Supply



 8. Kenya needs to increase labor force participation and decrease inactivity to reap the demographic dividend. Kenya

    is a young country with approximately 39 percent of its population of 47.6 million (in 2019) under the age of 15. 24 A

    further 4 percent is over 65, leaving 27.1 million individuals (57 percent) of working age. Out of this group, some 8.3

    million are inactive, with just 18.8 million (69 percent) in the labor force. The labor force participation rate (LFP) is

    particularly low for some groups – i.e., youth (15-24) at 38 percent; those with lower levels of education (at 61

    percent for those with completed primary education); and for people living in the North and Northeastern

    Development Initiative (NEDI) counties, including the refugee hosting counties, at 46 percent.25 Increasing the LFP,

    and decreasing inactivity, is important to make best use of the demographic transition that has begun. The largest

    age cohort in Kenya is between 13 and 17 years of age and roughly one million youth will be joining the labor force

    each year over the next decade.26



 9. Host communities have limited employment options and refugees face additional barriers to the formal labor

    market and to economic opportunities outside camps. Refugee barriers include policies restricting movement,

    administrative hurdles related to accessing identification documents, work permits, and business licenses. Other

    barriers to legal employment include low levels of education and skills among refugees and limited access to financial

    resources. Thus, many camp residents remain dependent on humanitarian aid. In Kakuma, for example, among

    refugees aged 18-35, 75 percent of men and 74 percent of women are inactive, while in Kalobeyei, 47 percent of

    young male refugees and 58 percent of young female refugees are inactive.27 Social and economic interactions

    between refugees and hosting communities have helped improve wellbeing and boost the local economies. While

    the host communities in Turkana have higher employment rates than refugees (62 percent vs 20 percent), the host

    communities in both Turkana and Garissa continue to face widespread poverty and limited employment options.

    Both counties are in the ASAL region and face recurrent droughts. Only 48 percent of children in Turkana attend

    primary school, compared to 82 percent nationally. At the secondary school level, the numbers drop even further

     seasonal jobs.31 Among camp-based refugees, 52 percent of youth (15-29) in Kakuma and Kalobeyei are not in

     employment, education, or training (NEET). The NEET are more likely to be in their 20s, to have no education and are

     not proficient in Kenya’s official languages. Many of the refugees employed in the camps work as ‘incentive staff’

     with humanitarian organizations supporting basic service delivery.32 Due to the regulatory barriers, many other

     refugees take low paying jobs, usually in the informal sector.33



 11. Improvements in productivity and earnings are also important to raise well-being in Kenya. Earnings are low for

     those in lower-tier informal employment – i.e., median earnings for individuals in lower-tier informal wage

     employment is some 5,000 Kenya Shillings (KES) (roughly US$37) per month, going up to KES 14,700 (US$108) for

     those in upper-tier informal wage employment, and further to KES 24,000 (US$177) for those in formal wage

     employment. There is a similar steep gradation among the self-employed across the job ladder. Although the quality

     of employment improved between 2016 and 2019, youth benefited less from the overall improvement and

     predominantly enter poor quality jobs. Kenya needs to create better jobs, with higher levels of productivity, and

     higher earnings, for the large incoming youth cohorts.34

          Labor Demand



 15. A majority of workers in Kenya work in micro-sized informal firms. There are about 140,000 firms in Kenya (of all

     sizes) which are registered formally at the national level, with the registrar of companies. Only 3 percent of such firms

     have more than 50 employees.44 In comparison, there are 7.4 million micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs),

     both formal and informal. Some MSMEs are licensed nationally, some licensed at county level, and many not

     registered at all (5.4 million). Over 91 percent of MSMEs are micro-sized informal firms; and medium firms make up

     just 0.7 percent of MSMEs. MSMEs employ a total of 14.8 million workers.45 Overall, a majority of employment in

     Kenya is in micro-sized firms.46



 16. MSMEs run solely by women are less profitable and less likely to survive than businesses run solely by men. Almost

     56 percent of MSMEs in Kenya are run by women, either solely or in partnership with other women. By contrast,

     around 36 percent of MSMEs are run by men or male-only partners, and a further 8.5 percent are run by male and

     female partners.47 An aggregation of enterprise survey and impact evaluation data collected from countries across

     the region, including Kenya, finds that businesses owned by women or having majority female ownership have 34

     percent lower profits, 38 percent lower sales, and add 38 percent less value compared to businesses owned by men.48

 17. A key constraint faced by MSMEs in Kenya is lack of access to start-up capital, among several others. Most informal

     sector firms rely on own funding to start up as well as continue to operate, with very low access to either banks or

     microfinance institutions. Young vulnerable entrepreneurs do not have savings to fall back on, which poses a barrier

     for them to start a business. Women face greater barriers. Between 2013 and 2016, microfinance institutions in

     Kenya denied loans to women-owned businesses at a 20 percent higher frequency than male-owned businesses,

     while savings and credit cooperative societies denied women-owned businesses at an 11 percent higher frequency.49

     Other key constraints include: (a) lack of soft skills and in particular socio-emotional, managerial, and entrepreneurial

     skills that are associated with entrepreneurial mindsets and help in successfully starting or increasing productivity of

     new businesses; (b) lack of relevant exposure and networks for starting and growing a business among youth and

     especially women; (c) lack of business support such as mentoring for young entrepreneurs and self-employed youth;

     and (d) limited information and capacity of youth to take advantage of entrepreneurship programs and market credit

     ecosystems.

20. Lastly, women in the informal sector lack income protection during and after childbirth. Paid maternity and

        Intermediation

21. There is limited access to intermediation services in Kenya. Outside of private intermediation services which cater

    mainly to university graduates and professionals, there is little by way of services that help match less educated and

    more vulnerable youth with appropriate jobs in Kenya.50 Even large formal firms in Kenya search for employees

    mostly through informal channels, such as trusted networks and contacts.51 Similarly, it is difficult for schools and

    training institutions to identify competencies sought by employers and for individuals and families to get a clear idea

    of which skills and occupations are most required in Kenya. A labor market observatory coupled with a public

    intermediation service which works with less educated and more vulnerable youth would help with improved

    placement of workers in good jobs.

        Existing Jobs Programs

22. The government currently implements jobs programs to tackle the constraints, but these programs face several

    challenges. These programs have limited effectiveness: expenditure on the jobs programs is low at only 0.1 percent

    of GDP (2016 – latest data); there is fragmentation across the large number of programs and implementing agencies;

    monitoring and evaluation of these programs is limited with little known about their impact; coverage tends to be

    regressive with little access among the poor and those in the informal sector; and the effectiveness of the

    intermediation programs is modest, lacking up-to-date information on labor demand.52

47. Subcomponent 3.1: Supporting savings through monetary incentives and nudges (US$6 million equivalent). This

    subcomponent will offer monetary incentives and nudges to the 190,000 Kenyan youth beneficiaries of Components

    1 and 2 to encourage them to save and build a habit of saving. Youth who start saving early in their careers and

    continue to do so can benefit from their savings growing over time and accruing interest. Women can benefit from

    the financial security that regulated savings offer as well as from access to these savings during periods such as

    pregnancy or after childbirth. The design of this subcomponent is informed by emerging international experience

    and behavioral science insights on features that informal sector workers desire, notably easy registration, flexibility

    to save, monetary incentives, and a benefit that can be realized in the short term.62

    • Auto-enrolment and auto-debit: All project beneficiaries will be registered in the Haba Haba scheme, and their

       individual accounts will be auto-debited with a flat benefit under this subcomponent, for the initial six months.63

       During this period, they will be able to access their account digitally, see the accumulated balance in real time,

       and be able to contribute funds.

    • Matching contributions to inculcate the savings habit: After six months, the auto-debit will be suspended, and

       individuals will receive a 100 percent matching, subject to a cap per month per beneficiary, for another six months,

       if they contribute to the scheme. Auto-debit and matching contributions have shown promise in improving uptake

       and persistency of savings, respectively.

D. Results Chain



54. The project aims to address barriers that vulnerable youth face in accessing better employment and earnings. The

    project’s theory of change is illustrated in Figure 2. Youth with lower levels of educational attainment lack the soft

    skills, basic skills such as reading, writing, digital skills, as well as more advanced technical skills needed to get good

    jobs or to set up thriving micro-enterprises. Women face further time-use barriers given care responsibilities and

    gender norms. People with disabilities and refugees face even greater barriers as outlined previously. The NYOTA

    project provides integrated interventions that address these constraints. Interventions that tackle both the lack of

    skills along various dimensions (socio-emotional, cognitive, technical, digital), on-the-job training, and job search

    support helps increase beneficiaries’ productivity and helps them find good jobs. Well-designed apprenticeship

    programs designed closely with private sector employers can help ease the transition from school to work.69 Similarly

    NYOTA offers multi-faceted support to starting businesses – including providing financing, business training,

    addressing behavioral facets, and connecting to networks. An ongoing impact evaluation of this intervention in the

    KYEOP project shows its effectiveness in leading to successful self-employment.70 For youth who are particularly

    hard-to-reach, the thriving social enterprise sector in Kenya offers solutions. Some examples include Kidogo, which

    trains women in quality childcare provision and helps them become "mamapreneurs”; and Solar Sister, working with

    women in rural areas helping these ‘solar entrepreneurs’ with providing last mile access/service to quality solar

    products. These interventions are expected to move beneficiaries from inactivity/unemployment/underemployment

    into better quality jobs/more productive enterprises in the short term.



55. The project further aims to facilitate youth savings. Vulnerable youth lack good schemes in which to save when they

    are able to and build a cushion which can be used to start a business or when facing adversities. They also often lack

    financial awareness to understand the importance of savings. The NYOTA project will address the key design,

    behavioral, and operational constraints that limit the participation of vulnerable youth in savings schemes.

    International experience suggests that the poor save, even in long-term schemes, if these schemes are easy,

    convenient, flexible, trusted, incentivized, and provide tangible short-term benefits.71 In the short term, this will lead

    to improved savings habits among youth and greater financial awareness.

E. Rationale for World Bank Involvement and Role of Partners



58. There is a strong rationale for public sector financing to address the severe challenges facing youth in the Kenya

    labor market and to overcome the barriers to savings for those outside of the formal sector. Kenya’s challenges in

    the labor market are notably severe for youth, posing risks to economic growth and social stability, especially given

    the demographic youth bulge. The government has made the generation of more and better jobs central to its Vision

    2030 and Kenya Kwanza manifesto, although Kenya’s precarious fiscal situation present challenges to channeling

    sufficient public financing toward jobs programs. World Bank financing can help supplement public spending and be

    catalytic in terms of unlocking youth employment, thereby contributing to inclusive economic growth. This will, in

    turn, help improve Kenya’s fiscal situation, and thereby the sustainability of Kenya’s future expenditures on youth

    employment and active labor market programs (ALMPs). That said, linkages to the private sector will play an

    important role in achieving the PDO and public-private partnerships noted in Component 1 will be critical in

    complementing public spending. In terms of social insurance and savings, schemes exist for those in formal private

    is required to improve the operation, effectiveness, and take-up of these schemes.

F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design



60. The NYOTA project design is based on the important lessons learned during the implementation of its predecessor,

    KYEOP. In a fragmented ALMP landscape, KYEOP demonstrated the value of implementing a comprehensive

    employment program, becoming Kenya’s first employment program to fully integrate supply and demand side

    interventions along with intermediation services. KYEOP interventions were also piloted on refugees and host

    community members through a Feasibility Study on Interventions to Foster Employment of Refugees and Host

    Communities (P173814) and were found to be relevant to this segment of youth given the right policy and regulatory

    environment on employment. The pilots on KYEOP interventions provide critical inputs and lessons learned for the

    adaptation and scale-up of employment interventions for young refugees and host community youth in Kenya. One

    of the key lessons was that entrepreneurship training that is accompanied by access to finance and technical support

    is more likely to be impactful, as reflected by the complementary nature of Components 1 and 2. Finally for the

    project to attain 5 percent target of youth beneficiaries living with disabilities as compared to 3 percent of KYEOP,

    more deliberate outreach and partnership with the National council of people with disabilities at county level.



61. NYOTA will refine and strengthen the comprehensive approach by incorporating several lessons learned. Among

    them (and organized by components):

   • Component 1: the critical importance of involving private sector employers in both the design and delivery of skills

       development programs.

   • Component 2: (a) the need to integrate an intensive mentoring engagement for youth throughout their

       entrepreneurial journey; and (b) integrate a results-based financing approach (“pay for success”) as the basis for

       government engagement with social enterprises, which have been found to be critical for reaching particularly

       vulnerable youth.

   • Component 4: (a) engage directly with county governments as a critical partner for improved implementation and

       as the basis for scaling up the achievements of KYEOP; and (b) address the monitoring and evaluation deficit

       identified during KYEOP so that there is evidence of the impact of these interventions.

       • ALMPs are effective in improving labor market outcomes of youth, demonstrating statistically significant positive

       • Youth-focused ALMPs are more effective in low- and middle-income countries than in high-income countries on

    trusted savings; monetary incentives to encourage savings; flexibility in design; interoperability with other public and

    save for longer durations. The planned rigorous impact evaluation of the NYOTA subcomponent 3.1 interventions

    C. Sustainability



71. Achieving sustainability is central to the design of NYOTA project. The project aims to strengthen youth

    employment, entrepreneurship, and savings outcomes in Kenya in a way that builds a sustainable system that

    continues to provide solutions long after the project closes. The capacity and systems strengthening investments

    under Component 4 will improve national and county government capacity to implement youth employment

    programs. Several outcomes from Components 1 – 3 are anticipated to be sustainable beyond the project’s end,

    including: (i) the long-term benefits of the skills training for the targeted vulnerable youth throughout their lifetime;

    (ii) the enhancement of the youth employment and entrepreneurship training ecosystem, including, for example,

    through the training and certification of new MCs who will provide more opportunities for mentorship over the long

    term; (iii) enhanced collaboration between the public and private sectors around skills development and on-the-job

    training as the basis for a longer-term, enhanced public-private partnership on youth employment; (iv) the full

    operationalization of the KLMIS and the digital online jobs matching platform which are intended to provide lasting,

    robust intermediation services after the project closes; (v) strengthened and more effective engagement between

    social enterprises and the government, providing a results based financing model that can be scaled beyond the life

    of the project; (vi) enhancements to the NSSF’s business processes and systems that will improve the experience of

    Haba Haba users, incentivizing participation and continued saving; and (vii) the generation of much needed catalytic

    evidence on what works and what does not work in terms of incentivizing savings among youth; among others.

       Citizen engagement



86. Citizen engagement will be mainstreamed throughout project implementation. At the design stage, the project has

    engaged citizens through consultations at the national and regional levels. At the national level, various KYEOP

    beneficiaries, Civil Society Organizations, Nongovernmental Organizations, Trade Unions, and Private Sector

    representatives were engaged by the MYAAS and the World Bank to discuss key challenges to be addressed by the

    project, as well as its expected results, activities, and implementation modalities. The project also applied lessons

    from the engagement with refugees and host communities during the implementation of the Feasibility Study on

    Interventions to Foster Employment of Refugees and Host Communities. At the implementation stage, the project

    will leverage both the government’s existing citizen engagement mechanisms, as well as new and dedicated, project-

    specific citizen engagement mechanisms that will be extended to include refugees and host communities. For

    example, the GRM that was successfully implemented under the ongoing KYEOP project will be extended and tailored

    to the NYOTA project in the first year of the project implementation. The project will also work with subnational

    governments to integrate the project in the County Integrated Development Plan, a process that requires citizens

    engagement in its development.



87. The project will continually assess the progress, efficacy and targeting of NYOTA’s key interventions through key

    stakeholder surveys. Recognizing that citizens engagement is an iterative process, the project will continue to engage

    a multitude of stakeholders to develop and inform the PIM ensuring that the project finds the right balance to serve

    the target audiences’ needs and will also regularly monitor the “level of satisfaction of beneficiaries” through online

    and phone surveys. The exact questionnaire will be developed when the project becomes effective. The surveys will

    target the relevant beneficiary groups and ensure adequate representation among female respondents, private

    sector employers, respondents with disabilities, and other vulnerable groups, and will ensure accessibility for all

    target groups. Findings from the surveys will be used on an annual basis as ‘feedback loops’ to inform the validity of

    the interventions and determine any course corrections, as appropriate. Midterm review assessments will leverage

    lessons generated by the surveys. NYOTA project also intends to close the loop by informing citizens about how the

    feedback has been integrated. Relevant local communities, including the private sector, people with disabilities, and

    other vulnerable groups will be sensitized and provided with information about the GRM procedures and

    complementary feedback tools. Complementary capacity building interventions will be designed, as appropriate, to

    ensure that the utilization rate by the public of these mechanisms is satisfactory. The project will also use the

    socioeconomic panel survey in refugee hosting counties under the World Bank’s Kenya Analytical Program for Forced-

    Displacement (P176078) to monitor vulnerabilities and economic issues impacting these refugee and host community

    populations.



               Climate Co-Benefits

88. The project aims to reduce the vulnerability of the poor and vulnerable to climate change-induced shocks and build

    their resilience. To achieve this, the project is fully aligned with the actions of the Kenya NAP by integrating both

    mitigation and adaptation measures into the project design and implementation. The goal of transitioning to a green,

    low carbon, climate resilient, equitable, and sustainable society also implies the need for new types of skills that

    would enable youth entering the labor market supported by NYOTA project. The green economy includes various

    sectors, such as renewable energy, climate-smart agriculture, sustainable forestry, and nature-based tourism (NBT).

    By promoting skills and jobs in these areas, Kenya can reduce its reliance on unsustainable practices and fossil fuels

    while creating new economic opportunities. The project aims to facilitate farmers in adopting sustainable agricultural



                                                                                                                Page 40 of 68

        The World Bank

        National Youth Opportunities Towards Advancement Project (P179414)







    practices, such as agroforestry and soil conservation techniques, which can improve crop yields and soil health. It

    also aims to create a market for small-scale farmers by promoting organic and local food products. Furthermore, NBT

    provides diverse employment opportunities, ranging from naturalist guides to hospitality management. Kenya has

    already taken steps to develop these skills through the Kenya Climate Innovation Center, which offers support and

    training for entrepreneurs in the green sector. The project will leverage over 160 conservancies spread across 28 out

    of the 47 counties of Kenya to achieve the twin objectives of wildlife conservation and building resilient

    communities.86 NBT contributes to resilience among pastoral communities by generating formal and informal

    employment opportunities in the tourism sector, while women can engage in self-employment activities such as

    beadwork and operating cultural manyattas. Youth employment will be encouraged through nature-based

    enterprises such as honey production, butterfly rearing, and community boardwalks for tourism. These nature-based

    enterprises and social enterprises will also receive training in sustainable business management and marketing skills.

    By investing in these skills and new economic opportunities, the project aims to help Kenya reduce its environmental

    footprint and build resilience to climate change. Promoting non-traditional fisheries in the blue economy is an

    important strategy to diversify livelihoods and build resilience in the face of climate change. Kenya's coastal region is

    home to a diverse range of marine species, but many of these are under threat due to overfishing, habitat

    destruction, and climate change impacts such as ocean acidification and rising sea temperatures. Developing skills in

    sustainable fisheries and other marine-based economic activities, including eco-tourism, culture heritage, and

    maritime transport and logistics, is crucial.

89. Strengthening and expanding social protection and social insurance mechanisms is an essential strategy to mitigate

    the impacts of climate disasters. Despite Kenya's progress in social assistance programs to address poverty, many

    poor and near-poor individuals working in the informal sector still lack access to such programs. The project will

    provide savings opportunities through the Haba Haba scheme, which not only builds capacity to cope with climate

    shocks but also contributes to the long-term viability of the NSSF. The NSSF system will be interoperable with the

    Enhanced Single Registry (ESR) to enhance targeting of the poor and vulnerable in response to climate disasters.

    Moreover, the project will extend novel saving schemes to informal sector workers, utilizing Kenya's expansive digital

    payments infrastructure and informal sector pension schemes, to enable those individuals, especially those in 12

    NEDI counties (including the two refugee hosting counties), to manage the impacts of climate shocks in the future.

    The project will be implemented in 12 NEDI counties where climate change has affected livelihoods.

90. In summary, the project will take proactive steps to address the significant challenges posed by climate change in

    Kenya, including reducing poverty, diversifying livelihoods, and building economic resilience. The project's

    mitigation and adaptation actions will focus on diversifying livelihoods, building resilience among beneficiaries

    through soft skills training, on-the-job training, financing entrepreneurship, promoting placement in green and low-

    carbon jobs, and promoting green and sustainable blue economies. The project will also develop an interoperable

    system to target climate-vulnerable beneficiaries and incubate savings initiatives to expand coverage to cope with

    frequent climate-related disasters. Strengthening capacity at national and local levels to improve social and economic

    inclusion services and disaster response, raising awareness about climate change mitigation and adaptation through

    communication, training, and dissemination. Additionally, the project will adopt energy-efficient

    appliances/equipment in social protection IT infrastructure which will reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. By

    implementing these measures, Kenya can build a more sustainable and resilient future for its people and the

    environment. The detailed activities by component are summarized below in Table 2.

    VI.     KEY RISKS





    92. The overall project risk to achieving the PDO is Moderate. The design of the project builds directly on the proven

        experience of the previous World Bank-financed project, KYEOP, which has been successful in improving labor market

        outcomes for vulnerable youth. However, the political and governance, macroeconomic, institutional capacity for

        implementation and sustainability, fiduciary, and refugee protection risks remain significant. These risks and agreed

        mitigation methods are outlined in the subsequent paragraphs.

    93. Macroeconomic risk is rated Substantial. There is a risk that the macroeconomic situation may affect national budget

        ceilings that may in turn compromise implementing agencies access to adequate budget ceilings to allow smooth

        project implementation. Mitigating this risk will entail close collaboration and monitoring with the national

        government to allow prioritization of the budget ceilings to go towards youth employment initiatives under the

        project to allow for timely project implementation.

    94. Political and governance risk is rated Substantial. The devolution and evolving institutional frameworks between

        national and local government may pose an ongoing challenge in implementation, this includes possible political/elite

        capture given that youth employment is a key priority and deliverable for the political class. To address this risk,

        building public ownership of the project and its objectives through a comprehensive public communication strategy

        will be undertaken throughout the project cycle.

    95. Institutional and capacity for implementation and sustainability risks are assessed as Substantial. The risk of

        coordination failure is substantial with many agencies involved, including the coordination with county governments.

        The project is a significant scale-up in activities versus KYEOP which will test capacity of implementing agencies. To

        mitigate this risk, the use the apex coordination bodies under the project, i.e., the PSC and PITC will be important to

        help identify and address any challenges in coordination.

    96. Fiduciary risk is assessed as Substantial. Based on the challenges and weaknesses identified during financial

        management and procurement assessments, the combined overall fiduciary risk is assessed as Substantial. The

        proposed flow of funds requirements given the implementation arrangements of various agencies and limited

        procurement capacity poses a substantial risk to the project. To manage these risks, financial management and

        procurement assessments have been undertaken for all agencies that will utilize project funds, and action plans to

        address the weaknesses identified have been developed based on these findings.

    97. “Other” risks, defined here as refugee protection, are rated Substantial. While offering a range of benefits to

        refugee protection in Kenya, the Refugees Act, 2021 contains exclusion clauses and expulsion modalities that are not

        fully compliant with the relevant provisions of the 1951 Refugee Convention. There is a range of refugee protection

        risks based on their specific vulnerabilities, primarily a lack of simplified access to labor markets and social cohesion

        challenges with host communities that could be exacerbated by perceptions of competition over jobs. These risks

        will be managed by close engagement between the DRS and County Governments; policy dialogue with Government;

        and joint engagement with relevant agencies such as UNHCR and other stakeholders through the Refugee Donor

        Group; the Technical Working Group supporting the development of the Marshall Plan; and the development process

        for the Refugee Regulations. In addition, the World Bank, in collaboration with UNHCR, will complete periodic reviews

        of Kenya’s refugee policy and institutional environment under its Refugee Policy Review Framework. Maintaining

        adequate refugee protection framework in Kenya is an effectiveness condition. Prior to disbursements from IDA

        WHR, the recipient will: (i) submit new Refugee Regulations to Parliament; and (ii) prepare/update and adopt the

        SEP to cover refugee hosting counties.

                   ANNEX 1: Implementation Arrangements and Implementation Support Plan



                                               COUNTRY: Kenya

                           National Youth Opportunities Towards Advancement Project



       Implementation Support Plan



1. To support project implementation, the World Bank will bring on board experts with global and Kenya-specific

   experience. Component 1 will benefit from lessons on design and delivery of skills programs in a range of settings.

   Component 2 brings on board experts on economic inclusion with experience across various African countries. On

   supporting savings under Component 3, the World Bank will ensure complementarity with the technical assistance

   being provided to the NSSF on social insurance for individuals in the informal sector. Improvements to payment

   systems will benefit from the World Bank’s global work on designing digital government-to-person payment systems.

   Lastly, Component 4 will draw on the local expertise and strong knowledge of the systems and monitoring and

   evaluation landscape in Kenya built over years of cooperation. Support will also be drawn from development partners

   and other stakeholders supporting youth employment initiatives including savings and child-care arrangements.



       Project institutional and implementation arrangements



2. The MYAAS will be responsible for the overall implementation and supervision of the project and the components

   implemented as follows: Figure 3 outlines the implementation of activities across the four components; the agencies

   responsible for implementation are outlined below.



   •  Component 1. The MYAAS will lead in the beneficiary youth intake and implementation of this component. In

      addition, MYAAS will engage the private sector employer organizations who will participate in the project through

      results-based framework contracting to provide work-based training. MYAAS will also be responsible for

      providing soft/socio-emotional skills training, childcare arrangements for participating female beneficiaries. NITA

      will be responsible for quality of training, recognition of prior learning, trade testing and certification of youth

      undergoing and completing the NYOTA program, revision and development of Occupational Testing Standards

      as well as support pedagogical upskilling of Master Craftsmen. Under this project, SDL&SD is responsible for the

      KLMIS, and NEA will provide support to Intermediation.

   • Component 2. The MSEA will implement this component and will oversee the disbursement of grants, linking

     youth to credit facilities and monitoring of youth businesses. MSEA will also work with Social Enterprises to

     provide employment to youth through a results-based financing agreement.

    • Component 3. The NSSF will implement this component and will coordinate with MYAAS and MSEA to ensure

      that beneficiaries of Components 1 and 2 are automatically enrolled into the Haba Haba scheme. An NSSF branch

      officer will work closely with county governments to answer any questions, relay feedback, and troubleshoot.

    • Component 4. The MYAAS will implement this component and will oversee the engagement with the county

      governments, M&E systems, GRM systems, and will be responsible for overall project management and

      coordination.

 Financial Management Arrangements:

3. The project budgeting will be done in accordance with existing government procedures . The budget will be based

    on the AWPB developed in consultation with all stakeholders and consolidated by MYAAS. The project planning and

    budgetary process will be implemented in accordance with the standard government fiscal year which begins on July

    1 of each financial year as provided for by the PFM Act 2012 and the Government Financial Regulations and

    Procedures. This will form the basis for defining the project activities and ensuring that sufficient funds are allocated

    to achieve the agreed results. The AWPB will be approved by the PITC and subject to the World Bank’s no-objection.

    There are challenges of inadequate provisions in printed estimates which limit implementation of activities planned

    in AWPB. Increased follow-up and close collaboration between Ministry’s Chief Finance Officer, World Bank, and

    National Treasury will help resolve these challenges.



4. All implementing and technical agencies will ensure there are designated finance and accounts staff who will be

   coordinating the processing of financial transactions for the project including required financial reporting . The

   designation of Project Accountants deployed will be based on ToRs agreed with the World Bank similar to the KYEOP

   ToRs. The current KYEOP designated qualified Accountants and Internal Auditors are expected to take up the same

   roles in the NYOTA subject to World Bank approval to ensure any staff who did not perform as expected is not

   transitioned. All staff will be sensitized on World Bank financial management requirements at the start of the project.

   There exists a Finance Management manual for KYEOP which will be enhanced/contextualized and adopted for the

   NYOTA project. Through KYEOP, the implementing entities developed a Management Information System which will

    be used for project financial reporting in addition to the use of Integrated Financial Management Information System

    (IFMIS) for processing payments systems. The financial module under KYEOP was not used and the upgrade will

    enable use of the module under the NYOTA project. The GoK is also enhancing the IFMIS system for project financial

    reporting, which once finalized will be adopted for the project.



5. The project expenditure initiation, authorization, and payments will be in line with PFM Act 2012 and PFM

   regulations (2015) of Kenya as elaborated and customised in the FM manual. Project work plans will be integrated

   into the internal audit work plans. Internal Audit reports covering project activities will be shared with the World

   Bank on a semi-annual basis (i.e., by June 30 and December 31). Regular internal audit, implementation support,

   monitoring and reporting will be undertaken. It is a requirement that the internal audit units at all implementing and

   technical agencies will sample representative NYOTA transactions to provide assurance on project implementation.

   The budget for internal audit work is included in the project funds under operating cost (Financing Agreement

   definition of reasonable incremental audit cost) and will have a separate line in the Annual Work Plan distinct from

   M&E.



6. The project design involves periodical payments to training providers and participating youths . As noted above,

   through KYEOP, the respective implementing agencies have developed customized MIS that will be used to process

   these periodical payrolls. Under KYEOP, Ernst & Young LLP, an international consulting firm was contracted by MYAAS

   to provide consultancy services for in-depth Financial Management and MIS Review whose recommendations have

   substantially been implemented and will continue to be adhered.



7. Audit arrangements: The audit committees are independent of the Ministries and are appointed in accordance with

   the PFM Act 2012. The implementing agencies finance staff will also regularly carry out financial monitoring reviews

   at the technical agencies which will serve to ensure expenditures are made and supported as expected and further

   provide capacity building on any areas of weakness that may be noted. While there exist audit committees at all

   entities earmarked to implement the project, under KYEOP there was also weak oversight on the project by the audit

   committees. Under NYOTA, the audit committees will be sensitized, through the Head of Internal Audit, to include in

   their work plan the follow-up of project related issues. A risk management policy framework will also be prepared

   with the help of the Internal Auditor General, and training offered to improve on risk management.



 Disbursements and funds flow

8. The disbursements from IDA will be based on Statement of Expenditures where an initial advance will be disbursed

    based on initial cash flow requirement for at least three months but within the agreed ceiling for the Designated

    account. Subsequent disbursement will be based on utilization and documentation of expenditures. The banking

    arrangements for purposes of funds flow will consist of: (i) three Designated accounts denominated in US dollars or

    Euros as agreed with the National Treasury and opened at the Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) for each of the

    implementing agencies (MYAAS, MSEA, and SDL&SD) and managed by the National Treasury, and (ii) a Project

    Account in Kenyan shillings to be opened by the National Treasury at the CBK or financial institution acceptable to

    IDA and managed by the respective implementing agency, from which the project’s payments will be made. All the

    technical agencies, i.e., NITA, NSSF and NEA will have project’s dedicated bank account at the CBK or financial

    institution acceptable to IDA for receiving project’s funds from the respective implementing agency. There will be

    continuous engagement with the implementing agencies and the National Treasury’s Resource Mobilization

    Department to unlock any challenges impacting on the fund flows. The flow of funds is illustrated in Figure 4.

9. The PMU and implementing agencies Project Accountants will be responsible for the preparation of financial

   reports for the project. The dedicated Project Accountants at PMU and implementing agencies will be provided with

   capacity building training at the commencement of the project which will include refresher financial reporting

   requirements among other financial management procedures. The PMU finance team will regularly review financial

   reports by technical agencies under it and identify any further tailored support that may be provided. PMU expects

   the required financial reports to be submitted (to PMU) by 15th of the following month after the period ends or as

   may be directed and would further submit quarterly interim financial reports to the World Bank within 45 days after

   the end of the quarter. There will be three IFRs prepared against each DA by MYAAS, MSEA, and SDL&SD. The SDL&SD

   will consolidate its IFR with the NITA, NSSF, and NEA reports since they share one DA. NITA, NSSF, and NEA will each

   prepare and submit their IFR to SDL&SD one day after the end of the quarter indicating the respective amounts

   received into the DA against their respective expenditures under the project for consolidation. The format of the IFR

   will be agreed between the World Bank and the Implementing Entities. Simple formats for the technical to

   implementing entities will be included in the FM Manual. Annual financial statements will be prepared and submitted

   to the OAG within three months of the end of the Financial Year for audit. The audited financial statements and

   Management Letter will be submitted to the World Bank by 31 December, i.e., six months after the end of the

   Financial Year.



10. On annual basis, the financial statements for the project will be audited by the OAG which is the Supreme Audit

    Institution of Kenya as mandated by the constitution. The audited financial statements will be submitted to the

    World Bank within six months after the financial year end in accordance with the Disbursement and Financial

    Information Letter and World Bank’s FM guidelines. Under KYEOP, some entities, for instance the MYAAS, have had

    long outstanding audit issues yet to be resolved. Under NYOTA, the implementing agencies will be required to ensure

    any upcoming audit issues are presented to PAC on an annual basis.

    authorities, state corporations, education institutions, and other GoK institutions. The PPADA sets out the rules and

    procedures of public procurement and provides a mechanism for enforcement of the law. Under the PPADA, the

    Public Procurement Regulatory Authority (PPRA) has been established in addition to the Public Procurement

    Directorate in the National Treasury. The PPRA has oversight and regulatory functions, including undertaking

    procurement reviews and audits. There is a Public Procurement Complaints Review and Appeals Board (Appeal

    Board) under the secretariat of the PPRA that deals with complaints received from bidders or consulting firms.



13. Project Procurement Strategy for Development (PPSD). The recipient has prepared a PPSD that will address how

    procurement activities will ensure the best value for money under a risk-based approach. The PPSD covers the

    identification of specific project needs and summary of procurement contracts, assessment of project operational

    context and implementing agency procurement capacity, market research and analysis, risk management,

    procurement arrangements, and contract management. The PPSD forms the basis on which the Procurement Plan

    (PP) and has set out the selection methods to be followed by the recipient in the procurement of goods and non-

    consulting and consulting services financed by the World Bank. The PP has been prepared for the first 18 months of

    the program and will be updated at least annually or as required to reflect project implementation needs and

    improvements in institutional capacity.



14. Systematic Tracking of Exchanges in Procurement (STEP). The project will use STEP. This is a planning and tracking

    tool for monitoring delays, establishing benchmarks, and measuring procurement performance; and detecting and

    flagging any debarred firms when bidder information is uploaded in the system. Key procurements will comprise of

    consulting services: firm and Individual consultants, assorted IT, and general goods including motor vehicles.
