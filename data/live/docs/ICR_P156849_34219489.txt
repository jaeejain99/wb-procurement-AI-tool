I. PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES







 A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL



   Context

   1. Country background. Viet Nam had made a strong recovery since the 2011 economic downturn, with a gross

   domestic product (GDP) annual growth rate of 6.8 percent in 2015. GDP was projected to grow by 6 percent in 2016.

   The Government of Viet Nam’s (GoV) Socio-Economic Development Plan (SEDP) 2016-2020 had raised issues of “slow

   improvement in education and training, science and technology, culture, social issues, health care and job creation”.

   Another concern highlighted in the Viet Nam 2035 Report1 was the downward trend of labor-productivity growth.

   As the labor force was shifting to more productive segments of the economy,2 skills requirements were rising. The

   rapidly aging population3 was beginning to have an impact on the labor force—the source of sustainable growth—

   necessitating new types of skills and different approaches to education. This all reinforced the need to create

   technology-based jobs.



   2. Sector/Institutional Background. Viet Nam had made significant achievements in expanding enrollment and

   improving quality of general education. It had achieved universal preschool education for five-year-old children and

   for primary education and was approaching universal lower secondary education. The number of students in higher

   education (HE) grew from 124,484 in 1991 to over 2.3 million students in 2015. Enrollment over this period doubled

   roughly every five years. Viet Nam had also attained higher levels of student learning achievement. The country

   ranked among the top ten countries participating in the 2015 Program for International Student Assessment on the

   science score.



   3. Despite the impressive gains in access, HE failed to meet the demands for high-quality degree programs to

   provide the population with the skills and competencies needed for producing greater value.4 Nearly 50 percent of

   employees interviewed in a survey claimed that the education system did not meet the skill needs of their

   workplace.5 Research institutes and universities produced too little research that was mostly not of sufficient quality

   and relevance for the economy’s needs and access to electronic learning resources to help them improve their

   learning and research capacities was not available. Public universities were hampered by a lack of de facto autonomy,

   which impeded setting or changing curricula without external approval, raising adequate revenue, or developing full-

   time, high-quality faculty.6 Finally, the Ministry of Education and Training (MOET) relied upon a paper-based data

4. Viet Nam had made significant gains in gender equality in HE, with more women enrolled than men. Female

labor participation was high, but challenges remained. There was a sizeable wage gap favoring men, and women

were underrepresented as authors of research-based publications. Further, women were underrepresented in the

applied science and technology fields.



5. Government Policy on HE. Historically, higher education institutions7 (HEI) were under the control of the

ministries to which they reported. Academics, hiring, budget, enrollment and tuition were all centrally determined.

In 1996, the two National Universities, one in Hanoi and one in Ho Chi Minh City, were the first entitled to special

autonomy rights. The 2005 Law on Education and the 2012 Law on Higher Education further loosened restrictions

on HEIs allowing them to create higher quality programs, raise and manage the revenue they needed to do that, hire

and pay faculty and enroll students based on their institutional requirements. A GoV Resolution (Resolution 77/NQ-

CP dated 24 October 2014) issued in late 2014 gave more autonomy to 15 pilot universities during the 2014-2017

period, and an additional three universities were waiting for their pilot proposal to be approved at the time. Those

pilot universities would no longer have to obtain approval on the: (i) number of students they enrolled; (ii) courses

and degree programs they could offer; and (iii) compensation provided to the faculty and staff members of the

universities. They also enjoyed more financial autonomy with respect to higher tuition fee caps and freedom in

revenue-generation decisions.



6. To complement the universities’ greater freedom, the GoV started pushing stronger requirements of quality

assurance. Viet Nam joined the Asia-Pacific Quality Network, the International Network for Quality Assurance

Agencies in Higher Education, and the Association of Southeast Asian nations (ASEAN) Quality Assurance Network.

The HE quality assurance system included internal quality assurance, external quality assurance and quality

assurance agencies. According to the MOET, all HEIs had to establish internal quality assurance units. At the time of

appraisal of the Support for Autonomous Higher Education Project (SAHEP, the Project), more than 75 percent of

universities and 50 percent of colleges had internal quality assurance units. In the 2011-2015 period, 90 percent of

HEIs and programs were expected to have undergone external evaluation.8 In the 2016-2020 period, 95 percent of

HEIs and programs were expected to have completed external evaluation, but both program and institutional

accreditation were behind schedule due to the limited capacity and personnel resources. The first (of four)

independent accreditation centers was established in 2013. By June 2016, more than 20 universities had completed

self-evaluation reports and by November 2020, 20 universities had completed institutional accreditation. MOET

planned to issue new quality standards for institutional and program accreditation and invest in capacity building of

these centers to accelerate the process. MOET planned to issue new quality standards for institutional and program

accreditation and invest in capacity building of these accreditation centers to accelerate the process.

8. Viet Nam National University of Agriculture (VNUA). VNUA was one of the leading HEIs in training and research

for agriculture and rural development. It had 15 faculties, five research institutes and nine research centers. It had

27 undergraduate (UG) programs in 49 disciplines, 19 master programs and 16 doctorate programs. In 2015, VNUA

enrolled 30,452 UG students (59.2% female) and 2,514 postgraduate (PG) students (46% female). The university was

granted autonomy in 2015 aiming to become an internationally and regionally recognized multidisciplinary

agricultural research-based university while ensuring education accessibility for disadvantaged students from poor

or ethnic minority backgrounds.



9. Hanoi University of Science and Technology (HUST). HUST was considered to be the leading science and

technology research and teaching institution in Viet Nam. In 2013, it was ranked first among Viet Namese universities

by the University Ranking by Academic Performance (URAP) and in 2014 and 2016 it was ranked first in Viet Nam

and the 577th in the world by Scimago Institutions Ranking.9 HUST was approved as a pilot autonomous university in

2015. It had three faculties (Foreign Language Studies, Physical Education, and Political Studies), 18 training

institutes, eight research institutes and six research centers. It had 78 UG programs (including 30 engineering

programs and 48 bachelor degree programs), 27 advanced UG programs, 46 master programs and 39 doctorate

programs. In 2015, HUST enrolled 26,456 UG students (18.26% female), and 3,952 PG students (28.92 female). It had

six programs accredited by the Commission des Titres d’Ingénieur10and European Accreditation of Engineering

Program, and one program accredited by the ASEAN University Network – Quality Assurance (AUN-QA).



10. Industry University of Ho Chi Minh City (IUH). Established over 60 years ago as an industrial vocational college,

IUH had become a multi-discipline university. It had 19 faculties, two research institutes and two training centers.

IUH had 36 UG programs, 14 advanced UG programs, 29 vocational programs, and two KOSEN vocational programs.11

In 2015, it enrolled 27,082 UG students (47.1% female), 3,452 vocational college students (41.3% female) and 275

PG students (28% female). IUH was approved as a pilot autonomous university in 2015. It was among the first four

universities in Viet Nam to receive the national institutional accreditation certificate in 2016. IUH’s ten-year plan was

to be among the top ten leading applied technological universities in Viet Nam and have at least one discipline among

the top 50 regionally recognized disciplines.

Components

14. The Project had two components. Component 1 was to support three selected autonomous universities so that

they could provide lessons for the wider HE system. Each of the university belongs to one of three types of HEIs,12and

would serve as pilot models whose lessons could then be generalized into the revised policy on HE autonomy and

quality assurance. Each university was to have its own subcomponent and would manage its own activities.

Component 2 was to strengthen the national HE system by supporting policy development and strengthening key HE

subsystems.

Component 1: Improved research, teaching, and institutional management capacity at three selected autonomous

universities (US$157.0 million equivalent, of which IDA US$140.0 million equivalent).

15. Each university (and thus corresponding subcomponent) aimed to achieve the following three outcomes: (i)

improved research; (ii) improved teaching; and (iii) improved institutional management. Since the selected

universities each had different missions, the plans for meeting these three outcomes was to be specific to each

institution. The amounts allocated to each of three subcomponents was as follows. The investments financed for

each of these subcomponents are presented in Table 1.

16. The three outcome areas that each university aimed to achieved included the following:

17. Outcome area (i): Improved research. Each institution would include, among other specific activities, five core

actions to strengthen research: (i) investing in research laboratory equipment to provide a better research

environment for its staff and students; (ii) formally constituting and monitoring research groups to produce good

quality and relevant research outputs and to attract funding for their work; (iii) establishing an international peer

review research committee to assist individual research groups and the university as a whole to improve the

relevance, productivity, and use of research; (iv) holding an annual Research Week at which students, faculty,

research groups and university leaders and management would lead and participate in a variety of activities that

more closely integrated research and the use of research results into the institutions’ day-to-day functioning; and (v)

specifying and reporting on its activities to strengthen collaboration with industry and the private sector.

18. Outcome area (ii): Improved teaching. This outcome was to be achieved through the implementation of program

accreditation initiatives that ensured that teaching personnel are: (i) using the most up-to-date course contents and

pedagogy; (ii) engaged in frequent reflection on their teaching; (iii) receive regular feedback from students and

employers on the quality and relevance of their teaching and subject matter; and (iv) required to frequently revisit

their teaching programs in light of peer review and assessment. Each institution was to engage in three core activities:

(i) investing in lecture halls, classrooms, practice rooms and faculty facilities to enhance teaching conditions, one of

the elements of program accreditation; (ii) carrying out more systematic reporting on stakeholder satisfaction with

teaching and learning through student surveys, employer surveys and targeted retrospective tracer studies; and (iii)

boosting program quality assurance and accreditation. Each university would ensure that graduate profiles were

available for at least two-thirds of all undergraduate degree programs and would create at least two additional

program cohorts that begin self-evaluation as a prelude to program evaluation.

19. Outcome area (iii): Improved institutional management. The universities would achieve this outcome by: (i)

reviewing and upgrading its management information system as a basis for institutional management improvement,

including with information from the system, the Higher Education Management Information System (HEMIS) and

accreditation data, reviewing and revising its strategic plans comprising the strategy for quality assurance, resource

mobilization, financial planning and provision for institutional accreditation; (ii) making investments in physical and

technical facilities; and (iii) producing an annual ‘Autonomy Implementation Report’ (AIR) presenting the

implementation of the previous year’s Autonomy Decision that highlights the ways in which the university’s

expanded choices under its autonomous regime was leading to greater financial and educational independence and

improved quality and relevance of research and teaching, discussing the AIR with its Council13, and transmitting the

report annually together with the minutes of the Council discussion to the MOET and the relevant line ministries.

Component 2: Strengthened National Higher Education Management System and e-Library establishment

(US$17.6 million equivalent of which IDA US$15.0 million equivalent).

20. This component was designed to strengthen key subsystems of the national HE system and help provide

institutional capacity to facilitate the transfer of lessons learned from the beneficiary universities. It was to

finance activities under four subcomponents.

21. Subcomponent 2.1: Strengthening the national accreditation system (US$1.20 million equivalent, of which

IDA US$1.00 million equivalent) would support the four independent regional centers for education accreditation

to enhance their capacity to conduct institutional and program accreditation processes following new national

quality assurance standards. IDA funds were to finance equipment, software and technical support for building

the management and tracking systems of the four centers. The US$0.20 millions of counterpart funding would

meet training and operating costs. After the investment, accreditation centers would be expected to expand their

ability to generate revenue from their training accreditation services, to subsidize their operational costs.

22. Subcomponent 2.2: Support for policy analysis for sustainable HE financing and autonomy (US$0.50 million

equivalent of counterpart financing) would support training, technical assistance, and consulting services for

MOET to advance the development of policies for sustainable financing of universities and for equitable access

to tertiary education. This would include the review of lessons learned for the implementation of the autonomy

agenda of the whole HE system. Associated operating costs would also be financed.

23. Subcomponent 2.3: Development of the Higher Education Management Information System (US$4.40

million equivalent, of which IDA US$4.00 million equivalent) would finance equipment, software and technical

support for the design, development and operation of a centralized, online management information system to

replace the existing paper-based data collection and reporting system so as to provide timely and accurate data

to inform policy analysis and decision making in HE. The system would collect date on student enrollments,

graduates, teaching staff, training fields, and financial data from individual HEIs.

 A. RELEVANCE OF PDOs



   Assessment of Relevance of PDOs and Rating

   31. When its financing was approved, the Project was relevant with the World Bank’s Country Partnership Framework

   for the Socialist Republic of Viet Nam (the CPF) for the Period FY18-FY22 (Report No. 111771-VN, dated May 4, 2017)

   as it responded directly to the CPF’s Focus Area 2 (Invest in People and Knowledge), Objective 8 (Strengthen the

   relevance and quality of tertiary education and labor market institutions). At that time, building upon its then ongoing

   portfolio in basic education, the World Bank was shifting its focus to tertiary education (academic and technical

   streams) to support the development of a stronger and more competitive labor force with skills that matched the

   needs of employers. A high-quality, relevant, and responsive tertiary education system for teaching, training, and

   research was considered critical to addressing the effect of increasing globalization, deepening structural

   transformation, rising wages, and automation on the nature of jobs in Viet Nam. The World Bank aimed to promote

   the development of a more competitive labor force by strengthening the links between providers of tertiary education

   and industry and enhancing the relevance, quality, and responsiveness of tertiary education institutions (TEI).

   32. The Project’s PDOs continue to be fully aligned with the country’s needs. The World Bank’s CPF for the Socialist

   Republic of Viet Nam for the Period FY23 to FY27 is currently in the process of consultation. As Viet Nam’s economy

   develops and integrates further into the global economy, industry and manufacturing, as well as agriculture and

   services, are expected to become increasingly sophisticated and new skills will be needed to contribute to a changing

   economy. The skills-sets sought by Viet Namese employers will evolve as the economy grows, requiring the labor

   market and HEIs to adapt, especially as workers with advanced education remain in short supply. Many TEIs (colleges,

   universities, and institutes of technical and vocational education and training) suffer from issues linked to quality and

 to adapt to the changing skill set needs of employers. Notwithstanding improvements, the existing regulatory

difficult to complete. There is also an absence of a strong policy to enforce compliance against HEIs to carry out an

institution-level accreditation, combined with lack of incentive to support them to go through the comprehensive

assessment process and register for accreditation. Finally, many institutes may perceive that it is more beneficial and

expeditious to accredit training programs (it is mandatory to carry out small revisions to training programs every two

years to maintain the program accreditation requirement) than to do a longer, institution-level accreditation. Progress

towards improving teaching is rated Substantial before the restructuring and Substantial thereafter.

C. EFFICIENCY



  Assessment of Efficiency and Rating





 53. The Project’s economic efficiency is High. The ex-post economic analysis replicates the analysis performed at

 appraisal utilizing actual costs and benefits to estimate the Project’s impact on the: (a) skills and employability of

 graduates, and (b) research and development (R&D) investment. The ex-post analysis’ findings for each of these is

 detailed in Annex 4 and summarized below.

 54. The first part of the efficiency analysis evaluated the likely impact on increasing the skills and employability of the

 beneficiary graduates, and ultimately, their lifetime wage earnings. The analysis employs the cost-benefit analysis

 (CBA) framework to estimate a plausible range of net economic benefits and internal rates of return (IRR) which could

 result from the project interventions on increasing the skills and employability of the beneficiary graduates. The

 analysis assumes that the beneficiary students in the participating institutions would benefit from the interventions

 through improved teaching and learning environment and better research capacity of the universities and thereby

 attain higher level of skills and earn higher lifetime wage earnings streams. Under the optimistic scenario, a beneficiary

 graduate would earn 9.39 percent higher wages throughout his/her working life. Employing a 5 percent real discount



                                                                                                                   Page 21 of 63

      The World Bank

      Support for Autonomous Higher Education Project (SAHEP) (P156849)







rate, the interventions are estimated to result in a net present value (NPV) of US$418.81 million, with an internal rate

of return (IRR) of 15.82 percent. In the intermediate scenario, the improvement in teaching would result in graduates

earning 4.59 percent higher wages over the working life, resulting in a net present value of US$172.18 million, with

an estimated IRR of 11.38 percent. Lastly, under the pessimistic scenario, it is assumed that graduates’ lifetime wage

earnings would increase 2.27 percent resulting in a net present value of US$52.96 million, with an internal rate of

return (IRR) of 7.70 percent. The “Improving the Skills and Employability of Graduates” component of Project is

expected to a yield positive net present value and an IRR which is substantially greater than the real cost of capital of

5 percent. Further, as shown in the annex, the cost benefit analysis results obtained at project completion turned out

to be better than those envisioned at the appraisal stage. This is because actual student intakes into the two

universities were greater than expected which the actual expenditures were US$6 million lower than budgeted-- only

US$63.62 million against the US$69.81 budgeted at appraisal.

55. The second part of the ex-post economic efficiency analysis attempts to determine the contribution of the R&D

investment to the economy’s output by estimating a probable range of rates of return to R&D investment. The more

statistically significant estimate of the gross rate of return to R&D investment (68.1 percent) is used in the baseline

scenario, together with the actual budget allocation to R&D activities (US$41.51 million) and the stated assumptions

that any investment towards the R&D capital stock is subject to a 15 percent annual depreciation rate and that the

real cost of public funding of 5 percent. The NPV of the investment under this scenario is estimated at US$99.84

million, with an internal rate of return of 45.81 percent. Two other scenarios were considered; even in the most

pessimistic scenario, where the gross rate of return to R&D is 34.4 percent, the internal rate of return is estimated at

13.71 percent. The ex-post NPVs of R&D expenditures turned out to be lower than estimated at appraisal (even though

identical gross rates of return to R&D expenditure were employed) due to the lower actual expenditure on R&D of

US$41.51 million compared to the budgeted amount of US$51.98 million. This analysis does not include the

incremental funding that universities mobilized and are expected to mobilize in the future given their autonomy and

their investments in research, equipment and facilities.

 A. KEY FACTORS DURING PREPARATION



 63. The Project was well aligned with the GoV’s strategic priorities and counted upon its full commitment.

 Specifically, it was well aligned with Viet Nam’s 2011-2020 Socioeconomic Development Strategy, especially the

 Breakthrough Area of Promoting human resources/skills development (particularly skills for modern industry and

 innovation”. The three universities supported by the project were among the list of 15 pilot universities that had

 been provided with greater autonomy and specialized in science and applied technology programs. They were

 delivering training and research in areas critical to Viet Nam’s economic and industrial development goals. Therefore,

 the Project responded fully to the GoV’s strategies.

 64. The World Bank has a long history of support to HE in Viet Nam. The Project was the seventh HE projects

 financed by the World Bank in Viet Nam since 1993. Previous HE operations included: (a) three HE Development

 Policy Operations (P104694, PP116353, P116354) that focused on the overall HE policy framework; (b) HE Project

 One (P004828)and HE Project Two (P079665) that provided small grants to several universities; (c) the New Model

 University Project (P110693 ) that aimed to establish an autonomous research-based university to demonstrate a

 new policy framework on governance, financing and quality; (d) the Health Professional Education Project (P131825)

 that supported four health education universities (implemented by the Ministry of Health and managed by the Health

 Global Practice); (e) the Fostering Innovation through Research, Science and Technology (FIRST) Project (P117394)

 which includes grants for consortia between research institutions and industry (implemented by the Ministry of

 Science and Technology); and (f) the recently approved Enhancing Teacher Effectiveness project (ETEP) (P150060)

 which includes support to selected teacher training universities.

 65. The Project’s design drew heavily upon lessons learned from this earlier World Bank assistance, and from

 global experience relevant to Viet Nam. The lessons learned from earlier assistance were reflected into the project

 implementation arrangements, the project’s focus on quality assurance mechanism through institutional and

 program accreditation, and institutional autonomy support. The project’s implementation arrangements involved

 five independent implementing agencies, that were project owners of their respective components. This enabled the

 HEIs to operationalize their autonomy and helped reduce layers of approval in project management. As Viet Nam

 was transitioning to a more decentralized and autonomous system of quality assurance, it was considered to be more

 effective to create a quality assurance framework aligned with international standards under the ministry’s umbrella

 for a transitional period. Experience from other countries demonstrated the benefits from assigning additional

 authority over academic affairs, human resources and financial matters to universities, and of the value of investing

 in institutional capacity as part of the decentralization process, and to approach delegation of authority in a phased

 manner. The project supported this approach by focusing on selected, strong pilot universities, supported by capacity

 building so that their lessons could be transferred to other institutions.

B. ENVIRONMENTAL, SOCIAL, AND FIDUCIARY COMPLIANCE



80. Environmental. The project was assessed as Category B in view of its location and since its activities were not

expected to generate significant adverse environmental impacts. It triggered the following safeguard policies: (a)

Environmental Assessment (OP/BP 4.01); (b) Physical Cultural Resources (OP 4.11); and (c) Pest Management (OP

4.09). This was because potential impacts during operation could include domestic wastewater and solid wastes,

chemical wastes from laboratories and potential use of synthetic pesticides in plant research, and construction for

IUH would require relocating 31 graves. Each of the project-supported HEIs prepared, consulted and disclosed an

Environmental and Social Management Plan (ESMP) that included measures to address the identified environmental

and social impacts caused during pre-construction, construction and operations phases. The ESMP included

requirements for screening of the research and studies to determine their environmental and social impacts and

mitigation measures during implementation. There were no issues with environmental safeguards during

implementation.

81. Social. The project was expected to have positive social impacts. However, during preparation, screening

revealed that it would have moderate resettlement impacts due to land acquisition associated with the extension

of the IUH campus within an area of 26 hectares. As a result, OP/BP 4.12, Involuntary Resettlement, was triggered.

A social assessment for the IUH’s campus and resettlement site was carried out and a Resettlement Policy

Framework (RPF) and Resettlement Action Plan (RAP) were prepared and consulted. However, due to delays in land

acquisition, IUH withdrew from the project (Section III B). Six months prior to the project closing, in anticipation of

the possible delayed completion of some construction works including the lengthen verification process from the

Government side, a post-closure action plan (PCAP) was prepared. At the time of project closure, all project

construction works were reported to be completed and the PCAP was closed.



          Fiduciary



82. Financial Management (FM). The FM Assessment carried out during appraisal identified several risks,

including: (a) staff of the participating universities did not have experience with World Bank-financed operations;

(b) the delegation of authority between the line ministries and the universities might not be clear and (c) annual

budget allocations to the participating universities might not be sufficient. There were occasional delays in

submitting financial reports and following World Bank recommendations, but overall FM arrangements with

information maintained by the implementing agencies and consolidated by the SAHEP MOET PMU, were found to

be satisfactory and provided accurate and timely information on the Project’s implementation progress, and that

loan proceeds were being used for the intended purposes. The main issue that affected FM almost continuously

were the MOET’s cumbersome budgeting process whereby the operating plans of and budget allocations to HUST,

VNUA and NEU were delayed and payments to contractors delayed as a result.

C. BANK PERFORMANCE



     Quality at Entry



84. The World Bank helped the Government prepare a project that responded to the Government’s priorities, and

incorporated lessons of long experience in the sector (Section IV A) . The Project’s design was concise, well-focused

and relevant, with activities responding to identified priorities. The Project’s institutional arrangements assigned

responsibility for project coordination to a unit in MOET and project management, including M&E, FM, procurement

and safeguard compliance, to PMUs within each of the implementing agencies. The Project’s RF was simple, clear,

concise and well-designed and contained well-defined indicators that were described in the PAD (Section IV A).

However, there were several processes that could have been advanced to preparation, including especially concluding

or at least preparing a final draft of the POM and completing the land acquisition for the IUH campus. However, these

were in part because the Project was approved in 2017 to benefit from the last IDA allocation before Viet Nam graduated

to IBRD. As a result, feasibility studies and other government requirements were not ready prior to approval by the

World Bank’s Board of Directors, as recommended. In addition, the project had to include collateral for the universities

for the on-lent part of the financing. This was a new regulation that was not anticipated during preparation. Therefore,

the World Bank’s team could not anticipate part of these delays that affected the disbursement of the World Bank

financing for civil works and equipment. The identification of risks underestimated the risk that problems with land

acquisition for IUH would have on the project.

immediately following approval, lengthy government approval processes (especially for the restructuring), and in the

middle of a pandemic of unprecedented proportions that impacted implementation. It did so by conducting many

technical missions (in addition to regular implementation support missions), mobilizing World Bank leadership and

V. LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS





       •   When the countries transition from IDA to IBRD lending, it is important to prepare both the Government

           and the World Bank of possible legislative changes that would impact the approval and, later,

           implementation of projects. In this case, the project preparation was rushed to benefit from financing

           under Viet Nam’s IDA allocation before the country graduated to IBRD. As a results several activities, such

           as the preparation by university and approval of feasibility studies by several line ministries that should





                                                                                                                  Page 32 of 63

The World Bank

Support for Autonomous Higher Education Project (SAHEP) (P156849)







    have been undertaken during preparation were delayed to after approval. Also, the eventual new

    financing arrangements involved a change in internal, government requirements that led to further

    delays. These included the exclusion of VAT from loan financing and the need for on-lending agreements

    with the implementing agencies. These had not been contemplated at appraisal and resulted in

    effectiveness delays and later modifications to financing arrangements, respectively.



•   The timing of a project’s preparation and implementation is important in producing results. The Project

    was prepared, approved and implemented at the right time and in strong alignment with the

    universities’ development and implementation plans. As a result, there was strong commitment from

    the implementing agencies to push forward with implementation despite delays that were beyond their

    direct control, including those resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic. Because of this correct timing and

    commitment, the Project was completed with only a six-month extension of the closing date.



•   As a corollary to the above lesson--but equally important in terms of results--decentralizing project

    management responsibility (including all fiduciary functions, M&E, etc.) to the beneficiary

    implementing agencies helps build ownership for results, especially if they are few and have needed

    capacity. Under the Project, all responsibility for project management was delegated to the beneficiary

    HEIs and to the MOET for sector-wide activities. In all, there were four (five before IUH withdrew from the

    Project) implementing agencies, under the overall coordination of the MOET. Despite the risks posed by

    lack of familiarity with World Bank procedures and requirements, those HEIs generally have strong

    capacity to implement projects and programs. Delegation of responsibility to them helped build their

    commitment to implement well the activities that were entrusted to them.



•   Project financing should not be considered for approval with incomplete land acquisition processes; if

    this is not possible, certain steps should be taken to advance the process and identify alternative

    activities for financing should the process not be concluded in a timely fashion. IUH, one of the original

    three HEIs to benefit from activities to improve research capabilities, teaching and institutional capacity

    could not move forward due to issues with land acquisition, and eventually withdrew from the Project.

    The activities it needed to implement to help achieve the PDO simply could not be carried out without

    planned land acquisition. Project teams should carefully identify the required steps in the land acquisition

    process for a project, advance completion of required steps in the process as much as possible before

    approval, pass judgement on prospects for satisfactory conclusion, and maybe even be prepared with

    alternative fallback alternatives if the land acquisition process cannot be concluded in a timely fashion.



•   When accreditation can be granted to both HEI institutions or their programs, a stronger policy is

    required to incentivize and provide support to HEIs to achieve institutional accreditation. The Project

    helped build capacity of the CEAs to process both types of accreditations. But it fell short of achieving

    the target of institutional accreditations while it exceeded by far the target of program accreditations.

    This was due not only to lack of capacity of the CEAs, although it could have become a constraint if more

    HEIs had come forward with institutional accreditation processes. Rather, this was due to the resistance

    of HEIs to go through the process of institution-level accreditation. Many HEIs lack expertise in self-

    evaluation procedures and have a weak quality assurance system and capacity that results in the

    institution-level accreditation process being cumbersome and difficult to complete. There should be a

    strong policy to enforce compliance of HEIs to carry out an institution-level accreditation, combined with

    incentives and assistance to support them in navigating the comprehensive assessment process and



                                                                                                           Page 33 of 63

    The World Bank

    Support for Autonomous Higher Education Project (SAHEP) (P156849)







       register for accreditation. Because of the difficulties faced with institutional accreditation, many institutes

       may perceive that it is more beneficial and expeditious to accredit training programs than to do a longer,

       institution-level accreditation since they are in any event required to carry out small revisions to training

       programs every two years to maintain the program accreditation.

                                  ANNEX 6. HIGHER EDUCATION SECTOR CONTEXT29





  Viet Nam’s higher education has made progress on a number of areas. MOET has played a proactive role

  in initiating positive changes towards modern governance through amendment to the Higher Education

  Law in 2018 and implementation of university autonomy agenda since 2014. Access to higher education

  has more than doubled since 2000, and about 54 percent of the current tertiary education students are

  females. Impressive progress is also seen in the qualification levels of academic staffing: the share of

  university lecturers with master and/or PhD degrees increased from 47 percent in 2007 to 72 percent in

  2015. The number of joint programs and internationally accredited academic programs has also

  increased. In terms of research output, the number of citable documents in per capita terms has almost

  tripled in the past 10 years. Viet Namese universities now appear in the global university rankings: Viet

  Nam National University-Hanoi (VNU-Hanoi), Viet Nam National University Ho Chi Minh City (VNUHCMC)

  and Hanoi University of Science & Technology (HUST) are in in the top 1000 on either QS World University

  Ranking or the Times Higher Education ranking.

  Viet Nam’s tertiary education expanded rapidly between 2000 and 2010, however student enrolment

  has stagnated since the early 2010s largely because of the government policy that puts a break on

  quantitative growth. The enrolment growth from 0.9 million to 2.3 million students between 2000 and

  2010 was driven by a huge increase in enrolments in private tertiary education institutions (TEIs) and non-

  university TEIs (i.e. TVET and professional colleges), with a corresponding increase in the supply of faculty,

  increases in the number of upper secondary education graduates, and the rise of household incomes. The

  Government then significantly revised the 2020 enrolment targets downwards, from 4.5 million to 2.2

  million.

  Currently, Viet Nam’s tertiary education enrollment is below that of 2010 level, and the corresponding

  GER is 28 percent, one of the lowest in East Asia. The reasons for low level of access include: (a) absence

  of a clear financing plan to achieve the originally set quantitative targets, (b) a fragmented tertiary

  education system of universities, colleges and VET sectors managed by multiple ministries, (c) an

  inconsistent regulatory framework that did not encourage private sector expansion even though a high

  target had been set, (d) insufficient student financial aid coverage for low-income students, (e) under-

  development of alternative modes of education including e-learning and MOOCs education, and (f) low

  quantity and quality pipeline of secondary school graduates due to low level of access and learning for

  children from disadvantaged backgrounds.

  Viet Nam’s tertiary education access gaps are most pronounced across income groups, and the situation

  has worsened over time. The nation’s substantial progress in equitable access to basic education has not

  been matched by corresponding opportunities at the tertiary education level. Estimates from the national

  household survey data show that the bottom two quintiles constitute less than 10 percent of all tertiary

  level students. Youth from the top quintile are 59 percentage points more likely to have tertiary education

  Quality and Relevance

  National household surveys suggest that university and college graduates have the highest labor force

  participation (88 percent), are more likely to have better wage jobs with contract and earn substantially

  more than secondary school graduates (wage premium of 50-90 percent). At the same time, a significant

  proportion of firms in Viet Nam report difficulties in recruiting employees with leadership and managerial

  skills, socio-emotional skills, and job-specific technical skills, suggesting large skills gaps.

  Traditional curriculum and pedagogy, under-developed ICT infrastructure, and inadequate staff talent

  management systems are identified as the main reasons for the low level of relevance. Curriculum

  development continues to follow a traditional content-based approach with heavy focus on theoretical

  knowledge, and less on competency-based skills required by the labor market. HEIs place more focus on

  lecturing than student-based learning and skills development. A related inhibiting factor is the low level

  of development of linkages with employers on curriculum revision, work-based learning/internships,

  faculty exchanges and student job placement services. In terms of internationalization, the number of

  joint programs and internationally accredited academic programs has increased but the numbers of

  inbound students and faculty remains low compared to other countries in the region. Despite impressive

  progress in the qualification levels of academic staffing, only 23 percent of academic faculty has a PhD

  degree Existing policies do not adequately incentivize pedagogical innovation/excellence, performance-

  based promotions/pay, and the building of a pipeline of future academic talent. Viet Namese graduate

  schools currently enroll more than 13,000 PhD students and produce more than 1,200 PhD degree

  graduates every year. At the same time, there are shortcomings in terms of quality assurance, funding

  resources and other resources to attract and retain talented PhD students and/or academic talent,

  nationally or internationally. Viet Namese universities in general lack the foundational infrastructure and

  ICT technology to take advantage of digital and/or disruptive technology to support innovative

  educational approaches in the classrooms.

  Research and Technology Transfer

  Viet Nam’s level of research and technology transfer is low compared to regional peers such as the

  Philippines, Indonesia, Thailand, Malaysia, and China. The number of citable documents per 1 million

  inhabitants of Viet Nam is higher than the Philippines, but slightly lower than Indonesia and much lower

  than Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand. When measured by the Hindex, that captures both the quality

  and impact of research output, Viet Nam ranks below all these countries. On technology transfer, Viet

  Nam has a low output at 1.24 patents per million of population, which is less than 10 percent of Malaysia

  and less than 1 percent of China. The key underlying challenges for the low level of research and

  technology transfer are associated with inadequate and inefficient financing, lack of critical mass of

  research talent and insufficient links to the global research frontier, low level university-industry

  linkage, and under-developed research/ICT infrastructure. The low level of R&D expenditure allocation

  is further exacerbated by the fact that university research is severely under-funded as the lion’s share

  goes to Government Research Institutes (GRIs), which are themselves fragmented. Furthermore, the

  limited resources in support of research and technology transfer in universities are spread too thin, do

  the impressive increase in the number of PhD degree graduates in recent years, Viet Nam has yet to reach

  a critical mass of high-quality research talent that could boost the overall research output and its impact,

  especially in terms of industry-relevant research and cross-border collaborative research. Even more

  challenging is the difficulty in attracting and retaining talented researchers who can nurture student

  research talent and generate high-quality research themselves. A related challenge is the fact that Viet

  Namese universities have not taken full advantage of linking with the global research frontiers, such as

  world-class universities and their world-class faculty, on collaborative research programs and

  internationalization of PhD programs.

  Low levels of university-industry collaboration in Viet Nam are the result of the low demand from the

  private sector as well as insufficient industry relevant research taking place at the universities. Not

  surprisingly, a large part of public funding on STI goes to R&D promotion and not industry relevant

  research that could lead to product development and commercialization.

  Finally, research infrastructure such as cutting-edge labs and equipment is still under-developed. There

  are no High- Performance Computing (HPC) facilities to support advanced research, and surprisingly many

  universities are not connected to VinaRen, the National Research and Education Network (NREN), thus

  denying researchers access to global research networks.

  Governance

  In terms of steering at the national level, the higher education system in Viet Nam is highly fragmented

  across many dimensions. There is no single body responsible for the entire tertiary education and

  research system. Two separate ministries (MOET and MOLISA) are responsible for managing the higher

  education sub-sector (universities) and TVET sub-sector (colleges) respectively, with little coordination

  between each other and limited pathways between institutions across the two sub-sectors. The two

  national universities, which are themselves comprised of several specialized universities, are directly

  managed by the Prime Minister’s Office. A further element of complication is the existence of several

  hundred government research institutes (GRIs), in most cases operating independently from the

  universities, resulting in ineffectiveness and inefficiency in both two types of institutions.

  Despite positive changes towards modern governance introduced in the 2018 amended Higher

  Education Law, multiple by-laws issued in recent years have contributed to the complexity of the

  regulatory framework. In addition to MOET, other agencies such as the Ministry of Planning and

  Investment, the Ministry of Finance, the Ministry of Science and Technology, the Ministry of Home Affairs,

  etc. are engaged, resulting in excessive bureaucratic control of higher education institutions and

  sometimes contradicting decrees/circulars issued by different authorities, making university management

  unnecessarily complex and inefficient.

  Viet Nam lacks a unified higher education or labor market information management system (HEMIS,

  LMIS) at the national level. This hinders evidence-based decision-making from all stakeholders. The

  national quality assurance (QA) system is still emerging and existing QA mechanisms are either only

  partially applied or have met with implementation bottlenecks.

  With respect to institutional autonomy and accountability, the recently-implemented reforms

  represent a clear step in the right direction, but the results have been mixed because of significant gaps

  between policy intent and implementation. To date, only 23 out of 171 public universities have taken

  part in the autonomy pilot reform. While Viet Nam performs well on the policy intent of autonomy, as

  autonomy in the amended HE Law 2018. Financial autonomy is largely associated with financial self-

  limitation is that university presidents are still appointed by MOET, thus not always fully adhering to

  talent, knowledge and capacities dispersed across a large number of separate member institutions that

  do not effectively share their financial and scientific resources.

  Similarly, institutional accountability mechanisms are still under-developed. Viet Namese universities

  are expected to (i) maintain education quality and academic integrity with credible QA system in place,

  (ii) comply with grievance mechanisms, and (iii) publicly share information on student placements,

  university performance indicators, financial statements, and minutes of the university council meetings.

  The implementation of this concept is yet to produce the desired outcomes – increase in transparency

  and quality.

  Resource mobilization and allocation with one of the lowest public spending levels as share of GDP

  and one of the highest levels of reliance on tuition fees, Viet Nam is a clear outlier. Between 2004 and

  2015, the government’s resource allocation to the education sector was a healthy 5 percent of GDP and

  17-18 percent of total government spending. However, among the education sub-sectors, tertiary

  education has received the lowest share of public funding allocation (0.33 percent of GDP, 1.1 percent of

  total government spending and 6.1 percent of total government spending on education and training). Not

  surprisingly, when benchmarked against peers and aspirational countries, Viet Nam’s public funding

  allocation to tertiary education (which includes higher education), is by far the lowest (figure ES.3). Per

  student public spending on tertiary education for Viet Nam was US$316 (15 percent of per capita GDP) in

  2015, also one of the lowest compared to its peers. Household contributions to higher education have

  steadily increased over time and now stand as the primary source of funding for the public universities.

  The public subsidy constituted only 22 percent of the total revenues for public universities in 2017 while

  tuition fees accounted for 55 percent of the revenue and the remaining 23 percent came from other

  sources (such as R&D, technology transfer, and other services, etc.). Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs),

  which are used internationally to fill the large financing gaps on infrastructure investment projects, are

  virtually non-existent in the Viet Namese higher education sector, largely because of significant

  legal/regulatory risks and lack of incentives for the private sector parties.

  The Government allocates recurrent funding resources to universities through block grants based on

  historical norms, and not directly linked to the actual number of students or any performance measure.

  Public universities in Viet Nam receive recurrent funding via their respective line ministries, except for the

  two National Universities which receive it directly from the Ministry of Finance (MOF). Per-student public

  funding also widely varies across ministries without any rational link to actual costs. For instance, average

  per student public funding in the 48 universities directly managed by MOET is under US$40/year; the

  comparable figure for Viet Nam National University-Hanoi is close to US$130/year. An important policy

  shift in 2015 stipulated a mechanism for public universities to reduce dependency on state budget and

  increase cost-sharing. However, such a policy appears to narrowly equate financial autonomy to no or

  reduced state budget support which may be feasible for only a handful of universities that are able to

  generate sufficient tuition resources through marketable disciplines. Most universities are unlikely to be
