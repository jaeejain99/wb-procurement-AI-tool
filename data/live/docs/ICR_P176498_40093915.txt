PROGRAM CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES
A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL

1.
This Implementation Completion and Results (ICR) report assesses the results of the Nepal Programmatic Fiscal
Policy for Growth Recovery and Resilience Development Policy Credit (3R DPC) series. The 3R DPC series was originally
conceived in 2020 as a series of three programmatic operations, aligned with the Government of Nepal’s Relief,
Restructuring, and Resilience (3R) plan to mitigate the economic and social impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic and to
advance fiscal, regulatory, and institutional reforms to improve service delivery and promote sustainable business growth.
Two operations in the series were approved in June 2021 and February 2024 in the amount of US$150 million and US$100
million, respectively. The Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA) provided parallel financing of 10 billion yen for
the first operation in May 2022. The programmatic series concluded after two operations with the third planned operation
dropped in November 2023.
2.
Nepal had seen steady but slow economic growth and impressive poverty reduction in the decade before
COVID-19. Nepal’s economic growth averaged 5.0 percent annually between FY11 and FY19, elevating the country to
lower middle-income status in July 2020. Growth was, however, slower than peers. Growth was supported by
consumption fueled by substantial remittance inflows averaging 24.5 percent of GDP per year over the same period.
Supported by remittances, the proportion of households living in extreme poverty also fell sharply from 24.8 percent in
FY11 to 8 percent in FY19. Sluggish growth was accompanied by limited domestic job creation, with a high youth
unemployment rate of 19.6 percent in FY19. Nepal’s high exposure and insufficient resilience to climate and earthquake
related shocks underpinned the government’s commitment to the GRID agenda, as articulated in the Kathmandu
Declaration.1 Fiscal and external balances were relatively contained: fiscal deficits averaged -1.1 percent of GDP per year
during FY13–19 and the current account surplus stood at 0.3 percent of GDP per year over the same period.
3.
The pandemic severely disrupted Nepal’s economy primarily through shocks to services and remittances.
Nationwide mobility restrictions from March to July 2020 devastated tourism, transport, and domestic trade, causing an
estimated one million job losses.2 Remittance inflows fell by 3.4 percent annually in FY20, contributing to Nepal’s first
economic contraction in 40 years FY20 (-2.4 percent).3 To mitigate debt servicing pressures, Nepal participated in the G20 debt service suspension initiative (DSSI), under which debt service in FY20 and FY21 was deferred. Nepal also received
debt service relief of SDR 17.3 million under the IMF Catastrophe Containment and Relief Trust (CCRT).4 Furthermore, an

4.
Nepal’s post-pandemic economic recovery started strong but lost momentum. Following the reopening of
borders, growth rebounded to 4.8 percent in FY21 and to 5.6 percent in FY22, but the recovery lost momentum. Growth
decelerated to 2.0 percent in FY23 due to monetary policy tightening and import restrictions. The current account deficit
narrowed sharply from 12.5 percent of GDP in FY22 to 0.9 percent in FY23, driven by lower imports driven by import bans
on selected luxury goods, higher cash margins on letters of credit and India’s export restrictions on rice and wheat. Exports
picked up gradually, with increased services exports offsetting decline in goods exports, while remittances surged on the
back of continued pace of emigration and currency depreciation. Driven by remittances, foreign reserves stayed strong at
10 months of import cover as of FY23. However, job growth and domestic labor market conditions remained tepid, with
many workers facing multiple employment transitions, lower-quality jobs, and earnings reductions, compounded by
limited social assistance.

Nepal’s fiscal performance in recent years reflects a mix of overall discipline and structural challenges in fiscal
management. As noted earlier, fiscal deficits remained relatively at around 1 percent of GDP from FY 11-19. However,

deficits have since picked up, reaching 5.8 percent of GDP in FY23, driven by revenue slowdown linked to slowing economic
growth and import restrictions, spending pressures from COVID‑19 response needs and spending commitments related
to fiscal federalism. Public debt levels have correspondingly picked up, from 27.2 percent of GDP in FY19 to 42.8 percent
in FY23. But nevertheless, debt levels remain moderate and the country is at a low risk of debt distress as per the May
2023 Joint Bank–Fund DSA. However, overall fiscal discipline and contained debt pressures mask significant fiscal
challenges, starting the quality of revenue collection. Aggregate revenue collections (averaging 21.6 of GDP between FY1924) are relatively strong compared to peers but over half of revenues deriving from trade-related taxes. This exposes the
budget to import volatility and contributes to a complex business environment. At the same time, weaknesses in fiscal
institutions—including recurrent overestimation of both revenues and expenditures, fragmented planning, and limited
credibility of annual budgets—undermine effective fiscal management. Capital expenditure planning remains particularly
weak, with persistent implementation challenges and slow budget execution. Addressing these structural fiscal challenges
is central to strengthening fiscal resilience and provides a strong rationale for the proposed reform program.5
6.
Nepal’s financial sector has remained stable, supported by a decade of incremental reform, but weak
intermediation is a concern. Financial stability improvements are reflected in the overall capital adequacy ratio (CAR)6 at
12.9 percent in FY24, well above the regulatory minimum of 11 percent with key supporting reforms including bank
consolidation, improved capitalization of state‑controlled banks, and progress in supervisory systems. However,
intermediation has weakened markedly in recent years, reflecting both real‑sector challenges and structural bottlenecks
in financial sector oversight. Credit growth has slowed sharply—from double‑digit expansion pre‑COVID-19 to only 4.8
percent in FY23—amid tighter monetary conditions, elevated interest rates, and banks’ increased risk aversion. Liquidity
pressures remain episodic. While reported NPL ratios are still within regulatory thresholds, there are concerns about
underlying asset quality, underscoring the need for continued improvements in loan classification, supervision capacity,
and regulatory independence. 7 These financial‑sector frictions mirror deeper economic constraints: a shortage of
bankable projects, weak competitiveness, and the persistent slump in manufacturing exports, which limits productive
credit demand and reinforces the economy’s reliance on trading activities and consumption‑driven growth. The result is
a financial system that is stable but insufficiently supportive of productive transformation.
7.
Overall, Nepal’s post‑pandemic recovery continued to be constrained by deep structural challenges. Political
instability, infrastructure gaps, and persistent regulatory and governance weaknesses limited the country’s ability to
attract investment and sustain higher growth. Private sector investment growth remained slow, and domestic job creation
insufficient, reflecting long‑standing barriers such as Nepal’s landlocked geography, high transport and logistics costs,
heavy reliance on remittance‑driven consumption that undermines external competitiveness, and regulatory hurdles that
raise the cost of doing business. Infrastructure deficits—particularly in energy transmission, transport connectivity, and
urban services—further hindered firm productivity and the expansion into higher‑value activities.
8.
Nepal also faces severe and recurrent climate and disaster risks. As one of the world’s most climate‑vulnerable
countries8, Nepal experiences frequent floods, landslides, droughts, and extreme weather events, all of which impose
heavy human, fiscal, and economic costs. These shocks repeatedly damage infrastructure, disrupt service delivery, and
reduce agricultural output—pressures that are amplified by rapid urbanization and topographic fragility. Environmental
stresses are compounded by high levels of ambient and indoor air pollution, which carry substantial public health burdens.

Rationale for Bank Support
9.
The 3R DPC series was devised as a central element of the World Bank’s country program in response to the
COVID-19 crisis. The World Bank country program in Nepal was adjusted in line with the WBG COVID‑19 Crisis Approach
Framework9, and responding to government’s own relief, restructuring and resilience program.10 The DPC was framed to
support all four pillars of the Framework—saving lives, protecting the poor and vulnerable, supporting sustainable
business growth, and strengthening institutions for a resilient recovery.
10.
In addition to addressing the social and economic challenges posed by COVID-19, the DPC series also aimed to
lay the foundation for the country to build back better. The DPC program goals were to: (i) strengthen regulatory and
institutional framework to build back better and greener; and (ii) enhance systems and regulations to protect the most
vulnerable and support sustainable business growth. To achieve these goals, the DPC series supported measures such as
reducing import taxes on essential medical supplies, improving healthcare waste management, expanding child grants
and employment support, strengthening foundations for an integrated social registry, facilitating FDI and access to
external commercial borrowing, and advancing debt management, climate responsive public investment, and green fiscal
reforms. The program was consistent with the FY19–23 Country Partnership Framework (CPF) objectives (Report No.
121029-NP), which identified three engagement areas: public institutions (particularly fiscal institutions), private sector
led jobs and growth, and inclusion and resilience.
11.
The World Bank initially opted for a programmatic series of three operations to deliver sustained and flexible
support that could span immediate crisis needs and longer-term recovery. However, several strategic issues emerged
with this approach: first, while combining an emergency COVID‑19 response with a wide-ranging structural agenda
enabled continuity during a volatile period, it also introduced tensions between the urgency of crisis response and the
longer gestation period deeper institutional reforms. Second, the programmatic approach did not unfold through a clear
relief‑to‑recovery progression, as many reforms ended up advancing in parallel rather than sequentially, and the
truncation of the series to two operations limited the intended deepening of reforms. Third, while the areas chosen were
broadly relevant, the design did not sufficiently address critical fiscal‑institutional gaps—notably weaknesses in budget
formulation credibility, revenue administration, expenditure controls, and intergovernmental fiscal architecture—which
limited the overall impact of reforms. Fourth, the breadth of topics—spanning fiscal policy, PIM, social protection,
environmental management, and investment climate—was ambitious relative to government capacity and amidst political
instability. Taken together, these reflections point to the need of a clearer separation between short‑term crisis relief and
medium‑term structural reform packages, with a sharper focus on core fiscal‑institutional bottlenecks that underpin
resilience and sustainable growth.
12.

(GRID) pathway. In practice, however, the implementation of the GRID approach faced important shortcomings. Nepal

and 17 development partners endorsed the Kathmandu Declaration on GRID on September 23, 2021, followed by a GRID

13.
The operation was prepared in close collaboration with development partners. Regular coordination with the
wider international community- including the IMF, Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA 12 ) and ADB helped
maintain complementarity across programs. Additional development partner collaboration was conducted through other
key coordination forums, including the International Development Partner Group (IDPG) and the Economic Growth and
Private Sector (EGPS) working group. The program actions also complemented IMF’s ECF program which was focused on
containing the impact of the pandemic and global shocks on economic activity and aimed to protect vulnerable groups,
preserve macroeconomic and financial stability, and support sustained growth and poverty reduction.13
14.
The DPC series was complemented by other World Bank operations. The Finance for Growth DPC series
(P178531), supported efforts to strengthen financial sector stability, diversify financial solutions, and expanded access to
financial sector stability, diversify financial solutions, and expand access to financial services. Reforms supported by the
3R DPC were backed by the following IPFs: (i) Integrated Public Financial Management Reform Project (P164783), (ii) Youth
Employment Transformation Initiative (YETI) (P160696), (iii) Nepal Urban Governance and Infrastructure Project
(P163418), (iv) Nepal School Sector Development Program (P160748), (v) Enhanced Vocational Education and Training
Project II (P163018), (vi) Higher Education Reforms Project (P147010) and (viii) Strengthening Systems for Social Protection
and Civil Registration (P154548). The Digital Nepal Initiative 14 , aimed at leveraging digital technologies for COVID-19
recovery, resilience building, and inclusive growth, also complemented the reforms supported under the 3R DPC series.

A. RELEVANCE OF PRIOR ACTIONS
Rating: Moderately Satisfactory
23.
A Moderately Satisfactory rating is assigned as most prior actions had a clear link to achievement of objectives,
but the relevance was undermined by substantial revisions as well as shortcomings in the effectiveness of the GRID
approach. While the PDOs were broadly relevant and framed at an appropriate level of ambition under the 3R DPC series,
the relevance of several Prior Actions (PAs) and the strategic coherence of the overall design present a more mixed picture:
a. Most of the PAs maintained a logical connection to the PDO and pillars as discussed in the section below, with
several reforms clearly advancing the core objectives of strengthening fiscal‑institutional frameworks and
enhancing systems for resilience. Actions on debt transparency, public investment management,
environmental safeguards, and social protection each mapped closely to the two pillars of the program and
provided a coherent chain of results from policy adoption to expected outcomes.
b. Despite the breadth of the agenda, most PAs were technically sound, substantively relevant, and well‑aligned
with the DPC’s intended pathways of change—thereby supporting an overall assessment that the operation’s
design retained significant relevance to achieving the PDOs.
c. However, prior action relevance was undermined by a few factors: first, the program’s alignment with the
emerging GRID agenda was conceptually consistent with global priorities, but its relevance to Nepal’s own
development planning proved to be limited. Nepal’s 16th Development Plan did not reference GRID and the
framework did not meaningfully shape government policy decisions or prioritization. Second, the PAs
reflected a breadth of ambition—across fiscal policy, environmental management, investment climate, and
social protection—that was challenging to operationalize in the prevailing political and institutional
environment. Third, several PAs addressed important issues but did not always target the most binding
constraints, particularly regarding core fiscal‑institutional weaknesses. The design weaknesses also arose from
over-ambitious scope and parallel DPC engagements, as well as loss of reform depth due to revisions of
indicative triggers.
24.
The first objective, -focusing on strengthening the regulatory and institutional framework to build back better
and greener-, was expected to be achieved under Pillar 1 through: (i) reducing tax distortions for increased production
and revenue efficiency; (ii) strengthening debt management and transparency; (iii) enhancing the efficiency of and
climate-responsiveness of public capital spending; (iv) building a green and climate-resilient infrastructure; and (v) building
back greener through improved solid waste management.
25.
Prior Action 1 aimed to address structural vulnerabilities in Nepal’s revenue system by reducing reliance on
import taxes and modernizing customs administration—critical steps toward reducing tax distortions for increased
production, export-led growth, and revenue efficiency. Historically, nearly half of Nepal’s tax revenue came from importrelated taxes, creating fiscal risks that became evident during COVID-19 when collapsing imports sharply reduced revenue
and constrained fiscal space. PA#1 under DPC1 supported the amendments to import tax rates for health-related items
as part of the FY21 budget and the approval of the Customs Bill by the cabinet. The FY21 Finance Bill amended import tax
rates for health-related items and exempted essential medical supplies from VAT and customs duties to support the health
response, while the Cabinet approved and submitted the Customs Act to Parliament, introducing provisions aligned with
WTO and WCO standards to streamline customs procedures and improve risk management. Building on this, PA#1 under
DPC2 supported institutional capacity improvements by mandating the Revenue Advisory Board (RAB) to publish a
comprehensive review of VAT exemptions to inform future tax policy adjustments. In the absence of the implementing

regulations of the Customs Act 22 in PA#1 under DPC2, complementary reforms supported by World Bank technical
assistance aimed to rationalize customs duties to lower input costs, reduce protectionism, and promote exports, drawing
on analytical work from the 2021 Nepal Public Expenditure Review. Together, these actions laid the foundation for a more
resilient and competitive economy by diversifying revenue sources, rationalizing tariffs, and improving trade facilitation—
key to achieving the program’s first objective of strengthening the regulatory and institutional framework for a greener,
more sustainable recovery.
26.
Prior Action 2 strengthened Nepal’s debt management framework and improved transparency. Fragmented
responsibilities across agencies hindered the development of a coherent debt strategy and limited disclosure of debt data.
PA#2 under DPC1 supported the Public Debt Management Office (PDMO) to publish biannual debt bulletins with debt
analysis and annual debt information starting FY21, and quarterly bulletins with debt statistics from FY22 onward; the first
biannual bulletin and corresponding data were published online, marking a significant step toward transparency. Building
on this, PA#2 under DPC2 supported enactment of the Public Debt Management Act, 2079 (2022), consolidating debt
management functions under PDMO and providing a legal framework for comprehensive debt operations. Supported by
World Bank technical assistance, PDMO also prepared a medium-term debt strategy and continues to issue regular
updates online. Together, these reforms enhanced transparency, reduced fragmentation, and strengthened Nepal’s
institutional capacity for debt management—key to achieving the program’s first objective.
27.
Prior Action 3 aimed to strengthen public investment management by introducing a systematic framework for
project selection and climate resilience—critical for improving capital budget execution and reducing vulnerability to

Rating: Moderately Unsatisfactory
34.
The 3R DPC series is rated ‘Moderately Unsatisfactory’ in meeting its development objective. Out of 13 Results
Indicators (RIs), 6 were fully achieved or mostly achieved within the planned schedule, 1 was not applicable, and 6 were
not achieved. While several prior actions led to measurable progress, particularly under the first objective related to
strengthening institutional and regulatory frameworks, the DPC series fell short of achieving its objectives overall,
primarily due to design shortcomings compounded by implementation challenges in a highly volatile political
environment. While majority of RIs were measurable, some of the RIs were not easy to validate due to lack of standard
reporting and data constraints. Furthermore, RIs’ targets were mostly assessed as appropriate and there were mostly
minor revisions in the DPC2 compared to the initial design under DPC1.
35.
A key factor undermining efficacy was the substantial revisions of indicative triggers between the first and
second operations. Several reforms initially intended to deepen and consolidate earlier actions were either dropped,
reformulated, or shifted to parallel DPC operations. These changes weakened the results chain, particularly under the
second objective related to protecting the most vulnerable and supporting sustainable business growth.
36.
In addition, the attempt to advance multiple reform agendas simultaneously strained government capacity and
diluted focus of the operation. While reforms related to climate resilience and environmental management showed
progress, system-level outcomes in social protection, human capital, and private sector development were limited. As a
result, progress under the second DPO lagged significantly behind expectations. Failure in achieving targets and the
partial realization of the more substantive goals of certain PAs stemmed from the impacts of COVID-19, frequent revisions
of government targets in the aftermath of elections and other external economic shocks compounded by the loss of
reform momentum amid a period of political instability from mid-2021 to mid-2023. Based on these outcomes, the
achievement of the objectives of this series is rated as ‘moderately unsatisfactory.’

III. OTHER OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS
A. POVERTY, GENDER AND SOCIAL IMPACTS
50.
The policy and institutional reforms supported by the series were expected to have neutral poverty and social
effects. Given economic vulnerabilities and changes in the operating context during the timeframe of the series, as
outlined in Section A, it is difficult to isolate positive or negative poverty and social impacts that are attributable to the 3R
DPC series. However, despite volatile economic performance since FY20 and persistent structural constraints and repeated
shocks, Nepal’s poverty rate has decreased substantially between 2011 and 2023, declining by 86 percent using the 2011
national poverty line and declined by 32 percentage points based on the lower-middle-income poverty line of $4.20/day
(2021 PPP) with extreme poverty (based on $3.00/day, or NPR 113 in 2023) dropping to less than 5 percent. Poverty is
expected to further decline in FY25 and FY26. The series’ focus on fiscal effectiveness, debt transparency, and climateresilient infrastructure laid important groundwork, but significant income and opportunity gaps persist, especially for rural
populations, women, and marginalized groups. The average per capita income of the wealthiest 20 percent Nepalis was
nearly twice that of the bottom 40 percent, and more than four times that of the poorest 20 percent in 2023. The limited
progress in expanding social protection and domestic job creation underscores the need for more targeted interventions
to address entrenched disparities and ensure that future reforms translate into tangible improvements for the most
vulnerable.
51.
There could have been some observable neutral to positive poverty and distributional impacts if Pillar 2
objectives were achieved. To be able to realize the potential for positive poverty impacts under 3R DPC series, primarily
the Integrated Social Registry (ISR) needs to be operationalized and complemented by new legislations that seek to
identify and target the poor, and at scale to be able to respond to disasters and other shocks. Also, the selection criteria

The program was also expected to generate significant positive gender‑related outcomes by addressing

services, incubation, and improved access to productive opportunities—thereby helping reduce gender gaps in access to
finance and economic recovery. These interventions collectively aimed at strengthening women’s economic agency,
enhance their capacity to weather shocks, and contribute to more inclusive and resilient growth trajectories, particularly
in districts highly exposed to climate and livelihood vulnerabilities.
B. ENVIRONMENTAL, FORESTS, AND NATURAL RESOURCE ASPECTS
53.
The operation is expected to generate significant environmental benefits, with minimal risk of adverse effects
on forests or natural resources. PA4, which reduced the customs duties on electric cookstoves from five to one percent,
will have contributed to the lowering of household air pollution (including fine particulate matter) and greenhouse gas
emissions. In 2015, household air pollution from biomass combustion caused more than 14,000 deaths and welfare losses
equivalent to about 35 percent of Nepal’s GDP. Since the duty reduction, imports of electric cookstoves have increased
by over 700 percent (albeit from very low levels - only about 1 percent of Nepal's population was using electric cooking
solutions), encouraging a shift from solid fuels to cleaner technologies. This transition improves indoor air quality and
reduces emissions, particularly as Nepal’s electricity is largely hydropower-based. Complementary power sector reforms
and a participatory Country Action Plan for clean energy adoption further support this transition. PA3, introducing unified
National Project Bank (NPB) guidelines, strengthens the integration of environmental and climate considerations into
public investment planning. This will help restrict approval of projects with harmful environmental impacts and promote
climate adaptation by reducing ecosystem degradation from floods, droughts, and salination. As the National Planning
Commission (NPC), which approved the NPB guidelines, is also responsible for assessing, reviewing, and approving priority
projects as well as overseeing public investment, these guidelines are likely to have a substantial impact on addressing
environmental and climate change challenges and to yield long-term environmental benefits. Lowering the minimum
threshold for foreign direct investment (PA7) may influence consumption and investment patterns, but potential impacts
are not expected to be significant. The newly enacted Environmental Protection Act (EPA) 2019 and Environmental
Protection Rules (EPR) 2020, has introduced several substantially stronger provisions such as in-depth alternative analysis,
inspection and monitoring, fines and penalties, environmental funds, climate change and also introduces the concept of
Strategic Environmental Assessment (SEA) for policies, plans, and programs to improve decision making, integrate

C. INSTITUTIONAL CHANGE/STRENGTHENING
55.
The first pillar of the 3R DPC series aimed to strengthen the regulatory and institutional framework to build
back better and greener. While several prior actions supported important legislative and procedural reforms, the extent
to which these translated into de facto institutional improvements varied significantly. Prior Action 1 sought to reduce
tax distortions through VAT administration modernization and the adoption of a comprehensive Customs Bill and its
implementing regulations. However, progress remained largely on paper, as key regulations were delayed, limiting actual
improvements in customs efficiency or reduced reliance on import‑based taxation. Prior Action 2 strengthened the legal
and procedural foundations of the Public Debt Management Office through regular publication of debt bulletins and the
passage of a comprehensive Public Debt Management Act; here, reforms were more substantively operational, with the
institutionalization of debt reporting practices representing a tangible change. Prior Action 3 enhanced the regulatory
framework for climate‑responsive public capital spending by establishing Financial Procedure and Fiscal Responsibility
Regulations, unified National Project Bank (NPB) guidelines, and revised budget formulation rules. Yet, despite these
improvements in the formal framework, de facto implementation of the NPB remained limited, as project selection and
budgeting often continued outside the prescribed processes. Prior Action 4 supported adoption of the Environmental
Protection Rules (EPR) 2020 and mandated climate adaptation and mitigation assessments for capital projects—an
important legal milestone—though much of the resulting improvement in environmental screening remains procedural
rather than fully embedded in decision‑making. Prior Action 5 supported the Public Health Service Regulations and the
rollout of national healthcare waste management standards, which have yielded more tangible on‑the‑ground change as
hospitals transitioned to non‑burn technologies. Finally, Prior Action 7 supported enactment of the Intergovernmental
Coordination Act, but this contributed primarily to formal institutional architecture: key coordination bodies remained
largely inactive, reflecting broader ambiguities in Nepal’s federal system and underscoring the gap between legal
frameworks and functional intergovernmental cooperation.
56.
The second pillar of the 3R DPC operation supported regulations intended to protect the most vulnerable and
promote sustainable business growth, but the extent of actual institutional change varied considerably across prior
actions. Prior Action 6 supported enhancements in social protection through the expansion of the PMEP and updated
guidelines for the Micro‑Enterprise Development for Poverty Alleviation program aimed at female entrepreneurs, as well
as approval of the framework for establishing the Integrated Social Registry (ISR). While these steps represented important
design‑stage reforms, most remained largely on paper, as implementation stalled: PMEP continued to face operational
inefficiencies, MEDPA coverage fell significantly short of targets, and the ISR did not progress beyond the approval of its
framework—preventing any meaningful system‑level strengthening. Prior Action 7 supported amendments to enable

IV. BANK PERFORMANCE
Rating: Moderately Unsatisfactory
58.
The Bank performance is rated moderately unsatisfactory. This rating reflects weaknesses in quality at entry and
program design, rather than shortcomings in implementation effort. While the Bank team maintained intensive
engagement and provided substantial analytical and advisory support throughout implementation, several design choices
made at preparation constrained the operation’s effectiveness. In particular, the scope of the reform program proved
overly ambitious relative to government capacity, ownership, and the prevailing political economy. The decision to
advance the 3R DPC alongside other policy‑based operations placed significant coordination demands on a single
counterpart institution and diluted focus. In addition, the integration of multiple agendas including COVID-19 crisis
response, fiscal reforms, and GRID agenda—resulted in a complex results framework that proved difficult to
operationalize. At the same time, the Bank demonstrated flexibility and sustained engagement during implementation,
adjusting the program in response to political instability and repeated shocks. These efforts helped preserve progress in
selected areas, but could not fully compensate for the initial design limitations.
Design and Preparation
59.
Analytical work to prepare and benchmark the prior actions in the 3R DPC series was robust, building on the
Systemic Country Diagnostic, World Bank Crisis Approach Paper, and the government’s reform plans (3R and GRID). The
design phase was informed by Nepal’s immediate COVID-19 response, which included a comprehensive medical, social,
and economic support package. Health sector measures focused on expanding testing and contact tracing, upgrading
hospitals, and establishing quarantine facilities, while social protection interventions provided food assistance, utility bill
discounts, and public works programs. Economic relief targeted tax-based measures and concessional lending to SMEs,
complemented by liquidity-enhancing actions from the Nepal Rastra Bank. The government has set up a “Relief,
Restructuring and Resilience” (3R) advisory panel chaired by the Secretary of Finance, with representation from key
government agencies and development partners. These short-term measures shaped the operation’s two-pillar structure:
reforms to strengthen regulatory and institutional frameworks for long-term, green recovery, and measures to protect
vulnerable groups and support private sector resilience (See Annex 5 for a detailed summary of the analytical
underpinnings for the 3R DPC series).

60.
The preparation phase sought to incorporate Nepal’s structural challenges and long‑term development
priorities, including rising fiscal pressures, institutional ambiguities under federalism, weak private sector performance,
and high vulnerability to climate shocks. However, while the operation was framed within the GRID development
pathway and aligned with climate‑resilience priorities highlighted in the Nepal Country Climate and Development Report,

reflection in the 16th National Development Plan. As such, anchoring the DPC strongly in GRID created a misalignment
between Bank ambitions and the level of domestic policy traction.
61.
Finally, the program design did not fully address one of Nepal’s most enduring and explicitly stated
development priorities—jobs and productive employment, particularly for youth and women. The absence of a stronger
jobs lens, despite its centrality to both government policy and long‑term inclusive growth, represented a gap in the
operation’s strategic focus. While the DPC appropriately integrated crisis‑response measures with forward‑looking
reforms in areas such as climate resilience and public investment management, the framing during preparation may have
overestimated government ownership of the GRID agenda and underemphasized employment‑focused reforms that were
more politically salient and economically urgent.
Implementation
62.
Despite frequent changes in Nepal’s leadership and shifting policy priorities, implementation of the 3R DPC
series proceeded with continued engagement from the Bank team. However, much of the “flexibility” demonstrated
during implementation was not a strategic choice but rather a response to circumstances that effectively forced
adjustments in the program. The prolonged political instability, combined with evolving government positions and limited
reform space, required the team to postpone preparation of the second operation and substantially modify the policy
matrix. Several key actions originally identified as indicative triggers during 3R DPC-1—particularly in fiscal, social
protection, and sectoral governance areas—were ultimately dropped, moved to other operations, or lowered in ambition.
This reflected not only the government’s shifting priorities but also the challenges in sustaining reform momentum during
periods of coalition turnover and policy uncertainty. Throughout this period, the Country Management Unit and task team
maintained high‑level and technical engagement, including through the Nepal Development Update, which helped sustain
dialogue and public awareness of core reform needs. Nevertheless, the overall reform package in 3R DPC‑2 was necessarily
narrower and less ambitious than initially designed, underscoring how political volatility constrained the Bank’s ability to
maintain the original depth and sequencing of the reform agenda.
63.
Despite an extensive analytical and advisory effort, government uptake of ASA recommendations was uneven,
and traction on many of the proposed reforms remained limited. Throughout implementation, the task team maintained
steady technical engagement with counterparts and produced a substantial body of analytical work—including the Public
Expenditure Reviews, annual Nepal Fiscal Federalism Update, technical support to the National Natural Resources Fiscal
Commission, the GRID PASA on solid waste management, the CCDR, and ASAs on employment, social protection systems,
and public sector reforms. These analytics were complemented by implementation support through operations such as
the School Sector Development Program, which supported the advancement of selected sectoral improvements in the
fiscal framework. Nevertheless, in several cases, the findings of analytical and advisory support did not translate into
concrete policy actions or were only partially aligned with the DPC prior actions.
64.
The follow‑through from prior actions into effective implementation was constrained by weak institutional
capacity, political uncertainty, and limited demand for technical assistance—issues that stalled progress in areas such as
the Integrated Social Registry, fiscal federalism reforms, and public investment management. As a result, while the
analytical work was rich and continuous, its impact on advancing the reform agenda was modest, reinforcing the broader
challenge of converting a large volume of ASAs into meaningful, de facto institutional change.

65.
In summary, while the 3R DPC series was prepared with high ambitions and supported by strong analytical work
and sustained engagement from the Bank team, the evidence from implementation suggests that the program design
and timing were constrained by limited and shifting government buy‑in. Rather than being a product of deliberate
sequencing, the flexibility shown during preparation and implementation was largely reactive—driven by political

matrix. Several key triggers identified during 3R DPC‑1 were dropped or significantly diluted by the time of 3R DPC‑2,
reflecting both the breadth of the initial agenda and the limited traction across multiple areas. Although coordination with
development partners such as the IMF and JICA remained strong, the overall program ultimately covered too many areas
relative to government capacity and political commitment. As a result, concluding the series with a second operation
allowed the Bank and the government to reset expectations and prepare for a more focused policy engagement moving

V.

RISK TO SUSTAINABILITY OF DEVELOPMENT OUTCOMES

66.
The sustainability of development outcomes under the 3R DPC series faces significant risks from Nepal’s
political context. While these operations aimed to strengthen fiscal resilience, promote green recovery, and protect
vulnerable populations, their trajectory was repeatedly disrupted by political uncertainties. For instance, commitments to
rationalize VAT and corporate tax exemptions under the Revenue Advisory Board’s recommendations were delayed, and
customs modernization measures stalled due to parliamentary gridlock and leadership changes. Despite the approval of
legislative framework for the Integrated Social Registry (ISR), there has been no progress on implementation due to
changing priorities. Similarly, offshore local currency bond issuance process has been stalled several times despite the
amendment of the provision on the issuance of bond in local currency (Second Amendment, 2020), in the absence of
reform ownership. Lack of effective implementation of reforms underscores how fragile reform momentum became in
the face of coalition politics and frequent cabinet reshuffles, which diverted attention from structural reforms to shortterm populist measures.
67.
Nepal is currently under an interim government following a significant political upheaval in September 2025.
Youth-led protests against corruption led to widespread unrest culminating in the resignation of the sitting prime minister
and the dissolution of the House of Representatives. The interim government’s primary mandate is to restore order and
hold parliamentary elections on March 5, 2026. At the sub-national level, funds, functions, and staff are managed by the
seven provinces and 753 local governments for which legislation, institutions, and administrative procedures have been
formalized as constitutionally prescribed while the task of enacting new laws and amending existing ones in line with the
2015 constitution is still in progress.
68.
Political and Governance risks are rated as Moderate. Nepal’s political situation remains fluid amid ongoing
political alignments and uncertainty around government formation following elections. While the extent to which political
risks can be mitigated through project-specific measures is limited, the World Bank will maintain continuity in policy
dialogue, and project design will include specific actions, as required, to build public support through broader stakeholder
engagement, strategic communication, and outreach.
VI. LESSONS AND NEXT PHASE
A. LESSONS LEARNED
69.
Nepal’s experience with Development Policy Operations (DPOs) during FY13–22 shows that overloading the
reform agenda and spreading effort across too many parallel operations reduces impact. Multiple small, sector-specific
Official Use Only

Page 32

The World Bank
Second Fiscal Policy for Growth, Recovery and Resilience Development Policy Credit
(P176498)

ICR DOCUMENT

DPOs created coordination burdens, diluted focus, and strained government capacity—particularly in a low-capacity,
politically fluid environment. Reform momentum was further constrained by limited technical assistance, the
government’s reluctance to borrow for capacity support, and the absence of complementary investment operations to
reinforce policy changes. Ambitious programmatic DPCs require strong and sustained government ownership, particularly
in politically fluid environments. In Nepal, frequent leadership changes and high staff turnover limited reform continuity.
In such contexts, selective and tightly focused operations may be more effective than broad programmatic series.
70.
A key lesson is the need to focus on a narrower set of impactful and feasible reforms, grounded in a clear
assessment of the available reform space. Programs should avoid layering unrelated agendas into a single operation—
such as combining emergency responses with long-term resilience objectives—unless implementation pathways and
institutional capacities genuinely allow it. Legal or procedural reforms alone proved insufficient; effective change required
follow-through through regulations, administrative actions, and operational support.
71.
Policy actions supported by Development Policy Operations are more likely to translate into de facto
institutional change when they are strongly anchored in timely complementary investment operations and sustained
technical assistance. Variation in outcomes across prior actions underscores the importance of reform champions,
sequencing, and implementation readiness. Actions supported by clear institutional mandates and established reform
momentum achieved stronger results than those dependent on new systems or legislative follow‑through. Under the 3R
DPC series, prior actions that were embedded in longer‑standing operational engagements and TA—such as debt
management reforms supported through ongoing engagement with the Public Debt Management Office—showed clearer
institutional uptake and operationalization. Similarly, public investment management reforms linked to the National
Project Bank benefited from preparatory analytical work and MDTF‑financed technical support, enabling procedural
institutionalization despite remaining implementation gaps. In contrast, reforms that relied primarily on legal or
procedural actions without strong operational follow‑through—such as the Integrated Social Registry framework or
selected social protection and federalism‑related reforms—remained largely at the design or approval stage, mostly due
to lack of ownership and timely procurement. This experience underscores that, particularly in low‑capacity and politically
fluid environments, DPOs are best used to reinforce reforms that are already supported by investment lending and
hands‑on technical engagement that is owned by the client, rather than as stand‑alone instruments to drive complex
system‑building reforms.
72.
Implementation challenges also underscored the importance of strong monitoring and evaluation (M&E)
systems, clear reporting mechanisms and reliable data. Weak M&E capacity, paper-based systems, and limited public
data made it difficult to track progress on indicators and reduced accountability. Collaboration among development
partners is critical in low-capacity environments, and identification of reform champions within government plays a vital
role during political uncertainty.
73.
In designing and implementing a program of reforms in a specific area, it is important to conduct a political
economy and stakeholder analysis to account for potential disruptions from elections, cabinet changes, and staff
turnovers and to understand the interests, influence, and incentives of key stakeholders. Throughout the 3R DPC series,
prior actions and indicators were frequently revised or dropped due to shifting government priorities and political
dynamics. For example, review of VAT exemptions and corresponding adjustments through the Finance Bill were replaced
by the review alone, despite a comprehensive stakeholder engagement and Customs Act was delayed by legislative
reforms. In social protection sector, guidelines for the National Social Registry were replaced by an Integrated Social
Registry framework, requiring further amendments. These changes driven by external events, bureaucratic change in
management, or sequencing issues—created a fluid policy matrix, reducing clarity and coherence. It is also important to
understand the influence and capacity of the MoF in mobilizing and coordinating targets reformed with the line ministries,

given that budgetary support does not directly incentivize line ministries’ engagement. Aligning project financing with the
client's capacity and prioritizing political economic issues during project selection, design, and implementation is,
therefore, important. This includes improving the capacity of institutions, engaging sub-nationally, enhancing citizen
engagement, and fostering greater ownership and coalition-building among Nepali stakeholders.40
74.
Another important lesson is the value of broad ex‑ante stakeholder consultation to build legitimacy and expand
political and social buy-in. Early engagement with political leaders, civil servants, subnational authorities, private sector
actors, civil society, and other affected groups can help anticipate resistance, strengthen coalitions, and ensure reforms
reflect the interests and incentives of key stakeholders. Several reforms in the 3R DPC encountered delays despite strong
analytical grounding, in part because constituencies were not adequately engaged upfront. Structured and inclusive
consultations can therefore enhance reform durability and mitigate risks stemming from political changes.
75.
To address these challenges, future DPC programs in Nepal would benefit from more consolidated operations
and avoid layering too many objectives into a single matrix. Advancing multiple DPC series in parallel can overwhelm
coordination capacity and dilute reform focus. Being explicit about the available reform space is essential to prevent
overloading the program with ambitions that exceed political commitment or implementation capacity (for example,
combining the COVID-19 response with GRID agenda). Reforms must also be followed through beyond the passage of
laws: regulations, administrative actions, and operational support are critical to achieve de facto change, and the 3R DPC
series demonstrated that legal or procedural reforms alone were insufficient without sustained follow‑through. In
addition, effective implementation requires strong and complementary sector‑level dialogue, donor coordination, and
technical assistance, coupled with investment operations where needed to ensure reforms take hold. Moving forward,
more consolidated DPCs, tighter alignment between prior actions and realistic implementation pathways, and stronger
government ownership will be vital to enhance the clarity, traction, and durability of Nepal’s policy reform programs.
Enhanced donor coordination, joint World Bank–IFC approaches for private sector development, and mainstreaming
resilience into fiscal and public investment reforms are essential. Finally, stronger monitoring and evaluation systems,
support for reform champions, and transparent stakeholder engagement will improve accountability and continuity.41

ANNEX 3. BORROWER, CO-FINANCIERS, AND OTHER DEVELOPMENT PARTNERS’/STAKEHOLDERS’ COMMENTS
Comments Provided by the Ministry of Finance
Thank you for sharing the ICR of Fiscal DPC. We are concerned that the overall achievement is 'moderately unsatisfactory'.
We understand that the achievement of results under the DPC series depends active participation from wide range of
stakeholders, ownership and readiness to change. This also relates to the broader challenge of making the policy change
relevant and effective. The completion of prior actions within the deadline was challenging, reflecting low level of
coordination among stakeholders. DPCs are provided exclusively in the form of budgetary support. However, in this
operation, the government systems for allocating adequate resources under the relevant budget headings to complete the
agreed prior actions needed to have been stronger. Inadequate budget allocations may have contributed to delays in
achieving desired results. In addition, the single tranche design poses a significant risk, as failure to complete even one
prior action renders the government ineligible to receive the entire budgetary support. Finally, given the nature of
budgetary support, the Ministry of Finance has considerable flexibility in the use of disbursed funds, and there needs to be
clearer discussion among Finance and line ministries to ensure resources are not just adequate but directed toward priority
and critical spending areas. The report has well presented the 'lesson learnt', which will provide important lessons for
future DPOs under preparation.

DPF Prior Actions and Analytical Underpinnings
Prior Actions
Analytical Underpinnings
Pillar A: Strengthen the regulatory and institutional framework to build back better and greener
Prior Action 1: To strengthen revenue collection, the Revenue Advisory
Board (RAB) has published a review of VAT exemptions (RAB Report), as
evidenced by dissemination of the final report on the website of the
Ministry of Finance.

Technical Assistance: Advisory support to MoF.

Prior Action 2: To reduce fragmentation of debt management and
increase debt transparency, the Recipient has enacted the Public Debt
Management Act, 2079 (2022), as evidenced by its publication in the
Recipient’s gazette.

Technical Assistance to PDMO: Debt Management Reform Plan (2019)

Prior Action 3: To enhance climate-resilient infrastructure spending:
(i) the National Planning Commission (NPC) has approved unified National
Project Bank guidelines covering urban, energy, transport, irrigation, and
agricultural projects and incorporated climate resilience; and
(ii) the NPC has approved and issued budget formulation guidelines that
makes compliance with the unified National Project Bank guidelines, and
entry into the National Project Bank a condition to receive funding
through the budget.
Prior Action 4: To promote markets and demand for electric cookstoves,
the Recipient has enacted the Finance Act FY22 to reduce import duties on
electric cookstoves from 5 percent to 1 percent, as evidenced by its
publication in the Recipient’s gazette.

Key findings: The lack of publication of fiscal implications of tax exemptions reduces
tax transparency and increases rent seeking incentives by connected firms.
Therefore, it is important to publish tax expenditure statements regularly.

Key findings: (i) Legal framework for debt management is fragmented and
incomplete; and (ii) sound practice requires a clear and transparent legal framework
to govern public debt management operations, including government guarantees.
ASA: Nepal PER (2021) – chapter on budgeting and public investment management.
Technical Assistance: The PFM Multi-donor Trust Fund (MDTF) Integrated Public
Financial Management Reform Project executed by the GoN supported the hiring of
team of technical consultants for the preparation of the unified National Project
Bank guidelines.
Key findings: Nepal can improve the quality of investment decisions by establishing
proper public investment management mechanisms and creating processes for the
selection and appraisal of capital projects.
ASAs: Nepal PER (2021); Electric Cooking Targets for Nepal’s 2020 NDCs (2021); and
Market Assessment of Efficient Electric Cooking Appliances in Nepal (2021)
Key findings: Nepal’s PER (2021) highlighted the importance of price incentives for
cookstoves and recommended subsidies for electric cookstoves to increase
adoption. The Ministry of Forest and Environment’s Assessment of Electric Cooking
Targets for Nepal’s 2020 NDCs (2021) recommended that financial incentives,
including customs duty reductions, should be granted for electric cookstoves. The
Market Assessment of Efficient Electric Cooking Appliances in Nepal (2021)
supported a reduction of import duties on electric cookstoves.

Prior Action 7: The Ministry of Industry, Commerce and Supplies has, by
way of a notification in the Recipient’s gazette: (i) under section 3 of the
Foreign Investment and Technology Transfer Act 2019 reduced the
minimum FDI threshold to promote FDI inflows to the Recipient; and (ii)
under Section 42 of the Foreign Investment and Technology Transfer Act
2019 granted automatic approval (without prior approval of the
Department of Industry) to equity investment in the Recipient for up to

the Foreign Investment and Technology Transfer Act 2019 and the

Technical Assistance: WB/IFC Post-Covid Private Sector Recovery Program

Key findings: Both the total value of conditional grants and the number of earmarks
have increased between FY20 and FY21, highlighting that excessive earmarking is
not just a transitional phenomenon. Transitioning conditional grants towards
sectoral block grants with limited earmarking can help subnational governments
fully assume their service delivery responsibilities.

Key Findings:
▪ Minimum FDI Threshold: The TA highlighted that minimum investment
requirement is often an arbitrary amount that has little economic significance to
the host country and may have the effect of prohibiting foreign investment in
sectors that could benefit from technology transfer, improved management
practices and other spillovers. Analysis of 110 investments laws revealed that
only 22 countries implement the minimum investment requirement. The
evidence indicates that the countries that practice minimum investment
requirements attract significantly less FDI compared to the world average and
the OECD high income economies. Countries that remain open to foreign
technology and markets have a greater likelihood of themselves becoming more
competitive and sustainable.
▪ Automatic Route: The TA highlighted that foreign investors find FDI entry in
Nepal time-consuming and costly compared to the regional peers, many of
which (such as India) implemented the FDI automatic route. Key challenges
included delays in obtaining regulatory approvals due to screening of all the
transaction documents and lack of digitization. The operationalization of the
automatic route will streamline the process of foreign investment by making it:
(a) simple and unambiguous: reducing approval process and screening of
transaction documents; and (b) cost effective: reduced delays will lower cost of
setting up investments for the foreign investors.
