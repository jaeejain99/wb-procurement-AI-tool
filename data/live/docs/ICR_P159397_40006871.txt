  I.    PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES





    A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL





Context

1.      Viet Nam's transition from a rural to an urban society has accompanied rapid economic growth and poverty

reduction, elevating the country to a lower middle-income status. Between 2004 and 2013, Viet Nam's GDP per capita

more than tripled from US$560 to US$1,800, and the poverty rate dropped from nearly 60 percent in the 1990s to about

13.5 percent in 2014. Urbanization has also accelerated, with over 34 percent of the population living in urban areas in

2015, up from 24 percent in 2000. With an urban growth rate of 3.1 percent in 2014, it was projected that over half of the

population would reside in cities by 2045.



2.       Developing a network of secondary cities with adequate infrastructure was critical for the country’s sustainable

and balanced development, and the Mekong Delta Region (MDR) was particularly important. While most urban

populations were concentrated in three major cities, Ho Chi Minh City, Ha Noi, and Hai Phong, smaller cities with 100,000-

500,000 residents were increasingly growing. In 2014, the MDR was home to approximately 17.5 million or almost one-

fifth of Viet Nam’s total population, with a quarter of them living in urban areas. Alongside urbanization, the industrial

sector played a bigger role in the MDR with its share of GDP contribution increasing from 18 percent in 2000 to 25 percent

in 2010.



3.       The secondary cities in MDR needed more investments to realize their full potential. The MDR’s provincial

capitals served as economic hubs and thus were growing with rural-urban migration. However, they lagged in basic service

provision and poverty reduction, revealing significant regional disparities. In MDR, despite the decline in the poverty rate,

the poverty incidence remained the second highest in the country with 1.7 million poor, especially among ethnic

    minorities. MDR was also highly impacted by climate change and experiencing higher ambient temperatures, abnormal

    weather conditions, saltwater intrusion, and increasing intensity and frequency of droughts and floods. The region needed

    to address infrastructure gaps and vulnerability to flooding to realize its full potential and keep up with rural-urban

    migration.



    4.       The Government of Viet Nam (GoV) promoted sustainable urbanization to achieve its development aspirations,

    with an emphasis on the role of secondary and tertiary cities. The Framework Master Plan for Urban Development in Viet

    Nam to 2025 and Vision to 2050 focused on achieving balanced and strategic growth through systemic management of

    urban centers of various grades and types in the country. Specifically, it envisioned the development of secondary and

    tertiary cities as hubs to drive development within larger urban areas and provinces. Further, the National Urban

    Development Program 2011‐2020 (NUDP) offered a vision to develop provincial capitals as models to boost the local

    economy and balanced regional development. The program highlighted the importance of meeting the increased demand

    for infrastructure and services in cities and effectively tackling climate change.



    5.       The World Bank has been supporting the GoV’s urban upgrading program through a series of projects

    addressing the dire infrastructure and service needs in Low-Income Areas (LIAs). The Viet Nam Urban Upgrading Project

    (VUUP) was the first in the series to support upgrading low-income, poorly serviced and densely populated areas in Nam

    Dinh, Hai Phong, Can Tho, and Ho Chi Minh City (2004-2014, US$222.5 million). It was followed by the Mekong Delta

    Region Urban Upgrading Project (MDR UUP), which supported six provincial cities 1 in the MDR (2012-2018, US$292

    million). Investments under VUUP and MDR UUP significantly improved the lives of the urban poor and benefited 1.9

    million and 2.63 million people in total, respectively. This includes over a million beneficiaries in 409 LIAs upgraded under

    both projects (VUUP 295 LIAs under VUUP and 114 LIAs under MDR UUP), representing about 12 percent of the total

    population in ten participating cities. In 2009, as part of these World Bank-funded projects, GoV formulated and approved

    the National Urban Upgrading Program (NUUP) to guide the upgrading efforts with a participatory approach.



    6.       Building on the successful experience, the Scaling Up Urban Upgrading Project (SUUP) expanded upgrading

    efforts to the remaining seven provincial cities in MDR and added a new emphasis on promoting integrated urban

    planning. Cities of Bac Lieu, Ben Tre, Long Xuyen, Soc Trang, Tan An, Vi Thanh, and Vinh Long were centers of trade,

    services, and industries in the region but lagged in the provision of basic infrastructure. As a result, the quality of life was

    low in LIAs and suboptimal connectivity led to the further exclusion of the urban poor from the wider city. The SUUP

    continued with the previous design of connecting tertiary infrastructure investments in LIAs with primary and secondary

    infrastructure investments in the city and complemented this with the support to promote resilient, green and inclusive

    infrastructure design and integrated and risk-informed master planning. This was critical given that at an early stage of

    urbanization, these cities could develop into a compact urban form.



    7.      With a strong track record, the World Bank was a credible partner for scaling up urban upgrading, and it was

    well aligned with the GoV and World Bank priorities. The SUUP supported Viet Nam’s 2011-2020 Socio-Economic

    Development Strategy, which proposed actions to develop infrastructure and promote harmonious and sustainable

    regional development, build up new urban and rural areas and effectively deal with climate change. The project

    contributed to the priorities of the 2016 Systematic Country Diagnostic (SCD) to build productive infrastructure and

    competitive cities and augment resilience to climate change and benefits from mitigation. Both the SCD and Country

    Partnership Framework (CPF) for FY2018-2020 (approved on May 4, 2017)2 drew from Viet Nam 2035 Towards Prosperity,

    Components

    11.    The project included four components which were implemented by the Project Management Units (PMUs) in the

    seven project cities: Bac Lieu, Ben Tre, Long Xuyen, Tan An, Soc Trang, Vi Thanh, and Vinh Long. The summary of the

    subproject investments per city and component is in Annex 3.

    12.      Component 1: Tertiary Infrastructure Upgrading in Low Income Area (Total Cost: US$62.8 million, of which Bank

    financing was US$54.2 million). This component supported tertiary infrastructure investments in LIAs including: (i)

    construction, rehabilitation, and upgrading of roads and lanes; (ii) construction and rehabilitation of drains; (iii)

    improvements to environmental sanitation by rehabilitating or constructing public sewers, constructing septic tanks,

    providing access to septic management services, and house connections to public sewers; (iv) improvement of water

    supply including the installation of metered domestic connections; (v) provision of metered domestic connections for

    electricity and public lighting in residential lanes and streets; and (vi) construction and rehabilitation of social

    infrastructure facilities such as schools, markets, community halls, public places and green spaces.

    13.       Component 2: Priority Primary and Secondary Infrastructure. (Total Cost: US$228.9 million, of which Bank

    financing was US$170.1 million). The component supported the improvement of priority networked infrastructure to

    increase connectivity of primary and secondary infrastructure with tertiary infrastructure in LIAs. Social infrastructure

    facilities such as public places and green spaces were included to benefit the urban poor. A hydraulic model was to be

    developed for the catchment areas of the infrastructure investments and integrated with the existing urban plans for flood

    and salinity intrusion control, drainage, and waterways investments.

    14.    Component 3: Resettlement Sites (Total cost: US$10.2 million, of which Bank financing was US$8 million). The

    component supported the construction of resettlement sites for project-affected persons, including the construction of

    primary, secondary and tertiary infrastructure and public facilities.

    15.      Component 4: Project Management Support (Total cost: US$28 million, of which Bank financing was US $7.7

    million). The component provided a Technical Assistance (TA) package to enhance the cities’ capacity to effectively

    manage urban development including (i) Implementation Support, (ii) Support for Investment Design and Integrated

    Urban Planning, (iii) Master Plan Revision in three cities (Long Xuyen, Vi Thanh, and Vinh Long).

    Rationale for Changes and Their Implication on the Original Theory of Change

    20.     The first restructuring was to cancel a portion of IDA credit proceeds (US$11.45 million) in response to the the

    request by the Ministry of Finance (MOF) to recycle unused IDA funds under the Viet Nam Inclusive and Sustainable

    Recovery Development Policy Financing (P181146), which needed to be processed before the end of FY23. The second

    restructuring that formalized the reduced scope of work was necessary for achieving the PDO with realistic targets within

    the remaining time and given available funds and other operational challenges. The project experienced a 19-month delay

    between project approval and effectiveness. The slow government approval process is discussed later as a key factor

    during implementation. Further, the project funds allocation through the government’s Medium-Term Investment Plan

    (MTIP) was limited and delayed in 2019 and 2020. Particularly, allocation of counterpart funds for compensation and

    resettlement was insufficient between 2019 and 2021. Compounded by the movement restrictions and supply chain

    21.      The second restructuring took place two years after the MTR and only a few days before the original project

    closing date. This is because descoping a project within the Government of Viet Nam’s system follows a similar process

    and timeline of project preparation, where revisions to each city’s Project Investment Proposal (PIP) and subsequent

     III.   KEY FACTORS AFFECTED IMPLEMENTATION AND OUTCOME





     A. KEY FACTORS DURING PREPARATION

 41.     A proven project design to scale up urban upgrading building on the World Bank’s long -term engagement. The

 project was part of the World Bank’s long-term engagement in the region and the last in a series of urban upgrading

 projects. SUUP followed a similar structure to the previous project (MDR UUP) and added a new emphasis by promoting

 integrated urban planning. Considering that seven participating cities were at an early stage of urbanization and thus had

 the potential to grow in a compact and coordinated manner, the project proposal to enhance the overall urban planning

 framework and practice was strategic and long-term. The importance of integrated urban planning is also reflected in the

 PDO. Given this, the project could have made urban planning activities more prominent in the design – for example,

 through a separate component– but due to the GoV stance not to use ODA funds to support technical activities,20 urban

 planning activities were folded under Component 4 on Project Management Support. The project complemented this

 with a detailed activity description in the Annex of the PAD and a set of intermediate indicators to measure the outputs

 of these activities.



 42.      Institutional arrangement: absence of central coordination. Unlike MDR UUP, for which establishing a PCU was

 evaluated as one of the success factors, SUUP was designed without a Project Coordination Unit (PCU) in the central

 government. As a result, project implementation and impact later suffered from the lack of PCU. Despite the strong tie

 with the Ministry of Construction (MOC) and its active participation during preparation, SUUP was designed in the end as

 a collation of seven separate projects without central coordination. This was largely attributed to the GoV’s stance against

 using ODA funds for “soft” activities, which seemed to have led to a decision not to spend ODA on project coordination

 for SUUP, particularly given the higher interest rate of IDA-SUF (see below on this).21 The Bank team tried to reduce the

 number of participating cities to a more manageable level (4-5 cities) but all seven cities engaged during preparation

 wished to remain part of the project. Although Vinh Long was designated as a coordinating city, the lack of PCU

 significantly increased the workload for the PMUs and World Bank team in engaging the central government for key

 decisions and technical guidance.22

 to the project owner, have changed the borrowing dynamic between ministries, provincial authorities, and corporations.

 The full impact of the new laws was yet to be seen at the time of the SUUP preparation, particularly as the central

 government was still figuring out the implementation guidelines. While such impacts could not be fully addressed at a

 project level, it ended up causing disbursement challenges during SUUP implementation and even after the closing.

 45.      Challenges with land acquisition and resettlement. The shortage of counterpart funds for resettlement was

 partly due to the drastic increase of land prices in several cities during implementation, which doubled or tripled the

 estimated cost of land acquisition in some cases. The estimated cost of LAR during preparation, which was based on the

 feasibility studies, deviated significantly from the market price of land during implementation. The social safeguards

 assessment in the PAD rightly focused on estimating the number of project-affected households but did not cover the

 land price issues much. In addition to land price increases, eligibility for full compensation became a point of contention

 during implementation, as the GoV regulation and practice does not provide full compensation for those without legal

 rights over property. The difference in LAR policies between the government and the Bank was not fully resolved in the

 end, leaving several pending compensation cases even after the project closing.

 46.      Impact of the Covid-19 pandemic. Slightly over a year after the project became effective, the Covid-19 pandemic

 hit, disrupting the implementation of most projects around the world. Mobility restrictions slowed the construction works

 and caused construction material price increases and a shortage in supply, which was exacerbated by the unpredictable

 and more serious impacts of the weather events in the region. Notably, several cities struggled to source sand and other

 materials necessary for timely construction progress. The pandemic- related risks for SUUP declined over time (from

 Substantial to Moderate in March 2021 and Moderate to Low in January 2023). Like the fund allocation challenges above,

 they were largely outside the project’s control and affected the overall Bank portfolio in the country. Given this, the

 project would have benefited from a longer extension to complete the works and achieve the PDO more satisfactorily.

M&E Design

48.      The project results framework consisted of indicators clearly linked to the two parts of the PDO with a baseline

 RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME

 69.     Sustainability of the improved urban planning capacity. Conducting highly technical work such as hydraulic

 modeling or using masterplans to guide area- or project-based investments will require continuous technical support from

 the central government. Not having the central ministry directly involved in the project increases the risk of sustaining

 the urban planning capacity built under the project.

 70.     Operation and Maintenance (O&M) of infrastructure investment. The overall quality of construction at project

 completion was satisfactory, increasing the sustainability of the infrastructure investment. SUUP developed O&M plans

 for primary and secondary infrastructure, while O&M for tertiary investments in LIAs is the responsibility of LIA

 communities as agreed in the CUPs. Consultants hired by the project prepared the O&M plans, and thus, they have an

 almost identical structure and content, presenting a construction maintenance plan and discussing issues of construction

 warranty (or defects liability period), quality management, and costs. Except for Vinh Long that prepared a specific O&M

 plan per construction package, other plans do not specify which department in the city will be responsible for O&M and

 how much budget will be allocated. During the ICR mission, it was clarified that the Urban Management Division of the

 City People’s Committee (CPC) will be responsible for O&M going forward. Beyond the construction warranty during

 which contractors will be responsible for any defects and O&M, cities may struggle with the O&M of the infrastructure

 investments, particularly given the limited budget of many cities.





     V.   LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

71.     Strengthening the institutional capacity of city governments in Viet Nam will be critical for managing complex

investments and sustaining their long-term impact at the local level. The absence of a central PCU was a missed

opportunity for the GoV to capture SUUP’s experience and apply project lessons to national programs such as the national

urban development or upgrading program. Given that scaling-up of upgrading involves newly participating cities with a

great need for capacity building, both the Bank and GoV could have invested in strengthening a national institution for

supporting local governments’ urban upgrading and infrastructure investments, as done in Indonesia. Participating cities

emphasized the importance of mobilizing technical support and building PMU capacity early on and getting timely and

effective direction from the project steering committee as a main lesson.30 Still, their performance varied in the end and

72.      For complex, high-impact projects such as urban upgrading, designing a series of programs supported by a

73.      A trade-off between implementation efficiency and results should be considered for project restructuring and

extension and clear communication for agile, proactive decision-making is critical. A major decision to reduce the scope

of works was made during MTR, which was adequate and proactive given the progress, remaining time and available funds.

However, some activities still experienced implementation challenges near the original project closing. At this point,

allowing sufficient time extension would have been beneficial for achieving the PDO, considering the commitment level

of the participating cities and the strength of the partnership between the Bank and clients. At closing, the relative

importance of having more fully completed projects was likely to outweigh the utility of closing the project early with

unfinished works. The golden time for adjusting the project objective or scope to facilitate the project completion on time

already passed after MTR. A longer time extension, such as a one-year extension, as granted to other projects in Viet Nam,

could have helped SUUP complete most civil works at higher quality, address remaining complaints and reach the status

of fuller disbursement and compliance. A more agile approach to restructuring could have also helped the project achieve

the outcome rating that is a fairer reflection of the results. The timing of restructuring is critical for split rating and a light

restructuring to formalize the reduced scope right after the MTR would have enabled the project to achieve this.

74.     Streamlining the GoV regulations on subnational investment planning and management and harmonizing the

procedures between the GoV and Bank to the extent possible will be important for future project financing. All

participating cities noted the difference between Viet Namese and Bank policies and regulations on financial management,

procurement and safeguards as one of the prime challenges. Navigating the new government policies on on-granting and

on-lending portions of the fund transfer to subnational governments was also a difficult experience for the whole of the

government and cities have requested for the MPII and MOF to collaborate closely and find a resolution to help expedite

implementation. This was not specific to SUUP and impacted the Bank’s overall portfolio in the country. For future projects,

upward accountability under SUUP whereby PMUs and cities engage provincial and central governments for guidance and

problem resolution, can be complemented by downward accountability with stronger central coordination and necessary

reforms. For the Bank project financing, the Bank can put more effort into identifying the known policy and regulatory

differences and clarifying the resolution mechanisms more systematically.

75.     Investing in effective land administration and management will be critical for facilitating access to land for

urban upgrading and infrastructure investments. Cities identify Land acquisition and Resettlement (LAR) as the biggest

challenge, whereby differences between the government and Bank policies created confusion. LAR is a common challenge

in Viet Nam, affecting other infrastructure projects, but was particularly significant for an urban upgrading project like

SUUP that operated in highly populated urban areas across seven cities. The analysis conducted by the Bank in May 202331

1.      Overview. At the time of appraisal, the project invested approximately US$330 million across seven participating

provinces, with the World Bank financing of US$240 million. Economic efficiency is evaluated across three Components.

Component 1 invests in upgrading 30 low-income areas (LIAs), with improved infrastructure mainly consisting of lanes,

walkways, drainage and sewerage systems, street lighting, power supply, water supply, and solid waste management.

Component 2 invested in (i) the rehabilitation and embankment of 16 canals, grouped in 14 civil work packages, that mainly

function as open sewers in all cities, and the creation of public spaces along two riverfronts in Vi Thanh and Tan An; and (ii)

secondary road and bridge projects.

2.       Through restructuring on May 28, 2023, and December 22, 2023, the project cancelled nearly US$71 million of the

Bank's funding following the work scope adjustment to achieve the project development objective (PDO) within the

remaining timeframe and available resources, as well as including procurement savings. Thus, at project closing, economic

efficiency is evaluated based on the restructured scope of work and provides information on the economic benefits of the

project, including investments in Component 1 (tertiary infrastructure for low-income areas), Component 2A (secondary

infrastructure for environmental sanitation), and Component 2B (secondary roads and bridges). However, at project

closing, four subprojects (two canal projects in Long Xuyen, the Canal 62 package in Vi Thanh, and the Nguyen Van Linh

Bridge in Soc Trang) were not fully completed with project funds and will continue to be completed with provincial budget

funds over the next two years, as committed by the provinces.

Methodology and Key Assumptions

3.      Methodology. The updated analysis uses the same methodology as outlined in the Project Appraisal Document

(PAD), adhering to standard economic appraisal methods for infrastructure improvement programs. This method compares

the annual discounted costs and benefits of the project to the society over the project’s life cycle in two scenarios–that is,

"with" and "without" project scenarios–, using Net Present Value (NPV) and Internal Rate of Return (IRR) indicators to

assess economic viability. The discount rate and conversion factors from the time of appraisal remain valid at the time of

project closing.

4.      Price basis. The estimated economic costs and benefits are expressed in constant values at 2024 prices. Since

expected income is projected to grow at a higher rate than general inflation, the analysis accounts for real income growth

throughout the benefit period (this is reflected, for example, in the significant increase in the average value of travel time

savings due to the transport subprojects). Economic costs and benefits are converted into US dollars (an international

reference currency) at the official 2024 exchange rate of 24,320 VND per US dollar. This rate is close to the market rate,

and adjusting for the shadow foreign exchange rate is not considered necessary.

5.      Conversion factors. The conversion factors are updated in accordance with the conversion factors used for other

projects at the time of project closing in Viet Nam and for World Bank projects.

estimated according to the commitment from the provinces (Long Xuyen, Vi Thanh, and Soc Trang).

7.      O&M Costs. At appraisal, O&M (Operations and Maintenance) costs are estimated as a percentage of the economic

investment costs: 3 percent for Component 1, 2 percent for Component 2A, and three different percentages for the road

and bridge subprojects (1% for routine maintenance, 5% for periodic maintenance every 4 years, and 30 percent for major

repairs every 12 years). At the time of project closing, O&M costs are specifically estimated for each subproject. However,

most provinces have not reported specific O&M cost estimates, so the O&M costs in the project completion report are still

estimated based on the percentages of economic investment costs.

Updated Economic Benefits

8.       Economic benefits of Components 1 and 2A. At appraisal (in the PAD), Component 1 planned to finance tertiary

infrastructure in 30 low-income areas (LIA) across seven participating cities, and Component 2A founded the restoration

and reinforcement of 14 canal packages, primarily functioning as open drains (in all cities), along with the creation of public

spaces along the two riverbanks (in Vi Thanh and Tan An). Following the project restructuring, the economic benefits at

project closing were calculated based on 28 LIA in the seven participating cities (with 2 work packages canceled, including

LIA 1 in Long Xuyen and LIA 2 in Tan An) and 12 canals (with 2 work packages canceled in Bac Lieu and Long Xuyen, including

the Xang Bridge canal and the Long Xuyen canal). Both Component 1 and 2A provide significant economic benefits for

residents living in areas affected by the subprojects, including:

 • Improved public health. As mentioned in the PAD, this is the most important economic benefit of the project. At the

   time of project closing, the data for assessing the benefits of improved public health were updated based on the actual

   number of beneficiaries as reported by the provinces.

 • Avoided flood damage. Improving the canals and rivers will reduce flood damage. Data on flood damage has been

   updated based on the actual reports submitted by the provinces.

 • Improved accessibility. For all the LIAs covered by the project, improving the proposed lanes and walkways will

   enhance access to the areas within the LIA, both for pedestrians and motorcycle users.

 • Improved air quality. Improving the drainage system - combined with investments in solid waste management - will

   not only help improve public health and reduce flooding but also create a more pleasant living environment.

 • Improved security. This benefit will be provided by improved street lighting.

 • Improved urban amenities. Creating public spaces along rivers and lakes is expected to enhance the well-being of the

   entire city’s population.

9.      According to the beneficiary data provided by the provinces after the project closing, the combined impact of these

benefits results in a significant improvement in the quality of life for approximately 79,100 people living in 28 low-income

areas (LIA) and over 360,000 people living in areas where canals and riverfronts will be improved.

Comments from Ben Tre



To the World Bank’s Task Team,

Ben Tre PMU would like to provide some comments related to the Implementation Completion Report (ICR) of the Viet

Nam Scaling-up Urban Upgrading (SUUP) Project.

First of all, Ben Tre PMU would like to thank the World Bank’s Task Team for your timely support during the project

implementation. Having reviewed the ICR of the SUUP Project, Ben Tre PMU found it is important to add and emphasize

the following assessments:

1. Through the project implementation, most cities (4 out of 7 cities: Ben Tre, Bac Lieu, Vinh Long, and Tan An) have

     completed all investment items under the restructured plan. The remaining three cities—Soc Trang, Vi Thanh, and

     Long Xuyen—have largely completed most of the items following the restructuring, and there is potential for the

     remaining tasks in these cities to be finished after the SUUP Project is closed. Therefore, Ben Tre PMU believes that

     the achievement rating of the project’s final outcome as "Moderately Unsatisfactory" does not fully reflect the efforts

     of the participating cities, nor the focused leadership from the local authorities in ensuring the project’s completion.

     Ben Tre PMU, therefore, respectfully requests that the World Bank reassess the project’s final outcome, which should

     be rated "Moderately Satisfactory".



2. Among the factors affecting the project performance, in addition to the COVID 19 pandemic, Ben Tre PMU believes

   that it is critical to add the wide range of socio-economic developments and climate changes in Viet Nam in general

   and the Mekong Delta region in particular in the 2017-2020 and 2020-2024 periods that significantly affected the

   delivery of project investments (for example, Ben Tre had to face a shortage of sand for filling roadbeds, etc.)



3. In the Ben Tre city sub-project, the LIAs in Component 1 should be considered a bright spot thanks to the

   determination of the whole city to implement the voluntary land donation mechanism (VLD) right from the

   beginning. As a result, 100% of the people living in the project area supported with a high degree of consensus in

   order to facilitate the swift completion of investments in LIAs, generating benefits to the community.



Ben Tre PMU would like to send these additional comments, hoping that the World Bank will consider them to have

another perspective on evaluating the achievement of final outcomes in SUUP Project.

Best regards.

Box 7.2. Improved livelihoods for the elderly population: Interview with Mr. Luu Van Nam from Alley 1 LIA 3

Bac Lieu City invested in six LIAs covering 85.1 ha of area. The project built and upgraded roads and drainage in LIAs,

connecting households to newly built or improved sewer pipes to better protect residents from rainfall flooding. The

PCR for Bac Lieu highlighted improved livelihoods for the elderly population, as captured in the interview with Mr. Luu

Van Nam from Alley 1 LIA 3, who explains how the project helped his livelihood, especially during the rainy season.

“My house used to be the most flooded in Alley 1, LIA 3. Whenever it rained heavily, the water in the house would take

a week to recede. To avoid damaging the furniture, I put bricks under the bed, wardrobe, table, and chairs, almost 20

cm high. Now, thanks to the project’s policy and the support of the local government and my family, I have been able

to rebuild a new house and raise the yard. Therefore, I am no longer afraid of heavy rains. I am very satisfied with the

project and very grateful to the World Bank, PMU, and local government for their attention to elderly and

disadvantaged households like me.”
