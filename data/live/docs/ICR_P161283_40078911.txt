I. PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES
A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL
Context
1.
At the time of appraisal of the ‘Investing and Innovating for Grassroots Health Service Delivery’ (GSD)
Project in February 2019, Viet Nam had seen decades of poverty reduction and strong economic growth. Poverty
stood at 5.7 percent of the population at national poverty lines in 2019, down from 58.1 percent in 19921. This positive
trend was also seen in the health sector, where rising life expectancy (74.1 years in 2019, up from 68.4 years three
decades prior2) was accompanied by improvements in maternal and child health (MCH) outcomes. Nonetheless, with
a Gini index of 35.7 in both 1992 and 2018, little progress had been made to structurally reduce socioeconomic
inequities3. Positive nationwide averages masked geographic, gender, and socioeconomic disparities. In the health
sector, infant mortality in rural areas was twice as high as in urban environments in 2019 (16.7 versus 8.2 per 1,000
live births)4. The latest Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS, 2013-20145) at appraisal showed deep inequalities in
service delivery and health outcomes for ethnic minorities. On average, 8.6 percent of children under five had an
episode of diarrhea in the two weeks before the MICS. This was 18.5 percent among ethnic minority children.
Similarly, 15.4 percent of children in the poorest quintile had diarrhea, compared to 5.2 percent in the richest quintile.
While 1.5 percent of all children did not receive basic immunization, this was 8.2 percent among the poorest quintile
and 7.4 percent among ethnic minority households.

2.
New challenges arose that risked deepening disparities and reversing the nationwide positive trends.
Climate change emerged an existential threat to the country, with Viet Nam being the 13th-most vulnerable country
to climate risks in 20196. Viet Nam also was one of the most rapidly ageing countries. In 2019, 11.9 percent of the
population was 60 or over, expected to skyrocket to 25 percent by 20507. These factors were only two contributors
to the overall rising burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs). NCD-related hospital admissions grew from 38
percent to 66 percent between 1986 and 20158, and the share of deaths attributable to NCDs rose to 81 percent in
2019, up from 73 percent at the start of the century9. These combined challenges created new demand and additional
pressures on already stretched resources, against a backdrop of rising fiscal pressures, with debt standing at 58.3
percent of GDP in 201510.
3.
The sustainability of health financing in Viet Nam was under pressure at the time of appraisal. Public
spending on health in Viet Nam rose from US$554 million in 2000 to US$6.8 billion in 2018. Adjusted for inflation and
population growth, this represents a four-fold increase. This public spending also increased as a share of total health
expenditure over the same period, from 37 percent to 47 percent11. Out-of-pocket (OOP) expenditures made up 45
percent of expenditures, high in comparison to regional and socioeconomic peers12. While health insurance coverage
had grown to 87 percent in 2018 since its introduction in 199213, rising fees for service due to fiscal pressures proved
a major sustainability risk for the health insurance fund and, in particular, the 13 percent uninsured patients. These
financing challenges were amplified by an overreliance on hospital care, where the fee-for-service and staff income
incentives stimulated over-servicing, while primary care was bypassed – despite this being the foundation of the
grassroots health system in Viet Nam. This grassroots health system consists of District Hospitals (DHs), delivering
primary and secondary care to approximately 100,000 individuals at the district level, and Commune Health Stations
(CHSs), delivering primary care at 5,000-8,000 commune residents. CHSs also oversee village health workers, critical
for community outreach.
4.
Against this backdrop, the GSD Project was devised to increase the quality and utilization of health services
at the grassroots level. The Project, approved in June 2019 and effective in May 2020, consisted of a US$80 million
International Development Association (IDA) credit, complemented by US$25 million in grant financing from the
Global Financing Facility (GFF), the Integrating Donor-Financed Health Programs Multi-Donor Trust Fund (MDTF), and
the Tackling Non-Communicable Diseases Challenges in Low- and Middle-income Countries MDTF, as well as US$20.55
million on local Government counterpart financing, and US$0.7 million in national Government counterpart financing.
Out of the US$25 million grant financing, US$17 million from GFF was used for partial buy down of principal and
interest of the IDA transitional credit, and the MDTFs were envisaged to support specific Project activities. The GFF
resources enabled the Government of Viet Nam (GoV) to lend while in transition from concessional IDA to IDA
transitional credit, and GSD was the first type of IDA transitional credit project in any human development sector in
Viet Nam.

5.
The Project’s conceptualization started from the observation that grassroots care is not only cost-effective,
but also best suited to deliver a range of existing and emerging diagnostic, preventive, and promotive care
functions. Nonetheless, only 74.8 percent of communes had a CHS in 2016, with only 70.1 percent meeting the
infrastructural, equipment, and competency commune health benchmarks, a figure as low as 50.0 percent in rural
areas such as the remote Northern Midlands and Mountain Areas where grassroots care is more sought14. Grassroots
health workers moreover lacked the skills and incentives to deliver essential preventative, diagnostic, and
management NCD and MCH services 15 . GoV recognized these challenges and opportunities of grassroots health
systems, with the five-year health sector plan for 2016–2020 focusing on strengthening the grassroots health system
(Ministry of Health [MoH] Plan 139/KH-BYT of 2016), in addition to GoV’s Masterplan for Easing Hospital
Overcrowding (Prime Ministerial Decision 92/QD-TTg of 2013). The GSD project aligned with these plans, as well as
with Prime Ministerial Decision 2348/QD-TTg of 2016, which included plans for investment in health reforms and
infrastructure, and identified overseas development assistance as a potential source of financing. In the year before
GSD Project appraisal, a new 10-year Central Party Resolution on Health Care (Resolution No. 20 NQ/TW, October 25,
2018) emphasized that priority should be given to strengthening of the grassroots health system, referring specifically
to the role of the CHS in managing NCDs, through a new basic essential service package for health insurance
reimbursement at the commune level.
6.
The GSD Project was also closely aligned with the World Bank Group Country Partnership Framework (CPF)
for the Socialist Republic of Viet Nam for Fiscal Years (FYs) 2018-2022, in particular Objective 6 under Focus Area 2,
“Improve access to quality public and private health services and reduce malnutrition”. The Project also had to
comply with GoV lending and decentralization regulations, which mandated domestic on-lending by Provinces as
independent project implementing agencies of lending projects within their administration. Each of the 13 Project
Provinces’ Provincial People’s Committees were required to approve and sign a subsidiary loan agreement with the
Ministry of Finance (MoF) as Borrower upon Project approval. Selection criteria for Project Provinces included the
poverty rate, willingness and ability to borrow, based on debt status, tax revenue, and a provincial debt and borrowing
threshold, among others. In total, the population of the 13 project provinces was 11,780,097, accounting for 12.6
percent of the country’s population. These provinces were among the poorest provinces of the country, where the
average poverty rate was 17.9 percent, more than double the national poverty rate of 7.9 percent.
Theory of Change (Results Chain)
7.
The Project set out to improve the quality and utilization of commune-level health services through a
threefold focus on (1) the availability of CHSs that meet national standards, (2) CHSs’ readiness to deliver essential
health services, and (3) enabling this services delivery through an appropriate policy framework and guidelines as
core outputs. This design translated to the Theory of Change (ToC) in the Project Appraisal Document (PAD) as below
(figure 1). For the purpose of this Implementation Completion and Results Report (ICRR), the Project Development
Objective (PDO) and critical assumptions have been added to the PAD-stage ToC. These critical assumptions center
around the expectation that the Project outputs and outcomes would result in increased patient trust in and use of
primary care services, and the adequate role of hospitals as referral facilities in support of CHSs, rather than as
preferred destinations for primary care.
Project Development Objectives (PDOs)
8.
The PDO in the PAD and Financing Agreement is to improve the quality and utilization of grassroots health
services, with a focus on the commune level, in the Project Provinces16.

project management at the central and provincial level, including MoH staff and/or consultants for the Central Project
Management Unit (CPMU). At the provincial level, this was envisaged to include the staff and/or consultants hired to
supplement the Provincial Project Management Units (PPMUs).

A. RELEVANCE OF PDO
Assessment of Relevance of PDOs and Rating – Rated Substantial

underpinning the Project, impacted the overall utilization of CHSs – though it is not feasible to distinguish how much
of this drop in grassroots health service utilization was driven by the COVID-19 pandemic or by the policy change.

Objective 1: Improve the quality of grassroots health services with a focus on the commune level in Project Provinces Rated High
19.
One PDO-level indicator directly measures the quality objective through the ‘Percentage of CHSs in the project

primarily perceived quality through an infrastructure lens, the PDO statement does not specify this focus on ‘hard’ quality.

staff training and quality monitoring at CHS indicators, which are achieved and surpassed. A robust analysis of the diverse
quality impacts of the Project is thus prudent. The ICR applies the World Health Organization (WHO) framework for quality
of care, consisting of three dimensions22: (1) effectiveness, providing evidence-based healthcare services to patients; (2)
safety, avoiding harm; and, (3) people-centeredness, providing care that responds to individual preferences, needs and
values. Nonetheless, in the assessment of Efficacy of the quality objective, the primary focus remains on the achievement
of structural quality in line with the initial focus of the Project as designed.

of 129, and the number of renovations being substantially achieved (323 achieved versus an anticipated total of 332). This
is particularly relevant for the poorer and ethnic minority communities served by these CHSs in remote, rural Provinces.
Second, the availability and performance of skilled HRH to deliver essential services in these CHSs is a major contributor
to this effectiveness. To that end, relevant data can be drawn from the IRIs of the Results Framework, where both the
training targets as well as the equipment procurement targets to offer essential services to manage tracer conditions have
been met and surpassed. The quality scorecards implemented as part of Component 2 of the GSD Project then give an
insight into whether this training and equipment has resulted in an improvement of the quality of services. The scorecards

C. EFFICIENCY
Assessment of Efficiency and Rating – Rated Substantial
32.
The economic analysis in the PAD envisaged that improvements in quality and utilization of health services at
the grassroots level would lead to a reduction in morbidity and premature mortality in Project Provinces, in particular
among the most disadvantaged communities, while lowering the cost of care through service delivery at the less
expensive and more easily accessible CHS level. While the PAD included information on the cost of NCD treatment for
particular tracer conditions including diabetes and hypertension, no estimated Net Present Value (NPV) or Benefit-Cost
Analysis (BCA) was included. Beyond accounting for cost savings at the CHS level for prevention and management of
NCDs, accounting for associated productivity gains and the impact of improved MCH services central to the Project could
add value to this economic analysis. The ICR therefore set out to examine allocative efficiency of the Project by calculating
and comparing the NPV and BCA at Project closing, by comparing the NPV and benefit-cost ratio using targets of the
revised Results Framework (intended NPV), and those that were actually achieved at Project closing (actual NPV). Details
of this economic analysis are captured in Annex 4, Efficiency Analysis, which expands on the economic analysis at
appraisal stage by accounting for cost efficiency and productivity gains through improved NCD and MCH service delivery
at the CHS level through the GSD Project. Accounting for the assumptions and applying the formulae detailed in the
annex, the actual NPV at Project closing is US$628,051,435, while the intended NPV was US$329,947,783. Similarly, the
actual benefit-cost ratio of the Project at closing was 1.7, compared to an intended ratio of 1.2. This demonstrates the
significant economic benefit of the Project through a strengthened PHC system in Project Provinces.
33.
In addition to this significant NPV and positive benefit-cost ratio, the implementation efficiency of the Project
itself was also assessed. Annex 3 shows the envisaged cost by component at GSD Project appraisal stage, as well as the
actual disbursement of the Project at closing. At Project closing, 87 percent of the IDA credit was disbursed (US$69.7
million out of a projected US$80 million), alongside 61 percent of counterpart financing (US$12.94 million out of a
projected US$21.25 million). Grant implementation stood at 59 percent disbursement (US$4.7 million out of US$8.0
million). The remainder of IDA credit financing (US$10.4 million) was returned at closing, while part of the grant was
closed and returned during the Project period, and the remainder returned at closing. It is important to note that, with
the ODA policy prioritizing infrastructure investment, a health project was not a favorable option for GoV to borrow at
IBRD interest rates. For a lower middle-income country transitioning from IDA to IBRD eligibility, such as Viet Nam, the
GFF buy-down grant encouraged Viet Nam to invest in human development and the health sector in particular. The buydown from GFF was thus a critical enabler of the Project and offered considerable additional efficiencies for GoV.
34.
Regular mismatches between annual budget allocations and needs across 13 PPMUs were observed
throughout Project implementation, hampering equipment procurement and construction in fast-moving provinces,
with returns elsewhere in slow-moving ones. This was observed for nine out of 13 Project Provinces even in the last

III. KEY FACTORS AFFECTED IMPLEMENTATION AND OUTCOME
A. KEY FACTORS DURING PREPARATION
41.
Governance Frameworks during Preparation. The two-year time lag between concept review (July 2017) and
World Bank Board Approval of the GSD Project (June 2019), and an additional eight-month period till Project signing by
GoV, driven by lengthy Project approval processes within GoV, caused delays between inception and implementation.
This reflects the significant effort required in a fluid lending environment, whereby Viet Nam had just graduated from IDA
to IBRD on July 1, 2017, to guide the Project through the preparation process and ensure its effectiveness. An additional
driver was the institutional setup of lending in Viet Nam, whereby subnational Governments (Provinces) act as
independent Implementing Agencies. A feasibility study for each Province as well as the overall Project had to be approved
by the concerned oversight ministries. In the absence of the possibility to have an umbrella project whereby the Ministry
of Health acts as central implementing entity, each of the 13 Project Provinces’ Provincial People’s Committees were
required to approve and sign a separate subsidiary loan agreement with the Ministry of Finance (MoF) as Borrower upon
Project approval, with the CPMU housed at MoH overseeing these individual PPMUs. By doing so, the intention was to
improve the accountability and responsibility of the Provinces on the loan. The Project’s design was further impacted by
the restrictive use of ODA resources focused on priority infrastructure development. GoV Decree 132/2018/ND-CP of 2018
limited the use of ODA resources, including IDA lending, to infrastructure development for health, and, once more,
entailed lengthy Government project approval processes. This caused preparation delays and restricted the inclusion of
‘soft’ project components such as the training and capacity building as well as regulatory support under GSD Components
2 and 3 to grant-financed sources only. This created a duality between infrastructure-oriented Component 1 and
component 2.1, which used IDA resources and was owned by the GoV under its ODA regulations, and major parts of
Components 2 and 3, which were primarily MDTF grant-financed. The use of these grant resources was, however, still
required to follow outdated GoV cost norms. These norms have not been updated in the last decade, meaning that
allowances for operational costs such as transportation, venue hire, and per diems were too low to ensure training and
capacity building activities could be adequately organized. The task team had expected a change to these cost norms
during implementation, however, this did not materialize.
42.
Construction design and project management setup. The decentralized setup of the project's implementation
arrangements, in line with GoV lending regulations, meant that consultants were hired for the design of CHSs by 13
individual PPMUs, each overseeing progress in their own province. This required the establishment of 13 separate
consultancy contracts upon budget allocation and approval. This approach allowed provinces to design CHSs relevant to
their specific geographical, environmental, and cultural situations, while still adhering to the standard functional design
set by the MOH. The initial design of the CHS in each province was technically reviewed by the CPMU and WB to ensure
compliance with guidelines and standards. Given on-lending limitations, this management setup was the only feasible
option although it was not the most efficient.

Provincial workplans and budget allocations, which were both over- and underestimated by the 13 PPMUs. These
PPMUs also were found to have different implementation capacities, with high staff turnover in several PPMUs. This
contributed to the varying Project implementation progress seen across Provinces, requiring more hands-on support from
the task team and CPMU. Further delays were seen due to the administrative organizational restructuring shortly after
Project effectiveness in 2020, whereby communes were merged, or financing sources reallocated, resulting in 10 out of
13 Provinces seeking significant changes to the list of CHSs to be supported, to be approved by the provincial authorities.
The implementation process was facilitated positively by the establishment of provincial steering committees chaired by
the Vice Chairperson of the Provincial People's Committee, consisting of concerned provincial department
representatives, including the Departments of Planning and Investment, Finance, and Civil Works.
45.
M&E: As per Section IV.A, weak subnational M&E frameworks were observed throughout Project
implementation. The World Bank and CPMU pursued several mitigating actions during implementation to mediate
associated challenges, including the provision of M&E training for PPMUs.
46.
Fiduciary: The PAD articulated the substantial FM risks due to the high level of decentralization and delayed
budget allocations in poorer provinces, with World Bank pursuing several effective mitigation strategies. This included
the need for designated accounts at the Provincial level with commercial banks satisfactory to the World Bank, as seen
across the Viet Nam portfolio. While improvements were seen over time during implementation, project internal audit
arrangements were set up as another mitigation measure. The need for enhanced FM scrutiny posed a significant burden
to the task team, including for expenditure claims and results monitoring. Compounding these challenges, FM challenges
in the health sector in 2022 indirectly affected Project implementation. While the GSD Project was not directly linked to
or involved in these challenges, this resulted in some hesitancy of Project and PPMU staff at the subnational level to
accelerate Project implementation, in particular procurement, while Provincial health staff was occupied with responding
to requests from ongoing inspections.
47.
Adequacy of grant financing structures. The project was co-financed by three grants: two from the abovementioned MDTFs, as well as GFF. These grants provided complementary support to the project. The MDTFs funded core
activities, while the GFF grant reduced interest rates of borrowing. Regrettably, due to stringent financial management
rules which limited the eligible expenditure categories for the grants and constrained cost norms, over 40 percent of MDTF
resources were unspent and were returned. The GFF buy-down grant was not tied to IDA disbursement rates, leading to
its full use. The share of interest bought down through the buy-down option was therefore higher than originally expected,
as the Project did not fully utilize the IDA credit.
Factors subject to World Bank control
48.
Adequacy of supervision: Despite multiple challenges, the supervision of the GSD Project showcased significant
resilience and adaptability. Although there were delays in the implementation of core Project activities, the collaboration
between PPMUs and the CPMU was enhanced, and the timely delivery of training by the World Bank was encouraged.
Proposed remedies during the MTR focused on accelerating reviews of Provincial plans and improving training efficiency.
Although project restructurings mainly targeted changes to the Results Framework, a more comprehensive
reconsideration of Project activities could have further strengthened health systems and service delivery quality. Rigid
ODA management and anti-corruption measures in place in Viet Nam constrained the efforts to support subnational data
collection, M&E capacity building, and additional support to trailing Project Provinces. The task team clarified during the
ICR process that different approaches for better supporting and realigning the project activities were considered but not

Implementation support from Australia’s Department for Foreign Affairs and Trade (DFAT) and GFF contributed

developed and available on the learning platform of the Hanoi Medical University for wider access.
Factors outside the control of government and implementing entities
50.
Public health crises: The outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic caused severe disruption to health service delivery
as well as delayed the start of Project implementation. Having become effective in May 2020, as the scale and magnitude
of the pandemic became apparent and most of project staff who were also Government civil health servants were
mobilized for pandemic response, the start of construction activities, training, policy, and Project management
engagement was understandably delayed. Major health care demand and supply challenges were observed affecting the
delivery of care to patients, which the World Bank team sought to address through Results Framework restructurings.
51.
Natural disasters: The PAD refers to climate resilience as a consideration in the construction and rehabilitation
of grassroots health facility infrastructure in line with climate risks, yet unprecedented extreme events posed equally
unprecedented challenges to infrastructure. This became particularly visible in the wake of Typhoon Yagi. This typhoon
hit Viet Nam and other Southeast Asian countries severely in September 2024, causing extensive damage to several CHSs’
infrastructure supported by the GSD Project, in particular in northern provinces home to a large share of ethnic minorities
and vulnerable communities the Project sought to serve, as visited during the ICR mission. GoV opted not to use the
balance of Project funds to support the restoration effort in the wake of the typhoon, instead preferring to employ
domestic resources and, where possible, insurance coverage of facilities to cover the cost of damage incurred.
IV. BANK PERFORMANCE, COMPLIANCE ISSUES, AND RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME
A. QUALITY OF MONITORING AND EVALUATION (M&E) – Rated Modest
M&E Design
52.
The GSD Project’s ToC was appropriate and the choice for simple indicators measurable with minimal new data
collection was suitable given the challenging Project implementation context. The GSD PAD noted in the Lessons Learned
section the M&E challenges observed, including regarding data collection, in two operations completed in 2016, and called
for action to ensure M&E systems and reporting arrangements were in place well before Project effectiveness. Data
constraints limited the selection of indicators, especially those beyond existing HMISs, in the Results Framework. As such,
the selection of readily available data and indicators that were part of standard reporting by MoH and facilities was wellconsidered, especially as cost norms and ODA funds use restrictions limited the inclusion of additional, project-specific
indicators and surveys. The Project also faced challenges on the accuracy, completeness and timeliness of Government
data, as across the Viet Nam portfolio – with the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2) system not established until
2021 and a learning curve on its use seen until Project closing, and the reliance on paper-based data sources prior.

Additional support for M&E strengthening was provided by GFF, yet given this capacity building is a lengthy process,

M&E Implementation
53.
The WB team routinely updated progress toward project indicators and tried to manage reporting issues during
implementation. The task team made efforts to regularly cross-validate the accuracy of the reported data, which was a
critical effort as responsible PPMUs had vastly differing data collection, analysis, and monitoring capacities. The task team
continued to adequately monitor and report on shortcomings in the scattered subnational M&E capacity in consecutive
AMs and ISRs throughout Project implementation. A criterion to evaluate the M&E capacity (accuracy and timeliness) of
the PPMUs was also added to assess the PMU’s performance during each Implementation Support Mission, with training
on M&E provided to all PPMUs at the start of the Project and continued throughout the Project implementation. While
the task team therefore made important efforts to ensure quality data were collected, reported, and analyzed to monitor
Project progress, PPMUs continued to differ in their capacities in data collection, analysis, and monitoring so that the quality
and accuracy of data reported by PPMUs remained variable.
54.
The Results Framework was revised twice to reflect the changes in the project implementation. These
restructurings were timely and constituted an opportunity to comprehensively update Project activities and indicators to
align the M&E mechanisms of the operation with the policy and sectoral environment changes over the Project lifetime. The
Bank team consistently emphasized to the Government the importance of making more fundamental changes to the
project development objective and results framework particularly in the context of significant reduction in utilization rate
of CHS due to COVID-19 situation. These changes would have necessitated amendments to the project’s Financing
Agreement and investment documents. However, the proposed revisions were not materialized by the CPMU and PMUs
due to the complexity and time required to update 14 separate project documents (covering the Ministry of Health and 13
provinces). On the Government’s side, this process would have involved a full cycle of review and approval at the Prime
Minister and President level—an effort that would take enormous time to pursue and could distract the project from
focusing of enhancing implementation progress. Therefore, M&E challenges could not be fully addressed to create more
confidence in data collection, quality, and analysis capacities in project management units.
M&E Utilization
55.
The project indicators were reviewed by the task team on every implementation support mission and were
utilized for resolving implementation bottlenecks and steering the project towards its goals. Despite the shortcomings in
the design and implementation of M&E, the Project’s M&E system was still adequate to assess whether the planned
activities were carried out and whether the project was on track in line with Project targeted outcomes, provided a sound
basis for the discussions between MoH and PPMUs on project implementation and changes to be made. The data utilized
also constituted the best and only available data for the M&E of Project progress and were optimally employed to the
benefit of the M&E in accordance with the results framework.
Justification of Overall Rating of Quality of M&E
56.
The overall quality of M&E is rated Modest. The Project’s M&E framework was adequate to monitor Project
progress. However, there were significant shortcomings in the design and implementation of the M&E system.

environmental and social impacts. In the 4th ISR in April 2021, the Safeguards rating was reduced from Satisfactory to

Fiduciary (FM and Procurement): The project had an adequate project FM system that provided, with reasonable

Loan Agreement. No significant FM issues were noted throughout implementation and FM-related weaknesses raised
during FM supervision were resolved on time. The project audit reports were all with unqualified audit opinions. In
addition, the withdrawal procedure and funds flow arrangement were appropriate. Issues with budget allocation from

there were no major problems for procured packages by both regular review during ISMs and annual procurement postreviews in select PMUs.
59.
The fiduciary SORT risks rating at appraisal was substantial, due to prior World Bank experience with delayed
procurement-related progress and possible integrity concerns, as well as the decentralization of the Project
implementation and management. From the 4th ISR in April 2021, the progress rating for both FM and procurement was
downgraded to moderately satisfactory, due to delays in the launch and execution of procurement contracts for the health
facilities and consultancy services, while infrequent inefficiencies were observed in contract management practices
warranting substantive review by FM specialists. The SORT rating for fiduciary risk was finally reduced from Substantial to
Moderate in May 2023 at the time of the 8th ISR. The limited procurement capacity of subnational staff and PPMUs did
warrant the continued close monitoring of contract implementation and eligible expenditures as per the FM assessments.
On equipment procurement specifically, delays were observed due to the review of equipment during the COVID-19
pandemic, where multiple devices were provided through alternative resources, and the need for rebidding of packages
due to supply interruptions because of the pandemic and anti-corruption campaign.
C. BANK PERFORMANCE
Quality at Entry – Rated Satisfactory
60.
The GSD Project was highly relevant at entry. The team was able to design a project in line with GoV PHC priorities
while placing equity and efficiency at its heart, also reflected in the choice of Project locations. As such, the team was very
effective in ensuring a pro-poor design based on careful mapping of socioeconomically disadvantaged districts within each
province which served as the starting point in the selection of Project Provinces and sites. This achievement of a strong
equity-focused PHC Project is remarkable against a backdrop of the on-lending requirements with Provincial ownership of
project design and implementation, the IBRD transition of Viet Nam and new ODA regulations. The grant resources
obtained by the team were critical as an enabler of the Project, in particular (i) the GFF buy-down grant, allowing the IBRD
interest rate of the loan to be bought down to a sufficiently concessional level for the loan to be acceptable to MoF, and
(ii) the MDTFs to cover the ‘soft’ elements constrained by the ODA regulation, as mentioned previously. The task team’s
success and strength of design were evidenced by the fact that the GSD Project was the first IPF to be approved following
Viet Nam’s graduation from IDA, as only IPF project in Viet Nam in that year.

The task team achieved considerable in-depth engagement with Project Provinces during preparation. In a new

Satisfactory considerations of poverty, gender, environmental and social safeguards, and social development

therefore carried out unparalleled amounts of subnational stakeholder engagement during preparation.
64.
While the risk assessment at design stage was thus performed thoroughly, the implementation arrangements
with 13 PPMUs remained challenging. As many PPMUs implemented a WB financed project for the first time, their
capacity needed to be strengthened as early as possible. This was built into the project design through the mobilized grant
funding. This was also critical as domestic on-lending regulations mandated institutional and implementation
arrangements led by Provinces through independent PPMUs which needed substantial support. Each PPMU was therefore
to have their own consultancy support, facility design, M&E, and fiduciary functions, and additional CPMU staff were hired
to mitigate this risk and training of trainers foreseen to build capacity at the province level. In retrospect, as the Lessons
Learned section in the PAD stipulates activities are to be designed to be independent of policy changes, possibly more
efforts could have been made prior to the project start to work with GoV to ease the restrictive cost norms on the use of
grant financing, which would have enabled the full use of acquired grant funding for capacity building.
65.
As per the Lessons Learned, the Bank team designed the Results Framework to use existing information from
the HMIS, aware of existing M&E constraints. The choice for simple indicators was appropriate, as was the planned
collection of data from HMIS. The team also included the implementation of feedback beneficiary surveys, which was an
additional important source of information on the quality dimension. The Bank worked within existing limitations and
designed an M&E system which the Bank was aware needed improvements during implementation.
Quality of Supervision – Rated Moderately Satisfactory
66.
Adequacy of supervision inputs and processes: The task team is to be commended for its hands-on supervision
inputs provided to each of the 13 Project Provinces throughout implementation. In every mission, the Bank task team
either had field trips to the provinces or had a review meeting with each of the PPMUs online to thoroughly review the
project progress and identify any bottlenecks in implementation for immediate actions. Each AM had an Annex with
detailed progress and actions needed for each PPMU, which allowed the team to closely monitor the implementation and
timely evaluate the performance by each PMU in every six months and recommend time-bound actions for improvement.
The implementation arrangements, with 13 implementing agencies each requiring support and with separate project M&E
and TA requirements, were a herculean task for the team. The acknowledgment of MoH and by the CPMU and the PPMUs
interviewed by the ICR team of the Bank team’s responsiveness and proactivity is laudable and went a long way to
overcome the considerable complexities of such decentralized project implementation arrangements.

The team also proactively undertook restructurings of the Project, including the updates to the Results
Framework and the extension of the closing dates of the supporting grants, which were timely in light of the fluid sectoral
context. These restructurings resulted in a partial improvement of progress towards meeting the intended Project
outcomes and objectives. For others, progress remained more limited. It is not clear why certain targets were revised

procurement and implementation arrangements and deepening the learning from the experience of the fast-moving
successful provinces. Similarly, activities that were unlikely to make meaningful progress before Project closing, such as
the policy component, could have been considered for cancellation or restructuring, though also here, a more
comprehensive restructuring was not possible in light of the lengthy administrative process and limited bandwidth of GoV
counterparts in particular during the COVID-19 period.

69.
The task team prepared a satisfactory operation and reflected major lessons learned from prior engagements
during preparation and tried to overcome the considerable challenges related to the complex implementation
arrangements during supervision. These challenges were severely exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic and policy
shifts. On balance, the overall Bank performance during preparation is assessed as Moderately Satisfactory. Performance
during implementation is rated as Moderately Satisfactory due to the issues described above, most serious of which is the
lost opportunity to make more effective revisions to the project design and the Results Framework in a timely manner (e.g.,
in 2021 post-GoV policy change) to ensure better alignment with the prevailing policy context and available alarming data
on utilization, among other indicators, recognizing the highly challenges context in which such revisions would need to take
place and the constraints detailed in paragraph 54..

V. LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
71.
The GSD Project delivers important lessons learned and recommendations that can inform future World Bankfinanced operations, especially those being implemented in devolved and decentralized health sector contexts with
varying project management and implementation capacity at the subnational level, as well as those country contexts
where restrictions around the use of loan or credit proceeds limit non-infrastructure-related activities.
72.
Enabling environment to facilitate decentralization: Decentralizing health sector management to empower
subnational authorities as Project implementers could be well-suited to the context of Viet Nam, enhancing transparency
and accountability in the use of provincial resources. To support the effective implementation of decentralization policies,
it is essential to strengthen financial management mechanisms and improve ODA governance, ensuring that subnational
entities are equipped with the authority and capacity to make timely and effective decisions. Future World Bank
operations in Viet Nam should also consider focusing on a smaller number of provinces to enable more targeted support
and streamlined implementation.
73.
When faced with significant policy and sectoral fluidity, ensure operational agility: During implementation, the
introduction of the policy that allowed to bypass primary care, the occurrence of an unexpected shock in the form of the
COVID-19 pandemic, and the slow disbursement of grant resources could have been an impetus to reset ambitions and
set renewed, more modest, yet still impactful objectives through substantive PDO and Project restructuring.
74.
Improving M&E capacity: The GSD Project highlights the importance of strengthening M&E capacity at the
provincial level. Effective monitoring and evaluation are crucial not only for tracking project progress but also for informing
policy decisions and enhancing transparency. The support from the GFF in improving the M&E and use of data capacities
for local health staff is highly valued, as it has substantially contributed to establishing more robust M&E frameworks.
However, more time and efforts beyond the project life and further awareness enhancement and investment are needed
to substantively address the observed M&E shortcomings. To ensure sustainable M&E practices in future projects, ongoing
training and resource allocation are paramount.
75.
Harness the power of innovative financing structures to the benefit of clients: The GFF buy-down was a critical
enabler of IDA transitional lending with full IBRD terms for health in Viet Nam. Such opportunities should be pursued for
client countries that face lending or capacity constraints, or face fluid lending environments, such as Viet Nam during its
IDA-IBRD transition.
76.
A key lesson learned for project sustainability is the pivotal role of the Balanced Scorecard (BSC) in
implementing effective interventions. By enhancing service quality at Community Health Services (CHS), the BSC has
demonstrated its value, leading provinces to independently expand its use with their own budgets. This highlights the
importance of investing in frameworks like the BSC to ensure long-term success and continuous improvement in service
delivery.

ANNEX 4. EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS
A4.1 The economic analysis at appraisal stage in the GSD PAD focused on cost savings from disease prevention, early
detection, and management of NCDs at CHS level, yet did not account for MCH services – critical to the Project – nor the
productivity gains associated with these improved services through the Project. The economic analysis at ICR stage
therefore seeks to provide a post-hoc insight in these critical areas and examines the allocative efficiency of the Project by
comparing the NPV and benefit-cost ratio using patient targets in the restructured Results Framework (intended NPV) with
the NPV and benefit-cost ratio using actual patients reached (actual NPV) at Project closing. Please note that both the
intended number of beneficiaries as per the Results Framework targets (intended) and numbers of beneficiaries included
as reached in the Results Framework at Project closing (actual) are assumed to have directly benefited from the
interventions of the GSD Project. The economic analysis at ICR stage follows the rationale of the appraisal-stage economic
analysis in its consideration that strengthening PHC will result in significant efficiencies in the health system. At PCH level
in lower-middle income countries like Viet Nam, expanding essential MCH services alone has the potential to achieve a
benefit-cost ratio of 11.329.
A4.2 To calculate the negative cashflow, in first instance, the cost for facility construction, rehabilitation, and
operation is calculated for the five active years of the GSD Project period, along with the necessary HRH training,
equipment, and other quality improvement measures. To that end, the actual Project disbursements of the IDA credit and
trust fund grants were used by year. Negative cashflow is also estimated for the five years following Project closure in the
NPV assessment. The projected annual cost for CHS operation for years 6 to 10 is therefore used to project the future CHS
maintenance and management cost upon Project closing. In 2015, mean annual CHS operation cost was estimated at 1,536
million Vietnamese Dollars, equivalent to US$70,165 at the time30. With an average inflation rate of 2.7 percent between
2015 and 202331, this cost is estimated at US$89,177 annually at Project closing. Therefore, this ICR uses an average annual
CHS operational cost of US$89,177 per CHS in 2024 as its baseline, with a 2.7 percent average projected inflation for the
2025-2029 period to calculate future CHS maintenance and management costs beyond the Project period. The total
maintenance and management cost beyond Project period is then the annual CHS operational cost times the number of
CHSs supported by the Project, standing at 424 at Project closing (versus a targeted 461) and held constant for the five
subsequent years. A discount rate of 2% is applied, the average 5-year Government bond as of August 2024.
A4.3 To calculate the positive cashflow, for the intended NPV, the annual targeted annual number of people who
received essential health, nutrition, and population services as per the restructured Results Framework was used, while
for actual NPV, the recorded annual number of people who received essential health, nutrition, and population services
was used. As per the Results Framework, these latter data were provided by the PPMUs in Project Provinces based on their
administrative records and included essential NCD and MCH services. Two major aspects are considered to contribute to
the economic benefit of the GSD Project in the NPV calculation: (1) the cost saved by beneficiaries making use of grassroots
health services at the CHS level instead of higher-level, more costly referral health services, and (2) the increase in
productivity of beneficiaries of CHS services through their improved health resulting from the essential health services
received. To compute the former, the costs saved by patients seeking care at the CHS level, the cost differential between
an outpatient visit at the CHS level and an outpatient visit at the hospital level in Viet Nam was used. In 2014, a CHS

ANNEX 5. BORROWER, CO-FINANCIER AND OTHER PARTNER/STAKEHOLDER COMMENTS
Comments received from the Ministry of Health
Firstly, we extend our sincere gratitude to the World Bank for sharing the draft of the Implementation Completion and
Results Report (ICR) for the GSD Project. We affirm that this project has been successful in numerous aspects, including the
development of new models that positively impact healthcare services within the primary health system, transformation
of the infrastructure landscape and address equipment shortages at the grassroots level, particularly for noncommunicable diseases. The project effectively utilized loan and counterpart funds, adhered to timelines despite various
challenges over the five-year period, and complied strictly with both World Bank and government regulations, with no
errors during implementation. The Ministry of Health and project provinces have clearly articulated the project's successes
and lessons in the end-of-project evaluation report, completed in May 2025 for presentation to the Vietnamese
Government and relevant ministries and agencies.
After a thorough review of the draft ICR report, we have attached feedback to clarify certain contents and propose
adjustments and additions to better reflect the practical context, implementation methods, and significant results and
lessons from the project.
The ICR was meticulously prepared with in-depth analyses, covering many critical aspects of the GSD Project. It accurately
reflects the implementation process and results, while highlighting valuable lessons for future interventions. The GSD
Project was executed in a unique context, facing more challenges than any previous health sector support project. While
the report identifies these challenges, they should be highlighted alongside development indicators and intermediate
results to provide a comprehensive picture. The project evaluation should consider the same context and challenges as
other projects to acknowledge the achieved results and the stakeholders' efforts in implementation.
We acknowledge that the "service utilization" indicator not meeting expectations might affect the project's effectiveness
evaluation. However, relying solely on this indicator may not fully capture the project's impact. The goal of increasing
service utilization at commune health stations is a long-term outcome, influenced by factors such as infrastructure,
personnel capacity, quality management, disease trends, related policies, and crucially, the intervention time factor. Many
components, such as upgrading commune health stations, were completed towards the project's end or were delayed by
the COVID-19 pandemic, necessitating more time to assess effectiveness.
Additionally, the report would benefit from including training—one of the major intervention areas that directly
contributed to improving service quality and utilization. This should encompass the implementation methods, challenges,
and results of training activities within the project's scope.
We are also concerned about the evaluation of M&E and the World Bank team's performance during the implementation
stage in the ICR report. While we agree with the assessment of early-stage M&E system weaknesses, we affirm that project
staff made significant efforts to enhance data collection, validation, reporting, usage, and in the context where the common
reporting information system of the entire health sector is currently facing many difficulties. M&E capacity improved
considerably over the five years, evidenced by more complete, quality data and timely reports in the latter years.
The Ministry of Health and project provinces' leadership highly appreciate the technical guidance and project management
of the World Bank team. Unlike previous projects with a single focal point, the World Bank team managed 14 focal points,
effectively supervising as if managing 14 projects. Although some activities could have been improved, we propose
reconsidering the unsatisfactory rating (MU) of the World Bank team, as this assessment differs from the evaluation by the
Ministry of Health and project provinces.
This feedback from the Ministry of Health/Central Project Management Project aims to enhance the ICR of the World Bank.
Thank you for considering these comments to further improve the report.
