 I.        PROGRAM CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES





A. Context at Appraisal



      Context





      1. In the thirty years leading up to the Climate Change and Green Growth Development Policy Financing (DPF)

         operation (P171006), rapid and inclusive economic growth had raised Vietnam’s status to that of a lower-

         middle-income country. Gross domestic product (GDP) growth per capita averaged 5.5 percent a year from

         1990 to 2017, yielding a per capita gross national income (GNI) of US$2,160 in 2017. Growth was inclusive:

         incomes rose across the income distribution, with no growth in inequality, and the percentage of people living

         in extreme poverty (US$1.9 per day) stood at less than 3 percent. Key social indicators – education, life

         expectancy, maternal mortality, and access to basic infrastructure – improved substantially, and were better

         than most countries at a similar income level.



      2. However, Vietnam was, and still is, one of the world’s most vulnerable countries to climate change impacts,

         and the intense exploitation of natural resources and environmental degradation linked to its rapid

         economic growth exacerbates its vulnerability. Droughts, salinization, extreme temperatures, and changes in

         growing seasons and floods1, compounded by sub-standard housing in exposed areas and a lack of assets to

         buffer shocks2, strongly impact the livelihoods of poor rural and urban households. Over the 25 years preceding

         the operation, extreme weather events resulted in 0.4 to 1.7 percent of GDP loss.3 By 2050, national income is

         likely to be reduced by between one and two percent compared to a historical baseline.4 Depletion and

         degradation of natural resources has increased the impact of climate change on those who depend on natural

         ecosystems for direct livelihoods and to moderate the impacts of hydrological extremes. In the late 2000s,

         when growth was peaking, natural resource depletion was in the order of 7 percent of GNI. 5 Utilization of land

         intensified, water resources were stretched, forests were being unsustainably logged, and capture fisheries

         and mineral resources were being depleted. Impacts were typically most severe on the poor: 30 percent of the

         poor population at the time of the operation was exposed to a historical 25-year flood, but this had been

         estimated to increase to over 50 percent with climate change impacts.6



      3. Vietnam’s greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions have been moderate in absolute terms but are disproportionate

         to the size of its growing economy. From 2000 to 2015, carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions nearly quadrupled,

         following faster relative growth than other East Asian countries. Vietnam was the 13th most carbon intensive

         economy in the world and the 4th amongst low and middle-income countries in East Asia (vs. 33rd and 5th

 4. The macroeconomic policy framework was deemed adequate for this operation. Economic growth was

    resilient and macroeconomic stability had been broadly maintained. The monetary and fiscal policy stance

    were considered appropriate and external buffers were robust. The impact of COVID 19 on Vietnam’s economic

    activities was expected to be significant but short-lived if the outbreak was contained in the next few months

    following the operation. The strong fundamentals of the Vietnamese economy, with a large current account

    surplus and relatively ample fiscal space at the end of 2019, offered the opportunity for the government to

    implement a fiscal stimulus that was expected to help compensate for the negative effects of the pandemic on

    the most vulnerable groups. The resulting temporary increase in fiscal deficit was expected to be

    accommodated by additional borrowing that would not affect fiscal and debt sustainability. Other short-term

    risks envisaged included a sharp slowdown in global economic activity and trade flows as Vietnam’s economy

    was one of the most open in the world. Vietnam had been managing the above external risks by diversifying

    its trade flows and improving its competitiveness. Vietnam’s adhesion to new trade agreements, e.g. the

    Europe-Vietnam Free Trade Agreement, supported this effort. Heightened global volatility underscored the

    need to maintain sound macroeconomic policies, including implementation of planned structural reforms, such

    as restructuring the state-owned enterprises, improving investment climate and investing in human capital and

    infrastructure.



 5. The World Bank aimed to support the Government of Vietnam to address its climate change and green

    growth challenges with the delivery of this operation, which was closely linked to a 2016 operation that was

    designed to be the first in a 3-operation series. The originally conceived 3-operation World Bank DPF series

    focused on mainstreaming climate within general planning and budgeting processes, as well as tackling specific

    climate impacts and issues in key sectors. The series supported the government’s Support Program to Respond

    to Climate Change (SP-RCC) 2016-2020, which facilitated policy reforms for effective implementation of climate

    change and green growth actions prioritized in the 2016–2020 Socio-Economic Development Plan (SEDP),

    National Climate Change Strategy (NCCS) and Vietnam Green Growth Strategy (VGGS), and Nationally

    Determined Contribution (NDC). The original series was expected to run from 2016 to 2019, but following

    approval of the first operation in June 2016, processing of the second operation was delayed beyond the 24

    months due to the public debt ceiling and constraints on government borrowing. It was then dropped, and this

    single, one-tranche operation was developed based on the policy dialogue under the originally planned 3-

    operation series.8 An Implementation Completion and Results Report (ICR) was prepared for that first

    operation. Although unable to proceed as planned, the first operation of the preceding DPF series played a

    critical role in shaping the form and content of the SP-RCC policy program and delivered results upon which

 6. This operation was aligned with the priorities articulated in the WBG Country Partnership Framework (CPF)

    Resilience). The CPF was fully aligned with the government’s 2010–2020 Socio-Economic Development Strategy

 7. This DPF operation supported selected and significant elements of the SP-RCC. The areas of policy reform

    were selected to capture the greatest opportunities for transformational interventions, and synergies

    between the climate change and green growth agendas of the NCCS and VGGS 9, building on priorities

    identified jointly by the government and development partners (DP) through the SP-RCC dialogue. They were

    based on a variety of analytical work undertaken by the Government, the Bank and DPs. This operation aimed

    to enhance the momentum of the originally planned series, ensuring continued delivery of the latter stages of

    the SP-RCC, and extending the policy ambition and delivery of results beyond those envisaged for the original

    series in key areas. This included supporting policies that aimed to lead to increased protection of water

    sources, application of water-saving practices over larger areas of farmland, additional reductions in air

    pollutants from vehicles, and further scale up of renewable generation capacity.

      Pillar 1: Climate Resilient Management of Landscapes



 10. This pillar supported the government’s efforts to adapt to climate change through increasing the resilient

     management of landscapes and natural resources, including water resources, as well as to more effectively

     manage public investment in resilience and green growth. The reforms under this pillar included support

     under three policy tracks: (i) Climate Smart Landscape Planning; (ii) Climate Budgeting; and (iii) Improving

     Water Resources Protection and Use Efficiency.

 11. Climate Smart Landscape Planning: In support of this policy track, this operation supported policies to

     improve forest landscape management and strengthen resilient planning of the Mekong Delta 10. For the

     former, further enhancement of legal and institutional measures for forest protection, restoration and

     management was considered critical for ensuring the sector’s sustainability and contribution to climate change

     adaptation and mitigation, especially in critical and highly vulnerable areas. For the latter, formulation and

     strengthening of the legal framework for spatial planning was fundamental to enhancing climate-resilient

     management of the Mekong Delta. Prior to the 2017 Planning Law, planning exercises in Vietnam were largely

     fragmented, sectoral-based and target-oriented, leading to inconsistencies, overlaps and conflicts, and

     inefficient investments.



 12. Climate Budgeting: Establishing the foundation for climate budgeting was considered pivotal to monitoring

     public financing for investments that enhance climate resilience. Climate budget tagging enables a stronger

     understanding of the composition and levels of public expenditures with climate benefits, which provides

     important information to policymakers to assist in monitoring climate policy implementation.



 13. Improving Water Resources Protection and Use Efficiency: Establishing the institutional and governance

     framework for water resources protection was considered instrumental to ensuring sustainability and security

     of water resources especially in the face of increasing climate change impacts. Further, providing incentives

     for more efficient irrigation systems was pivotal to addressing climate-induced and exacerbated shortages of

     water resources for the agricultural sector and the economy. Improved irrigation practices also bring economic

     benefits to farmers through improvements to farmers’ incomes.



       Pillar 2: Adoption of Cleaner Transport and Energy Systems



 14. Pillar 2 supported the government’s efforts to shift towards greener growth through cleaner transport and

     energy systems to reduce GHG emissions and address air pollution. The reforms under this pillar included

     support under two policy tracks: (i) Cleaner Transport; and (ii) Towards a Low-Carbon Energy Transition.



 15. Cleaner Transport: Improving automobile emissions standards is important for reducing both air pollution and

     greenhouse gas emissions associated from road transport. In 2019, transport accounted for about 10 percent

     of the country’s GHG emissions.11 Further, about 25 percent of Particulate Matter 2.5 (PM2.5) concentrations

     in Hanoi originates from the transport sector.12 Recognizing the pollution associated with road transport, the

     Ministry of Transport (MOT) had previously adopted EURO-4 equivalent13 vehicle emissions standards for new

     vehicles14 but emissions from in-use vehicles and used import vehicles remained unregulated. This policy track

     aimed to address this policy gap.

A. Relevance of prior actions



   Rating: Satisfactory



  17. The design of the PDO aligned with the World Bank Group’s CPF for Vietnam (FY18-22), notably focus area 3,

      which sought to ensure environmental sustainability and resilience. The PDO was also fully relevant with the

      government’s 2010-2020 SEDS and 2016-2020 SEDP, which underscored the need for strengthening resilience

 21. Climate Smart Landscape Planning: Improved Forest Landscape Management (Prior Action 1). The first prior

     action – the adoption of criteria for the classification of coastal protection forests and the development of

     requirements for sustainable forest management plans - was appropriate and relevant in achieving the

     development objective. The prior action for this reform area focused on coastal protection forests for their

     crucial contribution to climate resilience of the vulnerable coastal landscape of Vietnam, and the formulation

     of sustainable forest development plans. Specifically, a decree was issued on classification of coastal protection

     forests and respective management measures (Decree 156/2018/ND-CP dated November 16, 2018 on “Specific

     regulations to implement the law on forestry”) followed by a MARD circular (Circular No 28/2018/TT-BNNPTNT

     dated November 16, 2018 on “Guidance on sustainable forest management) guiding the development of

     management plans in accordance with the forest’s classification and in compliance with national sustainable

     forest certification systems. It provided a framework for expanding the coastal forest belt with different

     functions of protection for enhanced resilience and measures to properly manage the forests including aligning

     the revenue-generating permission with the functional category. The sustainable forest development plans

     formulated were expected to ensure accountability of forest owners, facilitate actions and transparency in

     rehabilitation, protection and conservation of forest resources, and support sustainable financing including

     payment for forest environmental services.



 22. Climate Smart Landscape Planning: Strengthened Resilient Planning of Mekong Delta (Prior Action 2). The

     second prior action – adoption of guidance for implementing the Planning Law, including process, content, and

     climate considerations for preparation of regional master plans and establishment of National Planning Council

     tasked with the responsibility of developing regional master plans - was appropriate and relevant in achieving

     the development objective. The effectiveness of the Planning Law and the subsequent issuance of Decree No.

     37/2019/ND-CP dated May 7, 2019 on “Regulations to implement the Planning Law” provided sufficient legal

     basis and technical guidance for spatial and integrated planning, and enabled the formulation of the first-ever

     regional master plan for the Mekong Delta region. It was also the first regional master plan that was formulated

     in the country. Decree 37 guided the process for developing master plans and the content required to be

     included in the master plans, including mainstreaming of climate change. Prime Minister’s Decision No.

     1226/QD-TTg dated September 24, 2018 on “Establishing the National Planning Committee” contributed to

     ensuring the engagement of all relevant agencies in the planning and planning appraisal processes. As a result

     of these policies, the Mekong Delta regional master plan (with a strong focus on climate resilience and

     sustainability) was formulated and approved by the Prime Minister in February 2022.



 23. Climate Budgeting (Prior Action 3): The third prior action – adoption of guidance for the identification,

     classification, and reporting of public investment allocations related to climate change and green growth - was

     appropriate and relevant in achieving the development objective. It was intended to provide an internationally

     recognized methodology to assess the overall level of public climate investment, as well as the alignment of

     that investment with national climate and green growth objectives. It was expected that MPI Decision No.

     1085/QD-BKHDT dated July 16, 2018 would be rolled out progressively starting in selected priority sectors.

     Following assessments in around 30 provinces in the Mekong, coastal and upland areas, MARD was targeted

     as the first ministry that would apply the budget tagging methodology, as it accounted for a large share of

     central budget climate-related investment. The MPI Decision aimed to put in place a consistent methodology

     for tracking climate budget public expenditures to help provide insight on the level and composition of

     expenditure towards adaptation and resilience, climate change mitigation and in support of NDC, National

     Climate Change Strategy and Green Growth Strategy policy objectives. This is especially important for assessing

     progress on investments in climate smart landscapes, which have significant levels of support from the state

     budget. The prior action, therefore, was appropriate and relevant to establish the foundation for a climate

     budgeting system which would lead to the goal of promoting climate resilient landscapes.

 24. Improving Water Resources Protection and Use Efficiency - Water Source Protection (Prior Action 4): The fourth

     prior action – regulations on groundwater protection to prevent saline intrusion and land subsidence and

     regulations to improve sand mining management and river works planning to protect water sources - was

     appropriate and relevant in achieving the development objective. The GoV’s commitment to strengthening

     surface and groundwater protection as a key part of its climate program was materialized in Decree No.

     167/2018/ND-CP dated December 26, 2018 on “Regulations on restriction to underground water exploitation”

     to minimize groundwater exploitation and Decree No. 23/2020/ND-CP dated February 24, 2020 on

     “Management of river sand and river protection”. Both decrees seek to impose restrictions on public and

     private entities to protect important resources from further depletion and decentralize the responsibilities of

     planning and management to the sub-national level. Following the issuance of the decrees, selected provinces

     have started to develop their plans and action plans for implementation. Decree 167 established categories for

     groundwater protection (i.e., four different groundwater protection zones) and the respective levels of

     restrictions to exploitation and management measures. For river sand and river management, similarly, there

     was clear guidance on zoning and protection measures in Decree 23. While MONRE continued to be responsible

     for overall framework planning and oversight, the responsibility for groundwater and sand management was

     clearly assigned to the provincial authorities, thereby increasing the accountability and contributing to

     enhancing enforcement on the ground. The prior action was therefore appropriate and relevant to contribute

     to Vietnam’s climate resilient management of landscapes.



 25. Improving Water Resources Protection and Use Efficiency - Water Use Efficiency (Prior Action 5): The fifth prior

     action – financial incentives for on-farm irrigation and advanced and efficient irrigation systems as well as

     guidelines for water service fees for irrigation - was appropriate and relevant in achieving the development

     objective. The GoV, since 2018, has adopted the two Decrees focusing on using financial instruments as

     incentives for better water saving practices (Decree No. 77/2018/ND-CP dated May 16, 2018 on “Regulations

     to support the development of small-scale irrigation works, in-field irrigation works and water saving

     irrigation”) and measures for increased irrigation efficiency and water productivity (Decree No. 96/2018/ND-

     CP dated June 30, 2018 on “Regulations on fee of irrigation goods and services, and financial support to using

     public irrigation goods and services”). These policies were the continuation of the measures supported under

     the water resources track of the original DPF series, contributing to further enhancement of the legal and

     institutional framework for water resources management in the agricultural sector and climate-resilient

     agricultural landscape management. Decree 77 provided clear guidance on the different incentives (both in

     terms of enabling infrastructure and financial support) for efficient/water-saving irrigation systems. The roles

     of MARD include technical support and issuance of technical standards/guidelines, while the provincial

     authorities are responsible for specific mechanisms, implementation programs and budgeting. Decree 96

     provided guidelines for pricing irrigation services in line with the Law on Price and built on the volume-based

     fee principle. Although implementation of these decrees has faced many challenges (e.g., technical and

     economic norms, unclear responsibilities at sub-provincial levels, budget shortage, etc.), they were expected

     to contribute to increasing the area of more water-efficient irrigation, especially in the poor and remote areas,

     and thereby directly contributing climate resilient management of landscapes.

 26. Cleaner Transport (Prior Action 6): The sixth prior action – new emissions standards for in-use and imported

     used road motor vehicles - was appropriate and relevant in achieving the development objective. Emissions

     standards encourage the adoption of progressively cleaner fuels in the transport sector, thus leading to a

     reduction in polluting vehicles and therefore reduced local air pollution. Stricter vehicle emissions standards

     are also a particularly efficient way to reduce overall GHG emissions from the sector in Vietnam, by as much as

     5 million tons per year in 2030, or about 5.5 percent of annual GHG emissions under a business-as-usual

     scenario in the same year. Prime Minister Decision No. 16/2019/QD-TTg dated March 28, 2019 applied more

     stringent standards starting in 2020 for in-use vehicles produced after 2008, 2021 for in-use vehicles produced

     between 1999 and 2008, and immediately upon effectiveness of the standard for imported used vehicles.

     Application of these standards were expected to directly contribute to reducing the negative impact on air

     quality and GHG emissions from this “dirty” sub-set of the vehicle fleet. The prior action was therefore

     appropriate and relevant to lead to a cleaner transport sector in Vietnam.



 27. Towards a Low-Carbon Energy Transition – Energy Efficiency (Prior Action 7): The seventh prior action –

     adoption of a national energy efficiency program for the period 2019-2030 - was appropriate and relevant in

     achieving the development objective. The program (adopted via Prime Minister Decision No. 280/2019/QD-

     TTg dated March 13, 2019) was expected to lead towards an efficiency rate improvement of between 8 to 10

     percent from the 2019 to 2030 period against a business-as-usual energy demand forecast. This program was

     expected to achieve this target through support to policy development, technical assistance, awareness raising,

     and by serving as a vehicle for resource mobilization that would lead to aggressive efforts to improve demand

     side energy efficiency across sectors. The program also was expected to provide support to the provincial level

     for setting up and implementing their own energy efficiency action plans. Improving energy efficiency was

     considered a least cost option for reducing emissions in the sector by reducing the rate of increase of energy

     demand and avoiding new coal fired power generation. The government recognized that there was a lack of

     technical expertise and understanding by industries, potential service providers and local financial institutions

     on modern energy efficiency technologies and practices and the adoption of this program was expected to

     address this challenge. Ultimately, the prior action contributed to the PDO as the program was expected to

     lead to increased uptake of energy efficiency measures which would directly support the objective of moving

     towards a low-carbon energy transition.



 28. Towards a Low-Carbon Energy Transition – Renewable Energy (Prior Action 8): The eighth prior action – revising

     the feed-in tariff and adoption of a standard power purchase agreement for wind power development - was

     appropriate and relevant in achieving the development objective. In early 2017, a revised FIT for utility-scale

     solar photovoltaic led to increased private sector investment (of about 2.7 GW) in new renewable energy

     generation capacity. The prior action (Prime Minister Decision No. 39/2018/QD-TTg dated September 10, 2018

     and MOIT Circular No. 02/2019/TT-BCT dated February 28, 2019) was expected to further incentivize

     investment in both onshore and offshore wind and address financing challenges faced by wind developers (for

     example, reduced risks related to force majeure and technical curtailment). The prior action was expected to

     lead to a significant investment in wind power generation, contributing towards Vietnam’s NDC GHG emission

     reduction goals and renewable energy targets. The prior action was therefore appropriate and relevant to

     contribute to Vietnam’s low-carbon energy transition.

     assess whether the PDO was achieved.



 44. The target was exceeded. Based on an analysis that compared emission reductions associated with the policy

     percent and 14.76 percent, respectively, for the 2019-2021 period. The relative reduction of exhaust HSUs is

     estimated at 9.21 percent. These exceed or meet the targets of 3, 7 and 9 percent for CO, HC and HSU,

     standards from the Ministry of Transport and Vietnam Register for 2019-2021 to estimate emissions in a

     business-as-usual scenario and in a “with policy” scenario. These emissions standards continue to be

     implemented and enforced.

III. OTHER OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS



A. Poverty, Gender and Social Impacts



  51. An ex-post review was conducted vis-a-vis the ex-ante findings from the 2019 PSIA report. The review

      focused on potential direct and indirect impacts on the poor/vulnerable groups that may be affected by the

      prior actions. A desk review was conducted for 4 out of 8 prior actions, including Prior Actions #1, 4, 5, and 8.

      These were selected for the review because these policies were anticipated by the PSIA to cause direct and/or

      indirect impacts on poor/vulnerable groups. The remaining four Prior Actions (PA #2, 3, 6, and 7) were not

      reviewed because these policies target non-poor groups and are not anticipated to create any potential direct

      and/or indirect adverse impacts on the poor/vulnerable groups. Secondary data were collected from the public

      domain and select expert opinion was used for the ex-post review.



  52. Prior action # 1, including Decree 156 and Circular 28 which aim to translate the Law on Forestry (2017) into

      practice, has delivered its overall intended positive impacts. Key positive impacts include overall improved

      management, use and protection of forest across the country. Forest dependent households and communities,

      especially ethnic minority (EM) communities, continue to have access to forest land they depend on for their

      livelihoods. Their right to use forest land is recognized legally – through local government’s effort in allocation

      of forest land to local people – through land titles. Land titles do not only ensure land use rights for those

      dependent on land but also encourage them to make financial and labor investment to generate additional

      income. Land titles can also be used as collateral for loan borrowing. As a result, forest coverage at country

      level has increased and has been protected through increased participation of those who have land titles to

      land and through Payment for Ecosystem Services. Customary rights to forest land use by ethnic minorities are

      ensured through allocation of forest land to EM communities.



  53. Prior action #4, including Decree 23 and Decree 167, has delivered its overall intended positive impacts albeit

      enforcement is slow. Decree 23, which strengthens protection of riverbed, riverbank and riverside – through

      regulating exploitation of sand, riverbed gravel, etc., involves a wide range of stakeholders (government and



                                                                                                             Page 23 of 40

The World Bank

Climate Change and Green Growth DPF (P171006)







      private sector). The main target group of this policy are organizations and individuals who are adversely

      affected through income loss due to restricted exploitation of river sand and gravel. Secondary data suggested

      that about one third of the provinces in Vietnam have implemented the policy amid high domestic demand of

      sand for construction. For Decree No. 167/2018/ND-CP, the key policy target group is private sector, including

      companies and individuals who are engaged in exploitation of groundwater. Secondary data suggested that

      where local governments are slow in formulating underground water restriction maps to facilitate

      implementation of restriction measures, groundwater exploitation is still ongoing. In critical areas,

      groundwater overuse may increase the risk of lowered water table, reduced surface water supplies and quality,

      and land subsidence.



  54. Prior action #5 has been delivering initial positive impacts as intended despite lack of incentives to private

      sector to invest in irrigation to improve irrigation access for farming communities, particularly in areas where

      irrigation access is essential for people to maintain their means of livelihood. The review, based on secondary

      information, suggested that the policy has some constraints to effective implementation, including a) lack of

      investment budget at local level; and b) application of advanced/water saving technologies not always being

      suitable to all farming conditions. As a result, some poor and vulnerable population who need access to reliable

      irrigation for agricultural production cannot benefit from the policy due to the lack of (i) matching funds on the

      part of the investors (i.e. organizations/individuals), and more importantly, (ii) the willingness and affordability

      of the poor in paying irrigation fees. Also, advanced/water saving techniques are not always suitable to all

      farming systems. This might exacerbate livelihood difficulties of the poor, especially of those whose farming

      activities are rainfed and rely on access to irrigation for food security and livelihood improvement.



  55. Prior action #8 has delivered its intended impacts to enable the private sector to invest in wind power, albeit

      there have been some technical constraints that slowed down their scheduled operation which result in loss

      of expected income. Social impacts (site-specific) are apparent, including land acquisition (both permanent and

      temporary) for installation of turbine towers, construction of internal roads, and transmission lines. Social risks

      are still existent during operation stage, particularly for plants located onshore, including flicker, noise from

      turbine operation, and blade throw.



  56. With regards to social risk management, prior actions have identified key, broad social risks – at national

      levels. However, management of social risk at local level is not managed consistently towards the expected

      outcomes of the prior actions because of slow or inconsistent enforcement. As a result, management of social

      risks at local level is working but is more ad-hoc, depending on the perceived and observed social risks at

      specific sites (e.g. response actions are typically taken quickly for areas where riverbank erosion has developed

      to the level that increases the chance of collapse which affects assets, crops, livelihood, and lives of local

      people). A time-bound approach and consistent enforcement/committment is essential at regional and

      provincial levels to ensure social risks at local level are identified and managed effectively – through measures

      that engage full and accoutable participation of both potentially affected and interested stakeholders. It is also

      a good practice to estimate and manage residual risks after measures to manage identified risks have been

      accounted for.

  59. The Vietnam National Assembly ratified the revised Law on Environmental Protection No. 77/2020/QH14 on

      (SEA) and Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA). As per Articles 26, 27 and 33 of the Law, the EA process

      requires a mandatory consultation with key stakeholders to aim at setting up mitigation measures for potential

      adverse environmental impacts and finalizing EA process (i.e., SEA or EIA), and ensuring the sustainable

      development of a strategy, planning or project and thus to improve decision making.

      classified as Categories I and II19 required to have an EIA and the EIA is reviewed and endorsed by MONRE.

      Dedicated environmental trainings are provided by MONRE’s Training Centre to designated staff of relevant

      government agencies from central to local levels to improve their environmental appraisal and monitoring

      capacity.

B. Next Phase



  74. There is a constrained lending environment for DPF operations in Vietnam, which limit the potential for

      further climate change and green growth DPF operations in the near future. Government regulations on

      management and utilization of loans have made it exceedingly difficult for DPF operations to materialize in the

      near-term. Given that, continued support on policy reforms will need to be undertaken through advisory

      services and analytics which can be linked with IPF. The recently launched CCDR provides an analytical basis

      for further support, with a focus on Vietnam embracing a new development paradigm that incorporates two

      critical pathways (resilient and decarbonizing) that will help it balance development goals with growing climate

      risks. The CCDR identified five priority policy packages that require the most immediate attention by

      government and represent the most urgent public and private investments needed to achieve adaptation and

      mitigation goals: (i) A coordinated regional program for the Mekong Delta; (ii) an integrated coastal resilience

      investment program for main urban centers and connecting infrastructure; (iii) a targeted air pollution

      reduction program in the Hanoi airshed to reach the WHO interim target by 2030 and enhance labor

      productivity gains; (iv) acceleration of the clean energy transition; and (v) a new social contract to protect the

      most vulnerable people. These five areas build on policy reform areas of this DPF operation and the lapsed

      preceding DPF series, namely the reform area on strengthened resilient planning of Mekong Delta under pillar

      1 and the entirety of pillar 2 on the adoption of cleaner transport and energy systems. Policy tracks of the

      preceding DPF series where these policy packages build on include those on integrated coastal zone

      management and air quality management.
