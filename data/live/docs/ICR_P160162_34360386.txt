I. PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES



 A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL



   Context



   1. Country Context. At appraisal in 2018, Viet Nam had undergone a remarkable transformation from a low-income

   to a middle-income economy within a single generation, fueled by the Doi Moi reforms and sustained GDP growth

   averaging 5.3 percent annually since the late 1980s. This economic surge, alongside the Government of Viet Nam

   (GoV)'s focus on inclusive social development, enabled a drastic reduction in extreme poverty and notable increase

   in shared prosperity. Urbanization, particularly in and around Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, accompanied this

   economic expansion, though it had been characterized by low-density and fragmented growth. While major cities

   had seen benefits, secondary cities and rural areas faced continued challenges like inadequate basic services and

   increased vulnerability to climate change.



   2. Sectoral Context. In 2018, Viet Nam's urban areas, comprising 34 percent of the population and contributing to

   over half of the national GDP, faced the challenge of low and stagnant urban density despite the global evidence

   suggesting that economic densification enhances productivity, spurs innovation, and facilitates efficient service

   delivery. This issue, largely attributed to Viet Nam’s City Classification System which incentivized rapid land

   conversion with little emphasis on density, resulted in fragmented urbanization and a host of urban management

   issues such as traffic congestion, air pollution, and informal settlements. The inefficiency of this sprawl was

   recognized at the time of project preparation, as were, in contrast, the economic and social benefits associated with

   the compact cities with tightly-knit development patterns, strong public transport linkages, and good accessibility to

   services and jobs. Such compact cities would not only be more efficient in their use of infrastructure but also reduce

   environmental impacts. Additionally, the need for gender-inclusive economic opportunities was recognized, as the

   gender gap in urban employment persisted, with a significant proportion of women engaged in informal work.



   3. Government Strategies. Recognizing urbanization's strategic role in its goals for industrialization and

   modernization, in 2009 the government developed the Framework Master Plan for Urban Development in Viet Nam

   to 2025 and Vision to 2050 (i.e., National Master Plan) aiming for well-planned urban growth to support Viet Nam's

   continued transformation into a high-income economy. The National Master Plan emphasized the importance of

   urban connectivity as well as the treatment of surface and wastewater (Section 5. “Plan for arrangement of national

   urban technical infrastructure”).



   4. Rationale for Bank Involvement. In its urban sector engagement strategy, the Bank likewise recognized the

   importance of secondary cities for balanced and sustainable urban growth, and a 2016 report jointly authored with

   Viet Nam’s Ministry of Planning emphasized the need for institutional strengthening to foster integrated urban

   planning as well as the need to improve road quality and the quality of urban transport services. The Bank’s approach

   at the time was to prepare multi-city lending operations for these secondary cities, and Thai Nguyen was part of a

   proposal for a five-city lending project (the other cities being Ky Anh, Tinh Gia, Hai Duong, and Yen Bai). A multi-city

   project – ‘Dynamic Cities’ – was designed, but at the time of appraisal the Government of Viet Nam deemed only

   Thai Nguyen ready to receive World Bank financing, therefore this standalone project was proposed to the Board

   instead. It was envisaged that successful aspects of the project could be replicated in the other cities once they were

   operationally ready for an urban lending project to be commenced.

Components



9. The project consisted of two components:

   (a) Component 1: Structural Investments – Rehabilitation and Construction of Urban Infrastructure (Original

       allocation: US$75.69 million IDA, US$11.83 million counterpart funding [CF]; Actual cost: US$50.51 million

           • Urban drainage: The overall improvement of the local drainage system (including construction of

Other Changes



17. Cancellation of funds. The first restructuring cancelled US$23 million of IDA Scale-Up Facility (SUF) credit 62970

and recorded a corresponding increase in the amount of counterpart funding (date of cancellation: 31 July, 2021).



18. Additional changes to the Results Framework The second restructuring adjusted the targets of several

intermediate indicators reflecting the reduced the scope of activities under Component 1:



- The additional wastewater treatment capacity provided was reduced from 1,100 to 930 cubic meters because of the

reduction in scale of the rehabilitation of ditches.

- The length of roads and bridges constructed or improved was reduced from 14.45 to 13.75 km due to the reduced

scale of investments in newly constructed roads and bridges and upgraded roads.

- The area of new or improved walkable public space was reduced from 151,925 to 116,605 square meters – a

reduction of almost 25% which followed from the two changes above.



19. The second restructuring also adjusted the results framework to reflect the changes to Component 2: the delivery

of three plans, rather than one, and it increased the target for the number of residents consulted because the project

had by then consulted on the redesigned elements, as well as the initial design.



Rationale for Changes and Their Implication on the Original Theory of Change



20. First Restructuring. The project was approved in June 2018 with a counterpart funding requirement of US$20

million. By August 2021 the counterpart funding needs had increased substantially, to US$43 million. This was

because, firstly, the amount and cost of land required for resettlement had been significantly underestimated at

appraisal – the number of households needing to be resettled was almost three times higher than anticipated and the

unit price of land was twice as high. Furthermore, in May 2020 the Government of Viet Nam had introduced a new

policy (Decree 56/2020/ND-CP) which required that certain project expenditures (taxes, front-end fees, commitment

fees, interest) which were to have been funded from the IDA credit use counterpart funding instead. Some IDA credit

might thus have been freed up and used for further investments. However, this would have required an extensive and

lengthy approval process, therefore the Thai Nguyen People’s Provincial Committee (PPC) decided that the overall

envelope of the project should remain US$100 million. Consequently, US$23 million of the Bank’s credit was no longer

required to achieve the project’s objectives and was cancelled in the first restructuring.



21. Second Restructuring. The ballooning counterpart funding requirement led the PPC to look for savings, and in

March 2022, they requested approval for a series of changes to the project’s Results Framework reflecting a modest

reduction in scale of the investments in drainage and roads (and therefore also a reduction in the number of project

beneficiaries). The rationale for expanding indicator 9 was to be more precise about the elements to be delivered,

and because without being approved the plans cannot have development impact. These changes were discussed

extensively with the World Bank during the Mid-term Review in November 2021.

    II. OUTCOME



A. RELEVANCE OF PDOs



Assessment of Relevance of PDOs and Rating



Rating: Substantial



23. At appraisal the PDOs were highly relevant to the World Bank Group’s then operative Country Partnership

Framework (CPF) for Vietnam, FY2018-2022 (Report No. 111771-VN) insofar as they supported the achievement of

two CPF objectives: Objective 5, “improve planning, management and delivery of infrastructure and land in cities”,

and Objective 10, “increase climate resilience and strengthen disaster risk management”.



24. The Mid-Term Review (November 2021) concluded that the PDO remained relevant to the CPF, which states that

“the need for further productive infrastructure remains, particularly in energy, transport, water, sanitation, and

telecommunications”. The PDO also continued to be substantially relevant to Viet Nam’s national development plans

and policies, including Resolution No. 06-NQ/TW, issued on January 24, 2022, “On planning, construction,

management, and sustainable urban development in Vietnam to 2030, with a vision to 2045” which identifies urban

management capacity as a problem facing the country, and which notes the importance of the development of urban

infrastructure and urban social services, including education.





                                                                                                          Page 11 of 63

  The World Bank

25. The Country Partnership Framework for Vietnam, FY2023-2027 has not been finalized but the PDO is aligned with

the CPF’s priorities of climate resilience, sustainable growth, and gender inclusion. The PDO's continuing relevance is

also demonstrated by the World Bank’s current Country and Climate Development Report for Vietnam (July 2022) ,

which notes that the region in which Thai Nguyen is situated is a potential hotspot for flooding.



26. The substantial relevance of the PDO is further supported by GoV’s continued engagement with the World Bank

in urban infrastructure projects, such as the Dynamic Cities Integrated Development Project (P168290, approved in

2019) which takes the same project model as Thai Nguyen focused on increasing access to urban infrastructure and

improving integrated urban planning and applies it to four other secondary cities in Viet Nam: Yen Bai, Hai Duong,

Tinh Gia, and Ky Anh. Another example is the Vinh Long City Urban Development and Climate Resilience Project

(P171700, approved in 2020), which, like Thai Nguyen, is providing new and improved drainage systems, and reducing

travel times. There are also discussions between the World Bank and GoV about streamlining the similar model of

support into Class-2 and Class-3 cities.5

30. The area prone to floods in the area covered by the project interventions met the target of 631 hectares. The

to the Xuong Rong canal (1.5km) and the Mo Bach canal (0.7 km). The immediate vicinity of the canals includes a bus

station used by workers commuting from Thai Nguyen to the major industrial sites south of the city.



31. The number of people benefiting from improved drainage in the area covered by the project interventions

exceeded the set target, with 1,323 benefiting compared to 1,207 as planned. As with other indicators measuring the

number of people affected which were likewise over-achieved, this was due to demographic change during the project

where an increase in the number of residents was observed within the geographic area benefiting from project

interventions. While the demographic shift has not been studied in detail, it is unlikely that the final results would

have differed significantly in a “no demographic change” scenario and therefore the results presented herein are

considered to be at target or close to target, all things considered. 51.8% of these beneficiaries were female, exceeding

the target of 51%, and 54% of them were from the bottom 40% of the income distribution. These beneficiaries are

people who live or work in the areas in which flood risk is reduced.

target with 3,291 benefiting compared to 3,178 as planned. To achieve this, 930 cubic meters of additional water

treatment capacity was provided, meeting the set target: 540m3 in the Phan Dinh Phung and Gia Sang areas of the

city due to investments in rehabilitation of the drainage system of the Xuong Rong ditch, and 390 m3 of capacity

provided in Tan Thinh and Thinh Dan due to investments in rehabilitation of the drainage system of the Mo Bach ditch.

887 households were connected to the new or improved wastewater treatment systems, exceeding the target of 840

the Xuong Rong canal and 1.3km the area of the Mo Bach canal, meeting the set target of 2.8km. The share of these

3,291 beneficiaries who were female slightly exceeded the set target, with 51.8% compared to 51%, as did the share

33. The number of people who have access to new and improved roads under the project exceeded the set target,

construction of Huong Thuong-Chua Hang road (including Molinh 1 and Huong Thuong bridges); 2.3km were due to

Dan bridge. Huong Thuong is a new four-lane, cantilevered bridge. It is free to use (it replaces a toll bridge) and, along

with the linking roads, it has reduced the journey time from the far side of the Cau river into the city from 15-20

minutes to 5 minutes. The new roads’ design draws on the city masterplan and considers the city’s climate-related

disaster risks (in this case, flooding). Of those who benefited from new and improved roads, 51.8% were female,

E. OTHER OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS (IF ANY)



  Gender



 46. The key gender-specific impact of the project was the disproportionate benefit to women of new and improved

 childcare facilities; the benefits of new, high quality and affordable childcare service accrue disproportionately to

 women because in Viet Nam – as in many countries – women perform the bulk of unpaid childcare. These benefits

 (for example the reduction in time spent on unpaid childcare) are captured in ‘Achievement of the PDOs: Efficacy’,

 above. However, the improved access to sanitation will also have had a disproportionately positive impact on women,

 since women are the primary domestic caregivers in Viet Nam10 not just for children but for people of all ages, and a

 key benefit of sanitation is that it reduces the incidence of disease, and therefore the need for care. Gender-

 disaggregated monitoring of project results was anticipated from the very start of the project and the gender specific

 targets were all met or exceeded.



 Institutional Strengthening



 47. During the Bank implementation support mission to Thai Nguyen City in December 2023 both City and Provincial

 officials reported that thanks to their experience managing both this project and the Second Northern Mountains

 Poverty Reduction project (P113493), the public administration is significantly better able to prepare and deliver

 successful investment projects. The technical support for asset management under this project significantly improved

 the city authorities’ urban planning capacity, notably their ability to record information on infrastructure assets and

 helped them to integrate this information with a national database.



 Mobilizing Private Sector Financing



 48. The project did not seek to mobilize private sector financing. Nevertheless, the physical infrastructure elements

 of the project (roads, bridges, sanitation) can be seen as private sector-enabling since they facilitate commerce and

 development and increase land values (see below), giving owners greater collateral against which to potentially

 borrow and invest.

III. KEY FACTORS THAT AFFECTED IMPLEMENTATION AND OUTCOME



 A. KEY FACTORS DURING PREPARATION



  53. Soundness of background analysis. The project was informed by adequate analysis of the national and regional

  context. The decision to proceed with Thai Nguyen alone, ahead of the other cities in the Dynamic Cities project as it

  was then envisaged, on the basis of the strong capacity of the PMU and city administration was vindicated by their

  strong performance in implementation. However, there were certain shortcomings during project preparation which

  are common to other lending projects in Vietnam.12 The PAD correctly recognized the need for “strong operations

  planning and high-quality project preparation”. Despite this, the number of households which would need to be

  resettled to implement civil works as planned was substantially underestimated: it turned out to be 466 rather than

  133.



  54. Cost analysis. Just as the amount of land was significantly underestimated, so too was the cost per unit of land,

  and again this was one reason for which the amount of counterpart funding required to complete the project

  ballooned, which in turn led to the scaling back of the project. This had relatively minor consequences for the number

56. Climate considerations. Climate change tends to make flooding more frequent and severe,13 and the project

successfully reduced flood risk in Thai Nguyen through the drainage investments, while the design of road and bridge

elements takes such risks into account. Furthermore, the PAD stated that the new public space “may include public

green spaces and promenades with lanes for both cyclists and pedestrians”, strongly implying that this element of the

project would support increased active travel. The new pavement is indeed a lane for pedestrians, and, with more than

600 trees planted, much of it is green. However, “Green spaces” might well be assumed to mean “car-free spaces” such

as parks or public squares, and this project did not create any of those. Furthermore, the quality of pedestrian

experience ultimately provided by the new and improved public spaces suffered because of the reduction in the

amount of public space delivered: many of the sidewalks are considerably narrower than as designed. This will have

the effect of reducing the attractiveness of walking as a transport option, compared to in the project as designed, and

will increase the propensity to use motorized transport (though walking on the improved roads is nevertheless safer

and more pleasant than it would be had the project not been implemented at all).



57. Reflection of lessons learned. The PAD lists several projects which informed this project,14 and six concrete lessons

which are said to be reflected in its design. This is helpful as it reveals what was taken into consideration and provides

a further dimension of measurable success. One of these – “adoption of community participation and consultation

throughout planning, design and implementation process” – does seem to have been reflected in the project’s design

and execution with real success: the strongly positive qualitative assessment of the different infrastructure elements

by the Thai Nguyen public is very likely in part because of extensive consultation. Finally, this section of the appraisal

document asserts the need for “strong, sustained political commitment” and notes that “the project has received

strong support from the central, provincial, and city governments.” Unfortunately, the project’s delayed inclusion in

the Government of Vietnam‘s Medium Term Investment Plan may indicate that the political support was weaker than

estimated.



58. Reflection of aims in project design. The project’s appraisal document gives as part of the background context

Viet Nam’s sometimes sprawling pattern of recent development. It implies that implementing Component 1 as planned

would promote more compact development. The considerations however go in different directions. Reducing flood

risk is indeed likely to facilitate higher density development than might otherwise have occurred, and the relevant

areas are close to the center of Thai Nguyen. However other elements under Component 1, such as fast, dual-

carriageway roads seem just as likely to promote development further from the city center than might otherwise have

been the case, i.e., although it is challenging to establish cause and effect in such cases, they may even promote less

compact development.

Factors subject to the control of government and/or implementing entities



 59. Consequences of GoV policy changes. Although the project was delivered on time and on budget it was ultimately

 somewhat downscaled, in part because of a change in policy on the part of the Government of Viet Nam (see

 “Significant Changes During Implementation”, above). Decree 56/2020/ND-CP issued on 25th of May 2020 stipulated

 that only counterpart funding, not IDA funds, could be used for certain expenditures. This put added pressure on an

 already ballooning counterpart fund budget for this project (see “Cost analysis” in “Key Factors During Preparation”,

 above) and one can with confidence expect that if these counterpart funds had been available to be used for land

 acquisition the project would not have had to be downscaled to the same extent.



 60. Delays. The project experienced delays in 2019, 2020 and 2021: in mid-2021 the majority of the construction

 packages were severely behind schedule. This was primarily because of delays in land acquisition (see above) which

 was in part, like the downscaling, caused by a policy decision: in this case there was an inadequate allocation of funds

 from central government in 19/20 because the project had not been included in GoV’s Medium Term Investment Plan

 (MTIP) 2016-2020. Thus, while the project was well aligned in policy and design terms with regional and national policy

 objectives (see section “Context at Appraisal”, above), there could have been stronger institutional coordination.



 61. Underspend on Component 2. At appraisal in 2018 US$6.56 million was allocated towards the activities under

 Component 2. The project restructuring approved in August 2021 allocated US$7.97 million. However, at closing only

 US$ 0.97 million had been spent on these activities. This is primarily because in 2021 the Thai Nguyen PPC began work

 on a new integrated plan for the whole province (including transportation, urban development, land management,

 waste management, wastewater, tourism).15 Therefore, all of the work planned under Component 2 was indeed carried

 out with technical input and guidance from the Bank, but it was to a large extent funded by the Province of Thai Nguyen

 in parallel, i.e. outside of the project. The work funded through this project was limited to adding a level of detail that

 was lacking in the provincial plans. World Bank experts have confirmed that the three plans delivered under Component

 2 are of acceptable quality, and the plans and strategies are being used by the city authorities to guide present

 development and to support the planning of future development. This meant the project as a whole cost less than it

 otherwise would have, but it again points to a lack of institutional coordination.



 62. Site Clearance. Until the project's final months, there were delays in clearing the land for roads, bridges and drains.

 This was in part because of dissatisfaction on the part of property owners regarding the level of compensation and in

 part because of difficulties in establishing land ownership in cases of family disputes and complex inheritance

 structures. These problems were all satisfactorily resolved (see ‘Environmental, Social and Fiduciary Compliance’,

 below) in time for the built elements of the project to be completed before project closing.

V. LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS



   84. Ensure the relevance of the project to the problems cited in justification for it. At appraisal, the project was

   justified repeatedly in terms of achieving higher density urban development. However, some elements of the project

   (especially the new roads) may have the opposite effect. For clarity and consistency, it is important that a project

   should plausibly be able to mitigate the problems cited in justification, and if the relationship is more complicated,

   this should be spelled out.



   85. Underestimating the amount of land required and its cost can have a serious impact on the scale and quality of

   the infrastructure delivered. The importance of avoiding this mistake was noted at appraisal as a lesson which was to

   inform the project. Nevertheless, underestimates in this area occurred, and they were a contributing factor to the

   substantial reduction in scale of the built elements of the project (see “Significant Changes During Implementation”,

   above) which reduced the quality of the pedestrian infrastructure. Some concrete steps which could mitigate the risk

   of this happening again would be 1. Stronger oversight of the preparation of cost estimates as a pre-condition for

   proceeding; 2. Payment for accuracy when it comes to feasibility consultants, and 3. Independent monitoring and

   selection of the consultants engaged for this work.



   86. Multi-provincial urban infrastructure projects that are thematically aligned should be designed and coordinated

   in a more streamlined manner. More should be done to coordinate with national-level entities such as the Ministry

   of Construction to provide upstream technical support to project design and ensure broader thematic alignment of

   development objectives. A national-focused program could provide for better management and coordination of multi-

   province urban infrastructure projects, while incentivizing cities to learn from each other and incorporate lessons

   learned. From the side of the government, the cities should engage with each other and national-level ministries

   earlier, including prior to submission of a formal Project Proposal. From the Bank side, a more streamlined financing

   approach such as a Series of Projects or Multi-Phase Programmatic Approach should be considered.



   87. Participatory design can support high levels of user satisfaction. This is not a surprising lesson, but the project

   strongly confirms what one might expect: that extensive consultation during the planning and implementation stages

   of a project can contribute to extremely high levels of project satisfaction. And while the coherence of the different

   elements of the project could be questioned, their selection does seem to reflect what many of the citizens of Thai

   Nguyen desired in terms of urban improvements.



   88. The benefits of a clear project structure. For the sake of clarity, the sub-components (1.1. drainage; 1.3. urban

   transport and so on) should have been separate components. This would have made easier the identification and

   tracking of costs, problems and successes associated with each element of the project throughout the lifetime of the

   project. It would have facilitated the extraction of data of a type which is useful for potential future Bank projects, for

   the Government of Viet Nam, and for general public policy analysis, for example, ‘how much did it cost to build or

   improve a kilometer of drainage channel in Viet Nam in this period?’, or ‘in this case, was building an educational

   facility more or less prone to delays than building a bridge?’.



   89. The importance of institutional coordination. There were two instances during this project when insufficiently

   close coordination between levels of public administration could have caused significant problems: Firstly, much of

   the activity delivered under component 2 was in fact funded not through but by the Thai Nguyen PPC under a separate



                                                                                                                 Page 27 of 63

      The World Bank

      Dynamic City Integrated Development Project - Thai Nguyen (P160162)







    budget line. The deliverables were of adequate quality, but if they had not been then questions would rightly have

    been asked about whether, if they had been financed under the project, there would have been greater scope for

    control and coordination, and therefore a better development outcome could have been ensured. Secondly, there

    were delays early in the project because through-delivery of IDA funds from central government in 19/20 took longer

    than expected because the project had not been included in GoV’s Medium Term Investment Plan (MTIP) 2016-2020.

    In this case the lost time was made up, perhaps helped by the downscaling. Again, if it had not been, then there may

    have been a risk to development outcome. These potential risks could have been mitigated with stronger coordination

    between the various levels of public administration and between the Bank and each of those levels.

                                      ANNEX 4. EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS





 The following analysis was conducted at project completion to evaluate the economic returns of the

 project. It evaluated the same 9 physical investments as under the economic analysis at appraisal, using

 the same methodology.

 1. Methodology

 The analysis employs the conventional economic appraisal methodology for infrastructure improvement

 schemes, comparing annual discounted costs and benefits of the project to society as a whole over the

 project life cycle in “with” and “without” scenarios using the Net Present Value (NPV) and the Internal

 Rate of Return (IRR) indicators to justify the economic viability. The discount rate and conversion factors

 at appraisal are valid at closure.

 Valuation of Economic Costs The economic analysis takes into account economic costs that are expected

 to accrue during the 25-year economic lifetime of a subproject. These costs consist of incremental

 investment costs and O&M costs. Investment costs include costs for civil work, equipment, resettlement,

 environmental management, consulting services and project management.

 Maintenance Costs At appraisal, the annual economic O&M cost was expressed as a percentage of the

 3. Cost Benefit Analysis Results

 The updated economic analysis reconfirms that all the sub-projects in Thai Nguyen DCIDP are

 economically viable. The updated IRRs of investments are in the range of 13.8 – 28.8 percent, higher than

 the economic opportunity cost of capital of 10%. Furthermore, with the exception of Le Huu Trac Road,

 all subprojects even have higher internal rates of returns than anticipated at appraisal. These imply that

 project restructuring was helpful (the analysis at appraisal may have been conservative, but so was the

 analysis at closing).

 The revision of scope of work of the Le Huu Trac Road affected its connectivity to the wider network.

 Traffic count at completion of the project was therefore significantly lower than traffic forecasted at

 appraisal. Consequently, the economic internal rate of return of the road reduced from 18.2 percent to

 16.8 percent.

 The project as a whole had an NPV of US$ 50.86 million and an IRR of 20.26%.
