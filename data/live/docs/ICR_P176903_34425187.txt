I.     PROGRAM CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES





     A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL



     Context

     1.    This Implementation Completion and Results Report (ICR) reviews the performance and results of the

     programmatic series of development policy financing operations (DPFs) on accelerating reforms for inclusive

     growth and resilient recovery to the Republic of Kenya. The programmatic series consisted of one tranche

     operation of US$750 million in IDA financing, and a second operation of US$520 million in IDA financing and US$230

     million in IBRD financing, for a total of US$1.5 billion. The operations were approved by the Executive Board of

     Directors in May 2021 and February 2022, respectively.

     2.    The Kenyan economy was growing before the COVID-19 crisis, but fiscal imbalances were mounting. Real

     gross domestic product (GDP) grew by 4.6 percent on average between 2011-2019, while poverty fell by 13

     percentage points between 2005 and 2019. For most of this period, economic growth was ‘pro-poor’ as poorer

     households saw a great consumption increase than richer households, and thus inequality decreased. Progress in

     other non-monetary dimensions accompanied these indicators. Kenya’s Human Development Index (HDI) which

     aggregates education, life-expectancy, and income indicators rose from 0.55 in 2010 to 0.60 in 2019, while access

     to electricity almost doubled in the last decade, to 75 percent of households in 2018. Government finances were,

     however, increasingly challenged. The fiscal deficit averaged 7.8 percent of GDP between 2015-19 as infrastructure

     spending rose, with revenues remaining relatively flat as a percent of GDP. As a consequence, the government debt

     burden sharply increased, from 42.9 percent of GDP in 2014 to 64.2 percent in 2019; over 20 percentage points of

     GDP in only five years. Rising interest rate costs accompanied the rise in public debt, which went from 2.7 percent

     of GDP in 2014 to 4.1 percent in 2019.

     3.    The 2020 global pandemic exposed Kenya’s economic vulnerabilities, making it urgent to support the

     country and addressing key structural issues. The economy was severely affected through mobility, trade, and

     travel disruption, including the ones implemented by the government’s containment measures. As a consequence,

     at the onset of the pandemic, domestic external demand sharply fell, unemployment rates increased, wage workers

     reduced their working hours, and many household-run businesses had to close. Overall, GDP in 2020 fell by 0.3

     percent, while the poverty rate increased by four percentage points (additional two million poor), erasing five years

     of progress in poverty reduction. Even if livelihoods had recovered substantially by the end of 2020, new waves of

     COVID-19 variants triggered partial lockdown policies, which caused renewed economic pressures. At the time of

     preparation of this DPO, vaccinations had already begun, and the government’s vaccination strategy aimed to

     inoculate all 26 million adult individuals by June 2022.

     4.    At the time of preparation, the Government of Kenya (GoK) paused its fiscal consolidation efforts to address

     the pandemic. Prior to the pandemic, the government was pursuing a broad-based reform program to address long-

     term, structural development challenges, as well as fiscal adjustment to reduce debt vulnerabilities. Recognizing

        the need to address fiscal vulnerabilities, Kenya’s National Treasury begun implementing a fiscal consolidation plan,

        and expected to reduce the fiscal deficit from 7.6 percent of GDP in FY2018/19 to 6.0 percent in FY2019/20.

        However, because of the COVID-19 outbreak, tax revenues fell below target as the economy slowed down, as well

        as from tax cuts to support households and firm liquidity. The GoK increased public spending to support the

        healthcare system, and the most vulnerable households and firms. The planned fiscal consolidation had to be put

        on pause and the fiscal deficit increased to 7.8 percent of GDP, with government debt rising to 65.9 percent of GDP

        in FY2019/20. The Joint WB-IMF Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA) was published in April 2021, stating that Kenya’s

        risk of debt distress remained high. Kenya participated in the Debt Service Suspension Initiative (DSSI) as part of a

        G20 initiative to support countries concentrate their resources to fighting the COVID-19 pandemic and safeguard

        lives, which provided relief through an estimated deferment of payments of US$639 million from January to June

        2021.

        5.    Longer-term development challenges in the electricity, climate, and human capital sectors remained,

        despite progress. Kenya Power and Lighting Company (KPLC) was on declining operational and financial

        performance path, threatening the financial sustainability of the sector and affecting overall fiscal stability.

        Inadequate budget transfer to fund the country’s ambitious last-mile electrification program, which saw its access

        rate almost doubling during the 2010s decade, and slow growth in electricity consumption caused liquidity

        constraints for KPLC, forcing it to resort to high-cost, short-term debt to finance long-term assets. Moreover, with

        floods and droughts increasing in frequency and severity, there was ample space to continue strengthening

        environmental governance and climate change adaptations strategies, including leveraging on Kenya’s vast natural

        capital. For example, water and sanitation management had become an increasing challenge due to the scarcity of

        the resource in Kenya as urbanization rates increased. Further, the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic revealed

        shortcomings in Kenya’s capacity to prepare for and respond to public health emergencies.

        6.     At the time of preparation of the operation, the Government was in its last two years in office, and the

        economic outlook remained uncertain. The government, which came into office in 2017, had prioritized four main

        pillars as part of its five-year medium-term plans, referred to as the Big Four. These include: (i) food and nutritional

        security, (ii) at least 500,000 affordable housing units, (iii) universal health coverage, and (iv) raising the share of

        manufacturing to 15 percent of GDP. Although the political environment had improved following the 2017 elections,

        political and governance risks remained substantial. By the time of preparation of this operation, the COVID-19

        pandemic had slowed the progress in the government’s agenda, while the political environment was becoming

        increasingly heated as the August 2022 Presidential elections were near. Although vaccinations had already begun,

        Kenya still faced health and socioeconomic risks from the pandemic. The base case was for the economy to partially

        recover in the near-term, with real GDP projected to grow by 4.5 percent in 2021 and 4.7 percent in 2022.1

        7.    In April 2021, the IMF Board approved a 38-month, SDR 1.66 billion (approximately US$2.34 billion)

        program under the Extended Credit Facility (ECF) and the Extended Fund Facility (EFF), which was a key

        complement to the government and World Bank efforts. The program was aimed at supporting fiscal consolidation

        and the economic recovery from the COVID-19 crisis. The last IMF financing prior to the new program was US$739

        million under the May 2020 RCF to address the impact of COVID-19.

        Rationale for this DPF

        8.    In this context, the rationale of this programmatic DPF series was to support Kenya’s inclusive and resilient

        recovery from the COVID-19 pandemic and address key long-term structural challenges, supporting policy and

        institutional reforms which were critical to the government’s efforts in the wake of the crisis. The DPF supported

      9.      Original Program Development Objective(s) (PDO) (as approved)

      The original Program Development Objective (PDO), as approved: Accelerating reforms for a resilient and

      sustainable recovery from the COVID-19 shock, and the continued advancement of Kenya's inclusive growth

      agenda.

      10.     Original Policy Areas/Pillars Supported by the Program (as approved)

      The original policy areas or pillars supported by the program, as approved:

             i.    Fiscal sector reforms to make spending more transparent and efficient and enhance debt management;

            ii.    Electricity sector reforms to strengthen the cornerstone utility (Kenya Power and Lighting Company

                   [KPLC]) and keep Kenya on an efficient, green energy path;

            iii.   Natural capital reforms to strengthen environmental governance, combat climate change, and make

                   land- and water-resource use more efficient, equitable and sustainable; and

            iv.    Human capital reforms to strengthen Kenya’s health and education sectors and lift inclusive growth.



      11. Both the PDO and the pillars were aligned and relevant for the ongoing country context . As described, the

      COVID-19 pandemic had severe effects in the economy, and it was key to accelerate reforms to avoid a more severe

      hit on Kenyan citizens. Returning to a fiscal consolidation path was a priority for the government (pillars 1 and 2);

      as part of its COVID-19 response, the central government raised spending on social programs by 9.9 percent in

      nominal terms in FY2019/20 and introduced new programs to support households and firms. Moreover, supporting

      the medium-term development agenda to secure a resilient recovery was key to continue the advancements on the

      country’s inclusive growth agenda (pillars 2, 3, and 4).2

      12. In addition, several reform actions strengthen the governance framework through systems and institutional

      mechanisms that contribute to the fight against corruption. These included prior actions as the new e-

      procurement systems, PIM regulations improved land valuation legislation, and improving the governance of the

      National Hospital Insurance Fund (NHIF).





      B. SIGNIFICANT CHANGES DURING IMPLEMENTATION



      13. At the time of appraisal of the second operation of the programmatic series, the Kenyan economy was

      recovering faster than expected from the COVID-19 crisis. The economy was recovering fast and real GDP was

      estimated to have increased by 7.5 percent in 2021. Domestic demand for industrial supplies rose which, in addition

      to higher global energy prices, drove higher import growth. Moreover, increased global demand in 2021 supported

      merchandise exports, particularly in horticulture and manufacturing. On the fiscal side, government revenues had a

      stronger than expected rebound, and thus fiscal consolidation efforts resumed. Overall, GDP growth was now

      projected at 5.8 percent for 2022, as wage and household incomes grew, and improved consumer confidence drove a

      recovery in private consumption.

14. However, despite the improved economic outlook, the environment remained subject to elevated

Revised Program Development Objectives (PDOs)



15. In this context, the PDO of the operation was refined and streamlined to better reflect the ongoing policy

priorities and changing context. The PDO looked to greater reflect the breath of the program and medium-term policy

priorities rather than mostly focusing on the COVID-19 recovery.

16. The PDO in the second operation changed to: Contributing to Kenya’s inclusive and resilient recovery from the

COVID-19 crisis by strengthening (1) fiscal management, (2) operational efficiency in the electricity sector while placing

Kenya on an efficient green energy path, and (3) the governance framework for Kenya’s natural and human capital

(environment, land, water and healthcare).

Revised Policy Areas/Pillars supported by the Program

17. Pillars 3 and 4 from the first operation were merged, as the indicative trigger on the new University Law was

dropped. As such, there was a relatively stronger focus on the health sector within human capital enhancement.

18.     The policy areas or pillars for the second operation were reduced to three:

       i.    Fiscal and debt reforms to make spending more transparent and efficient, and enhance domestic debt

             market performance;

      ii.    Electricity sector and public private partnership (PPP) reforms to strengthen the cornerstone utility (Kenya

             Power and Lighting Company, KPLC), place Kenya on an efficient, green energy path, and boost private

             infrastructure investment;

      iii.   Strengthen environmental and natural resource governance, combat climate change, and improve

             healthcare including pandemic response.

19. Despite the changes, the overall nature of the program didn’t change. It continued to target the same policy

areas that were the focus of the first part of the series, adjusting mostly to a highly uncertain and changing context.

In that regard, it was adequate that the second part of the series continue being part of the initial operation rather

than becoming a standalone.

      A. RELEVANCE OF PRIOR ACTIONS

Rating: Satisfactory

20. The PDO of this operation is relevant to tackling country-specific development constraints, identified in its

development strategy and set as priorities for the World Bank. The PDO is looking to directly address short- and long-

term challenges faced by the Kenyan economy, which were identified both in the Government’s Big four agenda and the

World Bank Country Partnership Framework. As previously argued, the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic allowed for the

support of the World Bank, with an adequate level of depth and scope of the reforms supported.

21. Relevance of Prior Actions has been rated Satisfactory as all prior actions have a strong and direct link to the

achievement of the PDO. All reforms supported a resilient and sustainable recovery from the COVID-19 shock, and an

inclusive growth agenda (objectives of the PDO). Policy reforms under the DPF were aligned to the government’s priorities

at the time of the operation. Given the COIVD-19 pandemic hit the country during implementation of the Big Four agenda,

the prior actions in the DPF series were aimed to not only support these development goals but to also to accelerate

reforms for a green, resilient, and sustainable recovery from the COVID-19 shock. Recognizing the need to address the

pandemic, the government launched a post-COVID Economic Recovery Strategy (ERS), designed to mitigate its adverse

socioeconomic effects and facilitate opening of the economy, accelerate the economic recovery, and attain higher and

sustained economic growth. Consequently, the DPF pillars were selected based on the need to strengthen fiscal

management to restore fiscal space, enhance operational efficiency in the electricity sector aimed at placing Kenya on an

efficient green energy path, and support governance framework to leverage on the country’s natural and human capital,

including support to its healthcare system.

Pillar 1. Rating: Satisfactory. Fiscal and debt reforms to make spending more transparent and efficient and enhance

domestic debt market performance.

              •   Ensuring clarity and transparency in the primary debt market: Assessment of DPF 1 PA3 and DPF 2 PA3

    in the primary and secondary markets.

33. DPF 1 PA7 and DPF 2 PA7: Kenya’s economy depends heavily on environmental goods and services. Natural capital

accounts for 35 percent of total wealth in Kenya, while 42 percent of GDP and almost 70 percent of total employment are

generated by natural resource-related sectors. Therefore, a critical aspect of the environmental policy reform agenda is

to mainstream climate change mitigation and adaptation considerations both at the county and national government

levels.

34. DPF 2 PA8: At the time of preparation, Kenya lacked standardized land value information. This was a critical

shortcoming in the transparency of the market for land, and resulted in speculative activity in public infrastructure

projects, increasing their costs. The Rating Act and Rating Valuation Act, which provided the legal basis for counties to

determine ratings for land valuation, was outdated. The reform supported the development of a national land value index,

which aimed to overcome these shortcomings.

35. DPF 1 PA 8 & DPF 2 PA9: Kenya is a water-scarce country, with low and declining freshwater resources. In Kenya,

rainfall is unevenly distributed, both spatially and temporally, and over 80 percent of land is arid or semi-arid. Critical

watersheds are degrading fast due to poor land-use practices, deforestation, and encroachment on riparian lands.

Moreover, over 30 percent of the population in Kenya lacked access to basic water services, 35 million people lacked

access to basic sanitation, and four million had no sanitation access at all and practice open defecation. Reforms under

these PAs also supported significant health and climate change adaptation benefits.

     V.    RISK TO SUSTAINABILITY OF DEVELOPMENT OUTCOMES

75. Risks to sustainable development outcomes of this operation arise mostly from economic and environmental

considerations. The operation successfully achieved most of its objectives, as represented by the results indicators.

However, there are still risks for the maintenance of achievement of the programmatic series development outcomes and

potential impacts to the PDO. First, macroeconomic volatility and the weakening of domestic and international demand

might dampen the government’s fiscal consolidation trajectory, increasing the likelihood of affecting pillar #1 of the

operation. Before the February 2024 Eurobond repurchase, Kenya’s ability to access global financial markets was

restrained. Global and local supply shocks, rapid interest rate hikes in advance economies, and global monetary conditions

led to a substantial increase in borrowing costs. Given the lack of diversified sources of foreign exchange inflow the country

has, Kenya experienced severe liquidity challenges during 2023 and the beginning of 2024, which pressured the exchange

rate and pushed inflation upwards, constraining domestic demand. As of Q2.2023/24 of the fiscal year, the government

was missing its fiscal revenue targets which, if persistent, could risk its fiscal consolidation objectives.

76. Moreover, there are risks that increased climate hazards might affect the overall PDO of this operation . Kenya is

highly exposed to climate change. Precipitation fluctuates significantly on an annual basis, and it seems that Kenya will get

wetter over the next few years, affecting its primarily rainfed agricultural sector. Poor, and vulnerable workers will be

differentially impacted by climate shocks, as they rely more on agriculture and less productive jobs as their main source

of income. Climate shocks are compounded by natural resource challenges, with about 85 percent of Kenya’s land area

   VI. LESSONS AND NEXT PHASE





     A. LESSONS LEARNED



Key lessons that emerge from the program experience:

78. Remaining flexible in a highly uncertain environment was key for the success of the operation. While this

operation was being prepared, the world was experiencing a high degree of economic, political, and social uncertainty

due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Even after the initial 2021 recovery and as vaccines being applied, new strains of the

disease were appearing. In this environment, the team adjusted the operation to reflect new government priorities, while

at the same time keeping the focus on building a stronger healthcare system. For example, as described, pillars were

modified as well as some triggers which became prior actions. With regards to health, indicative trigger #9 of the first

operation went from establishing a center for disease control to having a greater mandate as a national public health

institute in prior action #10 of the second operation.

79. Although the size of the policy matrix raised initial concerns, it was an opportunity to build consensus around

policy reforms which had already reached a point of maturity. Given the highly uncertain environment, concerns were

initially raised due to the size of the policy matrix and if the operation was overly ambitious. Still, and adding that prior

actions were successfully met, the proposed reforms brought consensus on policy areas which were already at a point of

maturity and leveraged from both the previous DPO series and strong analytical underpinnings. For example, indicative

trigger #7 and prior action #8 of the first and second part of the operation, respectively, built on a policy area (land reform)

which was less frequently used in DPOs at the time but had been significantly studied and key for Kenya’s environmental

governance framework. Discussions about the reform brought government and non-governmental stakeholders together

to a point of consensus, and the team was able to achieve its objectives.

80. Prioritize the policy dialogue around difficult reform areas. Between the two operations, indicative trigger #4 was

modified due to updated reform priorities from the counterpart, from a comprehensive tariff review to reducing costs and

cutting inefficiencies. At the time, embarking on a full tariff review could have required an upward adjustment to achieve

a full cost-reflective tariff, which was part of the envisaged trigger but still challenging to implement due to ongoing COVID-

19 effects. Going forward and recognizing the high degree of uncertainty, teams could consider prioritizing the dialogue

around sensitive and difficult reform areas upfront, which could support the realization of the proposed policy areas.

    81. It is key to involve key stakeholders throughout of the operation. The GoK was involved throughout the policy

discussion throughout the operation. However, initially, there was a rejection of the implementation of the water reform

           The World Bank

package, and a court case was filed which affected the achievement of the intended results. With the involvement of key

stakeholders, additional constraints were solved through cross-institutional collaboration.

82. The June 2024 youth-led protests opposing tax reforms under the proposed FY2024/25 budget highlight the need

to further strengthen the political and social risk evaluation of reforms supported by future operations. Although not

directly related to WBG operations in this case, these events pose the need to strengthen and confirm the public

consultation process by Government for future reforms and maximize the assent from the population. This is not restricted

to DPFs and could extend to other World Bank financing instruments including PforRs and IPFs. The protests have

strengthened the awareness for greater accountability on needed reforms, such as expenditure control and anti-

corruption measures that could contribute to the achievement of results indicators agreed upon in future DPFs.



     B. NEXT PHASE



83. Successful implementation of reforms will require political and social buy-in as demonstrated by protests calling

for the rejection of the revenue raising measures proposed in the FY24 Finance Bill. The protests were triggered by the

Finance Bill, but extended to calls for scrutiny of conditionalities from multilateral organizations tied to funding. While

strong government ownership was instrumental in the successful implementation of the reforms supported by the DPF,

recent events in the country point to a growing scrutiny from the public, especially the youth on proposals from

government that impact on the wellbeing of the population. DPFs have relied on strong coordination within the

government involving Ministries, Departments and Agencies (MDAs), which has been a key driver of government

ownership. Going forward, there will be need to foster trust from the public on the criticality and necessity of reforms

supported through DPFs.

84. An important factor in the successful execution of the reform program was the strong technical capacity that

exists among the policy making civil servant cadre in the MDAs. There was also a strong analytical foundation for the

choice of prior actions and the benefits of complementary support from Bank operations ( ASA, IPF’s and PFoRs. Going

forward, the strong cooperation and dialogue between the Bank and the government builds a track record of trust that

strengthens the foundation for further policy dialogue and reform.
