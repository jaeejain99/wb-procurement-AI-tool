    I. PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES



           A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL



      Country Context



      1.      In 2018, Ghana was among the ten fastest-growing economies in the world, despite persistent structural

      weaknesses. Annual economic growth was at 6.3 percent on the back of oil production, the inflation rate was down to 9.9

      percent in August 2018, 1 and fiscal consolidation efforts were succeeding. Ghana’s macroeconomic situation had

      improved under the International Monetary Fund’s US$918 million Extended Credit Facility. Following the December 2016

      elections, the new administration demonstrated some early results with macroeconomic reforms. However, economic

      growth was undermined by challenges pertaining to institutional quality and government effectiveness. Specifically,

      reforms to improve the quality of public sector performance and service delivery had not yet yielded results.



      Sector Context



      2.       At appraisal, the public sector was contending with structural, institutional, and governance challenges that

      had hindered the efficient and accountable delivery of public services. The public sector was vast, complex, and suffered

      from ineffective coordination among Ministries, Departments, and Agencies (MDAs), some of which had overlapping

      functions and mandates, as well as differing conditions of service — despite being in the same sector. This occurred most

      notably in the transport sector, which was served by five separate ministries. Over the previous two decades, the state’s

      capacity to deliver public services had weakened, as had accountability structures. According to the Mo Ibrahim Index of

      African Governance, all of Ghana’s governance indicators declined in the decade ending in 2015. The delivery of basic

     administrative services was encumbered by bureaucratic processes that caused excessive delays. For instance, it took over

     a year to obtain a passport. At the same time, citizens faulted the Government’s performance in providing a “reliable

     supply of electricity, water and sanitation services, basic health services, educational needs, and maintenance of

     any contact with a government official for the purposes of obtaining basic services. 3 Furthermore, despite

     automation of some processes, citizens faced constraints in accessing administrative services. Finally, Ghana did not

     have effective monitoring and evaluation (M&E) systems to facilitate accountability, transparency and the effective

     delivery of results.



     3.       The Government of Ghana (GoG) recognized the need to improve governance and launched a new strategy for

     reforming the public sector. The National Public Sector Strategy (NPSRS) for 2018-2023 was launched in 2018. The strategy

     acknowledged that Ghana’s public sector performance “had deteriorated in recent years”, citing factors such as weak

     institutional and human capacity, inadequate resources, lack of accountability, and poor coordination as barriers to

     past reform efforts. The NPSRS served as a foundation for the Public Sector Reform for Results Project (PSRRP), which

     aimed to enhance the speed and quality of service delivery. Two of the strategy’s three pillars anchored the PSRRP:

     Pillar 1 focused on restructuring internal service delivery processes and management frameworks to prioritize

     citizen-cantered approaches. It sought to mainstream delivery models and monitoring and evaluation (M&E)

     systems across Ministries, Departments, and Agencies (MDAs), emphasizing results-oriented reform Pillar 3

     emphasized revising Client Service Charters (CSCs), institutionalizing service delivery standards, and strengthening

     the regulatory framework for public services.4 By aligning these pillars with its objectives, the GoG aimed to create a

     more accountable, efficient, and citizen-focused public sector.



     Bank Rationale



     4.       The PSSRP was aligned with the World Bank’s Country Partnership Strategy (CPS) for fiscal years (FY) 2013–

     16, which was extended to FY18 (Report No. 76369-GH). The CPS had three pillars: (i) improving economic

     institutions; (ii) enhancing competitiveness and job creation; and (iii) protecting the poor and vulnerable. The PSRRP

     supported these objectives by improving upstream public sector performance through strengthened national M&E

     systems and enhancing downstream performance by improving the quality of administrative service delivery.



     5.       The Government’s NPSRS provided a strategic opportunity for the World Bank to support the GoG’s agenda

     to reform and improve public administrative services, which had been in decline. The reforms also aligned with

     the National Coordinated Program for Economic and Social Development Policies (2017-2024), which emphasized

     “deepening democratic governance; reforming and transforming public institutions; and effective management of

     public policy.” 5 The World Bank’s analytical work over the preceding five years highlighted weaknesses in public

     sector management, which negatively impacted human development outcomes and public expenditure. 6 These

     reforms were intended to complement ongoing operations to strengthen the institutional capacity in revenue and

     expenditure management, such as the Ghana Economic Management Strengthening Project (P152171) and the

     Public Financial Management Reform and Improvement Project (P151447).

Theory of Change (Results Chain)



6.      The PSRRP aimed to address two primary challenges: (i) inefficiency in the delivery of selected services by

selected entities; and (ii) limited accountability in the delivery of selected services by selected entities. The Theory

of Change (ToC) at Appraisal was based on the implicit assumption that addressing organizational weaknesses,

introducing performance and accountability tools such as Service Delivery Standards and Client Service Charters, and

strengthening M&E systems across selected entities would enhance accountability and improve the efficiency of

service delivery. Service delivery improvement was to be measured primary by (see figure1). However, Key

weaknesses in the ToC were evident in the implicit assumptions underlying its design. These assumptions failed to

adequately account for significant challenges, including capacity constraints, coordination issues, and the which were

not cognizant of the organizational characteristics of the targeted entities. These factors posed substantial

constraints during implementation, yet the ToC did not incorporate measures to address or mitigate these

constraints effectively. As a results, overcoming these structural and operational barriers was not factored into the

project’s design, limiting its effectiveness during execution.

     Components



     The PSRRP aimed to improve service delivery and enhance accountability in the delivery of public services. The

     project scope primary targeted eight service delivery agencies and their parent ministries, structured under four

     components 7:

     US$3.71 million). This component sought to: (i) strengthen the performance of selected entities, including the Office

     of the Head of the Civil Service [OHCS] and the Public Service Commission [PSC], to achieve their key objectives and

     results; (ii) Enhance the capacity of the Commission on Human Rights and Administrative Justice (CHRAJ) to handle

9.       Component 2: Improving Efficiency, Accountability, and Citizen Engagement in the Delivery of Selected

Services (Estimated Cost US$20.19 million, Actual Cost US$8.31 million). This component addressed key challenges,

including: (i) the lack of streamlined and standardized systems and processes for service delivery and results tracking,

coupled with inadequate and unpredictable budget releases for goods and services; (ii) limited access points and

insufficient institutional capacity within MDAs to formulate and manage efficient, accountable service delivery

processes for citizens and firms; (iii) limited coordination between sector MDAs; and (iv) delays, corruption, the lack

of complaints handling and tracking mechanisms and a general lack of accountability and citizen engagement

concerning key public services across MDAs. Bank support covered: the Driver and Vehicle Licensing Authority

(DVLA); the Ministry of Transport (MoT); the Births and Deaths Registry (BDR); the Ministry of Local Government,

Decentralization and Rural Development (MLGRD); the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA); the Ministry of

Environment, Science, Technology, and Innovation (MESTI); the Passport Office (PO); the Ministry of Foreign Affairs

and Regional Integration (MFA&RI); the Ghana Immigration Service (GIS); the Ministry of Interior (MoI); and the

National Information Technology Agency (NITA).



10.     Disbursement under the project included disbursements based on results referred to as disbursement-

linked indicators (DLIs) or performance-based conditions (PBCs):



        DLI/PBC 1. BDR takes measures to meet the targeted number of births registered within 3 months of the

        event.

        DLI/PBC 2. EPA takes measures for environmental permit issuance and renewal, as well as complaints

        handling for individuals and MSMEs.

        DLI/PBC 3. DVLA takes measures for driver licensing and vehicle registration.

        DLI/PBC 4. Service-Level Agreements are signed between NITA and relevant selected entities.

        DLI/PBC 5. Client Service Charters/Client Service Standards are updated and published on the selected

        entities websites.

        DLI/PBC 6. Prepare and implement a Gender Strategy and Action Plan applicable to all entities under the

        PSC and Office of the Head of the Civil Service (OHCS) and train selected entities to implement it.

        DLI/PBC 7. Complaints received by independent, third-party project-level complaint handling entity, and

        selected entities that have resolved such complaints within the specified times.

        DLI/PBC 8. Semi-annual public meetings held by the Office of the Senior Minister.





11.      Component 3: Improving Monitoring and Evaluation (Estimated Cost US$3.5 million, Actual Cost US$2.07

million). This component was designed to improve M&E across the government by: (i) preparing and implementing

M&E policy and performance measurement of the government’s results framework (RF) for priority programs; (ii)

developing and monitoring real-time performance of the MDAs and MMDAs; (iii) strengthening M&E capacity across

MDAs and MMDAs; (iv) implementing activities to enable stakeholders to interact with citizens and civil society; and

(v) conducting annual citizen assessment surveys and publishing the results.



12.     Component 4: Project Management, Coordination and Public Reporting (Estimated Cost US$2.55 million,

Rationale for Changes and Their Implication on the Original Theory of Change



17.      The first restructuring aimed to improve PDO indicators to better measure progress under the project in

alignment with the ToC. However, the restructuring did not fully address the capacity constraints and coordination

challenges identified early in implementation. For instance, the project retained the parent ministries, MLGRD and

MESTI, despite their activities being tangential to achieving the PDO. This exacerbated the PMU’s coordination

challenges faced. Additionally, revisions to PBC 2 introduced complexities by requiring the Passport Office (PO) to

issue passports and ensure their delivery or collection - tasks that exceeded the PO’s legal mandate. Nonetheless,

the restructuring helped simplify the project and accelerate progress, as evidenced by increased disbursements

following the changes.



18.     The second and third restructurings were justified by the need to extend key procurement processes and

cancel that could not be committed before the project’s closure. The restructurings did not affect the ToC. The

primary reason for extending the project’s closing date by six months — from December 31, 2023, to June 20, 2024

— was to recover almost two years of implementation time lost between May 2019 and the first restructuring, when

project implementation had been slow. The extension allowed the client to complete activities that were already

underway and deemed feasible within the short timeframe. Historically, disbursements under the PSRRP were slow.

The cancellation of the US$8 million became necessary when it became clear that the client could not absorb

additional funds within the remaining project period, even with the extension. Key activities, such as procuring

biometric passport printers faced significant delays and were no longer feasible. Dropping certain PBCs and

cancelling associated funds became unavoidable, especially given that by May 2024, no PBC disbursements had been

made.

E. OTHER OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS (IF ANY)





Gender



43.      The project did not have a specific gender tag, as this was not required at the time of preparation.

However, the implementation of the gender action plans promoted gender equality. In the eight entities that

implemented these plans, significant efforts were made to address gender inequalities and redefine gender roles

and relations. For instance, gender mainstreaming has become a core strategy for achieving gender equality within

Institutional Strengthening



44.      PSRRP activities were inherently designed to strengthen the institutional frameworks of the selected

entities. The project laid Key foundations to: (i) improve efficiency and accountability in the delivery of select

services through business process reengineering; (ii) establish and consistently implement a performance

management system; (iii) leverage digital solutions for enhance service delivery; and (iv) upgrade and ensure

compliance with the CSCs. Overall, the project introduced fundamental institutional reforms that require expansion

and consolidation over time to sustain the institutional capacity built through its implementation.



45.      Fostering Cross-institutional learning and coordination. The PSRRP significantly strengthened cross-

institutional learning and coordination. The project effectively facilitated platforms where entities could exchange

ideas, share lessons, and discuss what worked and what did not Quarterly progress reviews, attended by technical

teams and heads of entities, enhanced communication and fostered collaboration. These reviews promoted healthy

competition as entities sought to emulate good practices, creating a spirit of continuous improvement and

maximizing the benefits of the reforms. Additionally, the cross-institutional dialogue enabled entities to understand

the political economy of common challenges and collaborate on solutions. If sustained, the improved coordination

under the PSRRP has the potential to deliver long-term benefits for the country’s institutional and governance

capacity.









III. KEY FACTORS AFFECTED IMPLEMENTATION AND OUTCOME



A. KEY FACTORS DURING PREPARATION



46.      The three key factors that affected implementation and outcome are as follows: First is the delay in

effectiveness. The project was prepared over a twelve-month period and approved by the Executive Board of the

World Bank on November 23, 2018. However, the project did not become effective until May 8, 2019, almost six

months later. This delay in effectiveness was compounded by subsequent implementation delays, from which the

project never fully recovered. Second, the GoG had an opportunity to access a project preparation advance (PPA).

Although the GoG was aware of the option to access a PPA, the request was not submitted early in the preparation



                                                                                                              Page 16

           The World Bank

           Public Sector Reform for Results Project (P164665)                                                                ICR DOCUMENT





      phase. As a result, the GoG did not secure a PPA. This missed opportunity meant that anticipated implementation

      challenges, particularly those related to procurement and capacity identified during the Bank’s preparation

      assessments were not addressed early. If a PPA had been acquired and used effectively, it could have mitigated

      these challenges by initiating critical procurement activities ahead of time. Finally, there was a high TTL turnover.

      The project experienced multiple changes in TTLs, starting with the replacement of the initial TTL during the

      preparation phase. The new TTL finalized preparation and appraised the project. Several subsequent TTL changes

      occurred during implementation. While turnover is not uncommon in Bank operations, these transitions caused

      disruptions. Counterparts needed time to establish trust with incoming TTLs, and the new TTLs required time to

      understand the implementation context and the political economy dynamics. These factors adversely affected

      project implementation and may have ultimately impacted project outcomes.



      B. KEY FACTORS DURING IMPLEMENTATION



     (a) Factors subject to the control of the government/ and or implementing entities



     47. First, the GoG did not provide the strong leadership and coordination necessary to rally the implementing

     entities to and ensure the delivery of agreed commitments. A different implementation mechanism with sufficient

     and lasting authority could have guaranteed stronger coordination and management of the reform, leading to better

     outcomes. During the project preparation, it was understood that the OSM, (later OSPA), possessed adequate

     political capital and policy leverage needed to lead the transformation of the public sector. The OSM had led the

     preparation of the National Public Sector Reform Strategy (NPSRS) and was seen as the custodian of the reforms.

     However, the anticipated coordination and supervision role that the OSM was expected to carry out did not

     materialize, partly due to capacity issues. Over time, the OSM's power began to dwindle, and the political capital

     associated with this office continued to decrease, culminating in a shift to the role of Senior Presidential Advisor

     (SPA). This reorganization significantly affected the OSM’s ability to lead the expected reforms and, in turn, impacted

     the success of the project.



     48. Second, procurement delays at the Central Tender Review Committee (CTRC) level adversely affected

     implementation. Ghana’s local procurement processes are cumbersome and led to delays in project implementation.

     By law, procurements above a threshold of 1 million Ghanaian Cedis (US$ equivalent) are referred to the CTRC for

     review and approval. Because of the low threshold nearly all procurement items required the approval of the CTRC.14

     The CTRC’s sittings were conducted on an ad hoc basis, creating uncertainty about the timeline for the completion

     of the approval process. This in turn was exacerbated by the fact that the CTRC had a backlog of reviews. For instance,

     the consultancy for the gender action plans was approved by the CTRC after a 7-months delay.



     (b) Factors subject to World Bank control



     49. The World Bank could have controlled three factors that adversely affected project implementation and

     outcomes. First, the high TTL turnover. The project experienced several changes in Task Team Leaders (TTLs) during

     both preparation and implementation. These frequent changes interrupted implementation as new TTLs took time

     to understand the dynamics of the context. Similarly, counterparts took time to build trust and confidence in the new

     TTLs, each of whom brought a different management style. Second, the Bank could have been more proactive in

     addressing implementation challenges. Issues were identified early after effectiveness, but no action was taken to

       IV. BANK PERFORMANCE, COMPLIANCE ISSUES, AND RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME





        A. QUALITY OF MONITORING AND EVALUATION (M&E)





        M&E Design



        51.      The project’s Results Framework (RF) formed the foundation for monitoring results. Each implementing

        entity was responsible for providing the PMU with data and information about implementation progress and results.

        This data was collated by a dedicated M&E specialist within the PMU, whose overall responsibility was to track and

        report on the status of results indicators. Additionally, an Independent Verification Agent (IVA) was contracted to

        confirm and verify the results of the PBCs. The RF had four revised PDO indicators. However, some indicators were

        repeated at the Intermediate Results Indicators (IRI) level and were also reflected as PBCs, creating overlaps and

        inconsistencies. For instance, an entity could achieve a PDO-level indicator (which had a lower target) but fail to

        achieve the corresponding IRI and PBC (which had higher targets). For example, at the PDO level, the target for

        issuing driver’s licenses within 45 days of passing the driving test was 60 percent, which was achieved. However,

        the same indicator at the IRI and PBC levels had a target of 90 percent, which was not achieved. Furthermore,

        certain indicators were misaligned with the mandates of the implementing entities. For example, the Passport

        Office (PO) was evaluated on the issuance of passports, but the indicator conflated issuance with delivery or

        collection, responsibilities that do not fall under the PO's mandate. Similarly, a complaints-related indicator required

        service users to file grievances about administrative services with the Commission on Human Rights and

        Administrative Justice (CHRAJ), whereas CHRAJ clarified during ICR discussions that such complaints should be

        directed to the respective service entities, not CHRAJ.



        52.   The RF lacked provisions for monitoring g and evaluating key aspects of the project beyond results. Also,

        the M&E design did not effectively integrate the RF, the entities’ existing M&E systems, planned surveys, and

M&E Implementation



53.      The implementation of M&E faced significant challenges, with data being scarce, difficult to collect, and

adjust the pace of implementation, or address project performance issues identified early on. M&E implementation

was largely limited to occasional reporting on progress or lack thereof against indicators. While the PMU developed

and shared results stories for achieved indicators and milestones with stakeholders, including the public, there is

little evidence in project documentation to suggest that M&E data was strategically or operationally utilized. The

data was not leveraged to guide project implementation, influence policymaking, or engage clients in meaningful

about the entire M&E system is not comprehensive, and there is little evidence to demonstrate that the full M&E

system was implemented and utilized. Given the significant shortcomings in the M&E system, the overall rating of

the quality of M&E is Modest.



    B. ENVIRONMENTAL, SOCIAL, AND FIDUCIARY COMPLIANCE



56.          The PSRRP was rated as Category C for environmental and social factors at appraisal. Therefore, no

environmental and social standards policies were triggered. The project did not include activities that could cause

social risk or negatively affect the population, or any activities that required land acquisition or caused any physical

or economic displacement.



57.         The PMU’s procurement capacity was assessed at appraisal and deemed to be insufficient, and the

procurement risk for the project was rated High. This was due to largely to the PMU’s lack of experience and

inadequate knowledge of the Bank’s procurement procedures and the numerous procurement packages under the

project. To mitigate the risks, measures were put in place to provide continuous capacity building for the PMU,

including formal procurement training at the Ghana Institute of Management and Public Administration, as well as

      World Bank-organized training and seminars. However, despite this, there were various procurement-related

      challenges during implementation that negatively affected the project. Some of these have already been

      documented in previous sections of the ICR. They include: (i) the PMU’s poor track record of preparing quality

      procurement documents and leading the implementation of the procurement process; (ii) opaque procurement

      processes that often disregarded the Bank’s policies and procedures; and (iii) poor procurement planning, including

      not publishing contract awards; inordinate delays in launching and finalizing procurements; insufficient

      transparency and competition in procurement (as evidenced by the low number of bids and expressions of

      58.     Financial management (FM) performance was mostly unsatisfactory during the implementation of the

      the beneficiary agencies; and (vi) significant variances between actual and budgeted expenditures, and fixed asset

      management which were never fully resolved, thereby justifying the high-risk rating that was provided at appraisal.

      FM staffing was somewhat adequate, and the external audit function was strong, despite some delays in submitting

      reports. The 2024 external audit report will be submitted no later than June 30, 2025.



      C. BANK PERFORMANCE





      Quality at Entry



      59.      The PSSRP, which was aligned with the CPS at the time, was credibly informed by analytical work. The

      project’s strategic relevance was justified by the GoG’s own strategic documents, including the NPSRS which

      underscored the need for reforms in the public sector to improve service delivery. Preparation involved

      consultations with various stakeholders. Effort was made to embed the implementation arrangements under the

      auspices of a high-level government official- Office of the Senior Minister- reflecting the Bank’s understanding of

      the reform context at the time and the need for strategic stewardship. All the required assessments including

      environment and social, procurement and financial management assessments, were conducted to confirm the

      availability of the necessary structures for implementation and the Bank gave recommendations to remedy

      shortcomings, including in those related to capacity for procurement and financial management. The GoG did not

      implement some of Bank’s suggestions, including the need to hire outside professionals where there was limited

      capacity. The GoG preferred to use civil servants in project implementation roles.

      restructured on the heels of their arrival and implementation began to improve.16 The ground Task Team held

D. RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME





62.    The modest outcomes achieved under the project in pursuit of the development objective are at high risk.

The project only laid the foundation for the achievement of the development objective. Many key activities that

were envisaged under the PSRRP remain unimplemented and/or incomplete. In fact, during the ICR discussions,

almost all the entities requested funds and time to complete the activities and the sustainability plans that were

shared seemed to be centered around completing the activities, as opposed to sustaining or building on the

outcomes or impacts that these activities should have had under the PSRRP.



63.      The reforms under the PSRRP are not one-shot reforms, and more work is needed. Structural reforms —

such as the streamlining of processes, reorganizing key functions, and establishing a performance management

system — take time to yield tangible results. Furthermore, they need to be sustained and enhanced over time. It is

hoped that the catalytic effects of these reforms are harnessed as the GoG moves forward with improving efficiency

and accountability in service delivery. Training provided under the project strengthened the capacities of key

personnel in the public sector administration. However, training and capacity building need to be continuously

updated to keep pace with new technologies and changes. With funds from other development partners and indeed,

using its own budget, it is possible for the GoG to capitalize on key reform elements initiated under the PSRRP.



      V.    LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS



64.    Project design should include sufficient lead time for implementation and anticipate delays that the Bank

does not have control over. The project was designed to be implemented within a five-year period. The project was

later affected by implementation delays resulting from delays in effectiveness and the impact of an election.

Additionally, procurement delay is widely known as a problem across the portfolio. These problems could have been

anticipated and measures built in to ensure lower impact on project implementation and outcomes. Secondly,

selection of participating entities should be undertaken systematically within a strong methodology that ensures

that only entities that are ready to implement are included. Such a methodology should include strict and

transparent selection criteria to ensure that selected entities have the desire and capacity to drive change and that

proposed changes are part of institutional plans. A national strategy for reform does not substitute agency level

ownership and commitment to reform. Prioritizing and sequencing activities appropriately based on the operating

environment and the client’s capacity is also key. Implementing entities under the project were selected based on

functions rather than demand from the institution. The lack of commitment was evident in the low level of

outcomes. Thirdly, Task Team should be realistic an introduce restructuring as soon as it is clear that there are

implementation challenges related to the design on the reform context. Leverage restructuring opportunities to

salvage poorly performing projects can help turn around struggling project in good time. The PSRRP showed signs

of struggle very early on during implementation, but the Task Team did not initiate restructuring until two years

later. Fourth, PPAs can be strategically used to strengthen project preparation and lay the foundations for strong

implementation. While the Bank offered the opportunity for a PPA, the GoG was reluctant to, and ultimately did

not, take advantage of the PPA. The PPA would have been useful to initiate the procurement packages such as

those for passport printers earlier or to support capacity building of implementation teams.

ANNEX 4. EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS



The economic analysis employed an incremental approach, which compared scenarios ‘with’ and ‘without’ the

project (counterfactual scenario). The economic analysis considers the direct and indirect benefits and costs

associated with supporting Selected Entities, to improve their organizational efficiency and accountability to the

citizens. The analysis evaluates the net economic benefits to Selected Entities and the citizens, the financial viability

of the project, and the efficiency of project implementation. Where data is available at ICR, the efficiency of the

project is estimated using the discounted cash flow approach to calculate the NPV and IRR of the project.



Following three restructurings, US$24 million was allocated to the project million, and of this amount US$21.23

was disbursed by the end of the project, representing an overall disbursement rate of 60.7 percent. The shortfall

in the execution rate of project funds was due to part of the loan being cancelled, low capacity of the PMU, and the

weak commitment for implementation. The reallocation of additional funds to Component 4 increased its share of

actual project funding to 25.3 percent compared to the original estimate of 7.8 percent in the PAD. This was intended

to further strengthen the capacity of the Project Management Unit (PMU), to ensure effective implementation.



The project generated efficiency gains by supporting Selected Entities with ICT infrastructure and simplified

business processes and procedures. The deployment and maintenance of equipment needed for ICT infrastructure

and simplified business procedures due to the e-enabled services and the ability to access services and information

through multiple means (including through mobile applications) resulted in reduced revenue leakages from official

fees for public services, which distort economic behavior. The economic costs of the project involved government

staff time spent on project implementation, as well as training, communications, outreach, complaints handling and

DLI/ PBC verification – all undertaken as part of project implementation. However, the expected economic benefits

are likely to outweigh the economic costs of the project.



Under Component 1, the Selected Entities (OHCS, PSC, DVLA, BDR, PO) were expected to improve their

accountability to citizens. However, the economic benefits of implementing measures under Component 1, were

not captured in the economic analysis, because of non-implementation of key activities aimed at strengthening the

accountability of SEs and the lack of data on administrative cost savings.



The economic and financial analysis excluded the estimation of the returns on activities under Component 1,

because of the lack of relevant data and non-achievement of the PDO target on accountability. Rolling out the

updated CSCs increased operational costs; however, this was likely offset by lower administrative costs to the

Selected Entities, due to efficiency improvements. However, only 6 (including PSC and OHCS) out of the planned 25

Selected Entities updated and published CSCs on their websites. Additionally, the impact on turnaround time and

cost savings for service delivery by the compliant Selected Entities, were not documented during project

implementation, since the economic benefits of implementing measures under Component 1, were neither tracked

nor quantified during the project implementation. Additionally, key performance indicator on accountability ̶ for at

least 5 selected public services, 90 percent of service users are sent a text message with the receipt number/tracker

numbers, and the date of delivery of service ̶ was not achieved.



Under Component 2 DVLA, PO, BDR and were expected to leverage the streamlined business processes and the

provision of e-enabled service to improve organizational efficiency, quick turnaround time and reduction of

informal payments by citizens. The PO was supposed to achieve a target of 60 percent of applicants receiving their

passport within 21 days of submitting their bio data. The DVLA achieved a target of 60 percent of applicants notified

and issued with drivers’ licenses within 45 days of passing the driving test. At the BDR, there was an increase in the

There was some cost saving for citizens because of expected improvements in operational efficiency to Selected

Entities such as the DVLA, BDR, and PO. The PSRRP-supported activities to improve PO service delivery were

intended to reduce formal payments and other out-of-pocket costs for applicants and greater certainty and

predictability in service delivery times, contributing to cost savings. Cost savings were also made through services

provided by the BDR and DVLA, but these are unlikely to have the potential scale or impact as services provided by

the PO.



Adhering to service standards as envisaged in updated CSCs and improved customer orientation of existing public

services reduced applicants’ time and travel costs (by reducing the number of visits to multiple offices/agencies to

access), particularly for those living in remote areas and from low-income groups.



Economic costs for citizens also declined as it was no longer necessary to pay informal fees to intermediaries and

other agents to access services. As a growing number of applicants had access to services within the prescribed

timeframes specified in CSCs, the payment of facilitation fees and bribes became unnecessary. The distorting impact

of bribes causes lower use of official services which accounts for the real economic cost (the value of the reduced

services received by individuals and firms relative to the economic cost of the resources used to supply these

services). Thus, eliminating or reducing bribes represents a substantial improvement in economic welfare of

individuals and firms. Individuals also benefitted from improved transparency and access to information for better

decision-making.

the period of implementation (2019-2024). The financial benefits accruing directly to Selected Entities (BDR, PO, and

DVLA) comprise: First, (i) savings from the reduction of administrative costs in providing PSRRP-supported services

such as issuance/renewal of passports, drivers’ licenses and vehicle registrations, and registration of births and

deaths and issuance of birth and death certificates. Second, (ii) increases in revenue because of increased volumes

of service delivery from the Selected Entities. These improvements stem from stronger organizational performance

and e-business enabled operations by the three front-line Selected Entities.



Assumptions: An economy wide rate of return (EWRR) of 12 percent is used as the benchmark for assessing the

viability of the Project. While the PAD did not quantify the efficiency of the Project in terms of IRR and ERR, the ex-

was expected to generate similar financial and economic benefits. The analysis relies on the revenue stream from

BDR, DVLA and PO to calculate the IRR. The average unit price for a birth certificate, passport and a driver’s license

are Ghs 35, Ghs100-Ghs500 and Ghs530 respectively; the cost of passport issuance/ renewal was at Ghs 100 per unit

at the inception of the project until it was adjusted to Ghs 500 per unit in 2022.



The analysis estimates that the total number of citizens benefiting from the services of the Selected Entities (BDR,

DVLA and PO) increased, largely because of improved performance DVLA and BDR. This is largely due to the

expansion in the volume of service delivery arising from improved performance of DVLA and BDR. The incremental

The benefits expected from improving accountability in the delivery of services by Selected Entities cannot be

for monitoring the relevant results indicators to track the benefits of the project makes it difficult to quantify the

benefits derived.



Implementation efficiency



The PSRRP had several challenges that led to the slow rate of implementation during the first half of the project’s

life cycle (2019-2022). This was largely due to the weak capacity of both the PMU and the implementing entities, the

frequent changes in procurement staff and the task team leadership, on the World Bank’s side. After the first 2.5

years of project implementation, disbursement was only 16.9 percent of originally approved project fund of



                                                                                                                                                          Page 39

     The World Bank

     Public Sector Reform for Results Project (P164665)                                                 ICR DOCUMENT





US$35million. Restructuring of the project helped to fast-track project implementation during the second half of the

project. This is attributable to the following measures: removing three selected entities, reducing project funds by

US$11million and extending the closing date by six months. Total disbursements reached Ghs 120million (US$21.23

million), representing a disbursement rate of 98.7 percent compared to total restructured funds available, at the

close of the project.



The impact of implementation performance on efficiency is assessed by comparing IRR under different scenarios

to the benchmark discount rate of 12 percent. The financial analysis indicates that the project was viable, if the

actual disbursement of US$21.23 million at closing is used to calculate the efficiency ratios. The NPV and IRR are Ghs

60million and 18 percent respectively. Without the partial cancellation of the loan, the use of the original project

cost of US$35 million yields a lower IRR of 15 percent compared to the base case scenario of 24 percent.



Overall, efficiency is rated as Modest. The incremental economic gains attributable to the Project are mixed: The

delay in project implementation during the first half of the project cycle undermined the optimal efficiency level the

project could have generated. Activities aimed at improving the efficiency of the Selected Entities partly contributed

to the achievement of the PDO, while activities aimed at improving the accountability of Selected Entities had limited

impact on the achievement of the PDO. The DVLA and BDR contributed the most to achieving the PDO targets and

ensuring that the IRR of the project exceeded the minimum benchmark discount rate of 12 percent. This could have

been higher if the target on the issuance of passports had been achieved.
