I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT



      A.        Country Context



             Nepal is a landlocked country of about 29 million people. Most people (80 percent) live in rural areas,

      although the urbanization rate (about 6 percent) is higher than the regional average. The poverty headcount (at

      the international poverty line of US$1.90 per day) was 17.4 percent in 2019, down from 30.1 percent in 2014. 1

      The country’s Human Development Index (HDI) is 0.602, which ranks 142nd in the world for reporting countries.

      The inequality adjusted HDI is 0.446, and life expectancy is 70.8 years.2

             Despite various challenges, Nepal’s economy demonstrated impressive growth and resilience over the

      past decade when faced with a wide variety of shocks. During FY2012-19, Nepal’s economy performed

      reasonably well despite being hit by three large exogenous shocks: the 2015 earthquake, 2016 trade disruptions,

      and 2017 floods. A fourth shock, the COVID-19 pandemic, derailed the strong growth trajectory established over

      the previous three years. Economic growth contracted by 2.1 percent in FY20, the first contraction since FY1983,

      as a nationwide lockdown from March to July 2020 imposed to curtail the spread of the virus significantly

      affected all sectors of the economy. The economy is estimated to have grown by 1.8 percent in FY21 even

      though renewed containment measures were imposed in the fourth quarter of the fiscal year (FY). Agriculture,

      contributing over one-fifth of nominal gross domestic product (GDP), has been a bright spot, registering 2.2 and

      2.7 percent growth in FY20 and FY21, respectively, on the back of favorable summer monsoons. After

      contracting during FY20, the industry and service sectors are estimated to have grown by only 0.9 and 1.6

      percent in FY21, respectively. The World Bank 2020 South Asia Region COVID-19 phone monitoring survey shows

      that 45 percent of those who recovered from a job loss have switched sectors and taken jobs with lower earnings

      and skill requirements, indicating that many households have been pushed to marginally above or below the

      poverty line.

            The COVID-19 pandemic derailed the economy, but partial recovery is under way. The fiscal deficit

      narrowed to 4.6 percent of GDP in FY21. Economic growth is projected to recover gradually to 4.1 percent by

      FY23. The baseline forecast projects a gradual medium-term recovery, with growth accelerating from 3.7

      percent in FY22 to 5.8 percent by FY24. The baseline assumes (a) no new nationwide strict containment

      measures are imposed; (b) a near complete vaccination of the eligible population by the end of FY22 (81.7

      percent of the population ages 18 and higher have received full doses of vaccine by March 25, 2022); and (c) a

      gradual increase in international migration and tourist arrivals, reaching pre-pandemic levels by FY24.

      Vaccination deployment is expected to unleash pent-up demand for most service subsectors. Industry sector

      growth is projected to be supported by increased hydropower production including from the recently completed

      Upper Tamakoshi plant. However, agricultural growth is projected to decelerate in FY22, reflecting a decline in

        The war in Ukraine is expected to impact Nepal’s economy through commodity prices . Nepal’s trade

with Ukraine is limited to less than two percent of total imports―including mostly sunflower oil, dried peas, and

and provincial elections are expected in late 2022. Local elections were recently concluded in May 2022. At the

sub-national level, funds, functions, and staff continue to be managed by the seven provinces and 753 local

governments for which legislation, institutions, and administrative procedures are being formalized as

constitutionally prescribed. Meanwhile, the federal government has been streamlined with a focus on national

policies and oversight.



B.      Sectoral and Institutional Context

       Increasing regional connectivity and improving trade facilitation is critical for Nepal to build back

better. Although a gradual economic recovery is underway in Nepal after it suffered an economic contraction

from the COVID-19 pandemic, additional measures are needed to rebuild its economic resilience in the medium-

and long-term. To build back stronger, Nepal has prioritized regional connectivity and trade facilitation to unlock

its economic potential, while strengthening its institutions in ways that recalibrate its development towards a

Green, Resilient, and Inclusive Development (GRID) approach. According to a recent United Nations -sponsored

study, a 40 percent reduction in trade costs in Nepal could yield welfare gains of 56 percent to its GDP, and

intraregional export gains of 106 percent to its total exports.3 The main drivers of high cost and inefficiencies in

trade and transport in Nepal are: (i) low levels of technology adoption in trade facilitation; (ii) inadequate

transport and logistics infrastructure; and (iii) policy, regulatory, and procedural impediments that have

constrained cross-border movement. Solving these challenges in line with Nepal’s GRID agenda is thus critical

to unlocking the growth potential from connectivity and trade in a way that provides resilience against climate

change risks, natural disasters, and biodiversity loss.

       Addressing Nepal’s low level of technology adoption in trade facilitation is critical considering the

current lack of efficiency in trade processes and systems. Opportunities exist for Nepal to reduce trade costs

and improve competitiveness by accelerating efforts to facilitate and digitalize trade procedures. Cross-border

paperless trade could result in savings of over US$140 million in annual trade transaction costs. By 2019, Nepal

had implemented only 2.1 percent of all measures included in the World Trade Organization Trade Facilitation

Agreement (WTO TFA) ratified in 2017 and ranked 112th of 116 countries for implemented measures.4 A lack of

trade digitization not only runs against the need for reducing face-to-face interaction in the context of the

COVID-19 pandemic, but also adds complexity, delays border clearance, provides opportunities for corruption,

and hinders private sector participation. Costs associated with obtaining, submitting, and checking documents

by different jurisdictions can account for as much as 3 percent of the total costs of trade.

        Building Nepal’s sustainable, green, inclusive, and climate-resilient transport and trade infrastructure

is a priority in view of the urgent need to:

a) Open Nepal’s connectivity. More than 95 percent of Nepal’s trade is currently via land and 60 percent of

   Nepal’s trade is either with India or is routed through India, the majority passing through three cross-border

   locations5 and two regional corridors.6 Trade in goods accounts for 40 percent of Nepal’s GDP. Inefficiencies

   in connectivity and delays have directly impacted the competitiveness of Nepal’s exports and economy.

b) Upgrade Nepal’s longest trade corridor—the East-West Highway (EWH). With 95 percent of the land-

   traded goods passing through the EWH and the Kathmandu-Birgunj-Kolkata corridor,7 the South Asia Sub-

   Regional Economic Cooperation (SASEC) Operational Plan identified the upgrading of the EWH as a priority

   project to enhance regional connectivity. The Asian Development Bank (ADB) and the World Bank are

   upgrading the eastern side of the EWH, and the World Bank has committed to support the upgrading of the

   western part between Butwal and Gaddachauki. The road is part of Asian Highway 2, which connects Nepal

   to India through the Dodhara Chandani border point on the western border of Nepal. Through link roads,

   the EWH also connects to India via its main cross-border points in Biratnagar, Birgunj, Bhairahawa, and

   Nepalgunj. More efficient highway corridor development for seamless vehicle movement and congestion

   reduction is critical to help significantly reduce greenhouse gas (GHG)8 emissions. Inclusive development as

   well as regeneration of the particularly dense and unplanned urban areas from Butwal to Chanauta (i.e., the

   project corridor)9 are also needed to provide communities with economic opportunities and safer

   infrastructure that is inclusive of non-motorized transport.

c) Provide climate-resilient transport and logistics infrastructure and services. The Intergovernmental Panel

   on Climate Change suggests that Himalayan regions like Nepal will continue to experience significant

   changes in weather patterns due to climate change. Historical and recent data collected via the World Bank’s

   Climate and Disaster Risk Screening tool further confirm the high level of risk for transport and trade

   infrastructure pertaining to extreme precipitation, floods, landslides, wildfires, and increasing

   temperatures. Increasing temperatures escalate the instances of cracks on roads from increased fatigue of

   the pavement. Major highways are blocked for weeks at a time10 or destroyed by landslides, mudslides,

   debris flow, and floods, including the section of the EWH from Butwal to Chanauta, which lies in the

   geologically fragile and climate-sensitive regions of the Nepal Siwalik. Climate-related impacts and damage

   to transport infrastructure along the EWH severely disrupt transport flows and services, resulting in an

   increased cost of trading and lack of access to jobs, schools, hospitals, etc. The productivity of key economic

   sectors, such as agriculture, is compromised by limited access to all-weather roads. Furthermore,

   continuous fragmentation of the ecosystems (including landslide zones but also forests, wildlife, etc.) along

   the highway corridor adversely impacts its resilience and sustainability. A more integrated planning

   approach at the landscape level for a green and climate-resilient corridor development is required.

d) Develop gender-responsive infrastructure. There is a lack of gender-responsive infrastructure in Nepal,

   which hinders women’s accessibility to the economic and mobility benefits from improved road

       Improving Nepal’s policy and regulatory framework for effective freight and people movement remains

a challenge, with:

a) Cumbersome border processes and documentation compliance requirements. In Nepal, about 30 percent

   of consignments are processed through “green lane” facilitation. However, risk management is not

   improving clearance times for low-risk traders, given the minimal difference between green and yellow

   lanes, since both require documents to be submitted and endorsed. Border delays continue outside

   regulatory processes, such as in cargo handling, movement, and storage. Complicated and non-transparent

   tariff and/or nontariff measures also affect the free flow of goods and services across borders.

b) Sanitary and phytosanitary systems not in line with latest international standards. To expand agriculture

   exports, investments are required in infrastructure, equipment, and human resources to certify that goods

   meet the required standards of major regional and global export markets. However, laboratories in Nepal

   are not internationally accredited and, as a result, their trading partners, including India, do not recognize

   their certifications. Consequently, traders undergo costly and time-consuming inspections in accredited

   laboratories in India. Currently, samples of Nepalese export products are sent for testing in laboratories in

   India, which takes a minimum of nine days, but often up to three or four weeks.

      Regional to national level benefits and poverty reduction. Nepal’s participation in the ACCESS MPA

Program is expected to enhance trade between Nepal, India, and Bangladesh by removing bottlenecks and

strengthening weak links at selected border points and along the main identified trade corridors. This project

focuses on the EWH corridor (with an overall focus on the western section from Butwal to Gaddachauki at the

western border with India), which interconnects with regional transport routes integral to freight traffic, and

key border points between Nepal and India. The proposed activities will help reduce the cost of trade,

considering in particular a reduction of climate-related disruption costs along the EWH. The project is designed

to support economic growth and alleviate poverty by: (i) supporting innovative measures in line with the GRID

agenda; and (ii) ensuring that increased cross-border trade and overall benefits generated from regional

transport infrastructure and trade development benefit the country and Nepal’s poorest communities, as poor

people in countries of eastern South Asia are often disconnected from global and regional—or even local—value

C.       Project Beneficiaries

       The project is expected to have physical, economic, social, and environmental benefits at the regional,

national, and local levels. The project will contribute to unlocking Nepal’s economic potential through improved

connectivity and trade, both between the districts and provinces along which the EWH crosses as well as

regionally between Nepal and the other countries in eastern South Asia (third-country trade). The project will

lead to an expanding economy and economic integration through increase in access to Indian and Bangladeshi

markets. Local economies, producers, and consumers, along the EWH will also benefit from faster access to

agricultural and industrial markets, and services (healthcare, schools etc.). The project will support the GoN in

adopting innovative GRID approaches, systems, tools, and capacities for transport and trade development. It

will enhance not only the skills and systems of government agencies and departments, but also their outreach

as well as collaborative planning and implementation.

       Road users and local communities will benefit from a safer transport network and overall improved

connectivity, including, in particular, vulnerable road users, pedestrians, cyclists, and motorcyclists. The

communities near the green resilient urban bridge will benefit from an improved urban living environment,

social and cultural spaces, and increased income generation. Communities will benefit from job opportunities

and skills development during the civil, operational, and maintenance works. These benefits will be particularly

inclusive for women and socially excluded communities through the adoption of universal, gender-sensitive

designs for infrastructure and access to jobs, as the project is supporting the creation of women-led businesses.

     implementation progress, and facilitate coordination among the three IAs.

Economic Analysis

      The economic analysis focuses on the main infrastructure investment under the project, along the BGC

section. The implementation of more efficient digital trade systems, which will be developed under the project

and piloted at Birgunj and Bhairahawa border points, will also contribute to the overall economic benefits and

reduction of carbon emissions by helping decrease transaction times and increase freight throughput.

       BGC section. Economic evaluation for this section of the highway was carried out using a social cost-

benefit analysis and comparing two scenarios: (i) without the project, with only normal maintenance to the

existing road, and (ii) with the project, upgrading it from the current narrow two lanes to an improved safe and

climate-resilient four-lane road with paved shoulders and service lanes. This was done over a period of 30 years16

and at a project discount rate of 12 percent with suitable assumptions for traffic growth (including the increase

in freight throughput) for various categories of vehicles during different time periods. The benefits of the

reduction in road user costs of motorized traffic and savings in journey times of passengers and goods were

estimated using the HDM-4 Model.

for the Gorusinghe–Chanauta (GC) road section, to 79-90 km per hour for both road sections, resulting in an

increased level of service. Reaching optimal travel speed and smoother traffic flow will thus result in a reduction

in fuel consumption over time. Consequently, carbon emissions are expected to initially increase by 22,652 tons

E.         Climate Adaptation and Mitigation

      Climate considerations were at the core of this project design. The key risks identified by the Climate and

Disaster Risk Screening are summarized in the context section. Project activities were defined and prioritized

with the key objective of addressing identified climate change challenges, as presented in detail in the Climate

Technical Note.

       Climate adaptation. Considering the high climate vulnerability level and recent severe climate-related

disruptions and damages faced on the EWH,22 this project was carefully designed to support and finance

transformational climate-resilient and green infrastructure development along the EWH corridor. Maximizing the

investment by financing strategies, measures, and activities to increase its climate resilience is of paramount

importance. Subcomponent 2(c) will finance a resilience planning strategy and related civil works to address

climate risks (such as landslides, flooding, watershed protection measures) at the landscape level, i.e., not just

inside but also mainly outside the RoW (which will be innovative in Nepal, as it is often overlooked in standard

investment projects). Subcomponent 2(b) will address the issue of climate resilience impacting the major urban

bridge infrastructure but most importantly impacting the vulnerable urban communities living in the vicinity of

the bridge, in or along the riverbeds, vulnerable to flooding, in unsanitary conditions. Climate-resilient

engineering measures against flooding, landslides, and temperature risks are incorporated in the design and

construction of all road and bridge infrastructure supported in the RoW under Subcomponents 2(a) and 2(b).

Component 1 will finance the development of a climate and disaster risk management module as well as an

emergency response plan as part of the digital trade systems. Component 3 will finance activities promoting

climate resilience planning best practices through related reforms and capacity building.

       Climate mitigation. The project will contribute to climate change mitigation through reducing emissions

from travel times and congestion by contributing to: (i) the seamless movement of trucks and reduction in truck

idling time (Component 1 and Subcomponent 3(a)); (ii) the provision of service lanes (Subcomponent 2(a)) to

promote non-motorized transport; (iii) inclusive urban design in the green resilient urban bridge development

area (Subcomponent 2(b)); (iv) investment in nature-based solutions, large scale afforestation, and wildfire

F.      Gender Equality

       The gender assessment of the road corridor found that women overall have fewer economic

opportunities compared to men. Though wage-earning and self-employment opportunities have increased,

women’s economic engagement is limited to non-formal enterprises. With the COVID-19 pandemic, the

opportunities and businesses for women were further adversely impacted.23 Overall, only 32 percent (239 of 739)

of the enterprises in the six wards along the road corridor are run by women. This figure is consistent with the

national statistic of 30 percent.24 Women entrepreneurs and road users face various barriers to access the

economic benefits of road expansion. There are no dedicated market areas along the corridor. Roadside vendors,

who are mostly women, and their customers will face an increased risk of road safety and security with the

highway expansion. Further, women tend to have limited business skills (including financial and digital literacy),

access to timely market information, and the capital required to scale up an enterprise. Lack of gender-responsive

infrastructure, such as separate toilets and resting areas, makes travel difficult for women.

       The project will improve regional and national trade and services. To ensure that women (entrepreneurs)

in the project corridor can also benefit from the increased economic activity, at least three market areas will be

built along the highway with dedicated spots reserved for women entrepreneurs and traders. The project will

ensure that the market areas offer free Wi-Fi and a digital bulletin board for timely access to trade and market

information, support the construction of gender-responsive and safe infrastructure including separate toilets and

resting spaces, and include actions to address GBV and human trafficking risks. The DoR-DCID will enter a

Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) with the relevant municipalities for the operation and maintenance of

market areas before bidding of works.

       The project will provide accredited entrepreneurship training for at least 350 local women, which will

include digital and financial literacy. The training will also facilitate linkages to other relevant, NGOs, financing

(digital banking) and government services for follow-up support to establish or expand businesses (in sectors

such as trade and agriculture) that will benefit from the road expansion. The project will incorporate the latest

global evidence and best practices from skills-based training for women’s economic empowerment, such as

personal initiative training.25 Women graduating from the training will be prioritized for designated spots in the

market areas. This will be managed by the relevant municipality under the MoU.

       The project includes two gender indicators in the Results Framework. An additional associated indicator,

namely, the number of women receiving entrepreneurship skills training (Baseline: 0, Target: 350) will be tracked

in the annual progress reports only.

VI.   KEY RISKS



            The overall risk of achieving the PDO is rated Substantial. The main risks are discussed below.



            Political and Governance risks are Substantial. Elections are being conducted across all three tiers of

      government during 2022, of which local level elections have been recently concluded. In principle, the federal

      system provides opportunities to decentralize development benefits and make service delivery more effective

      and accountable. The pandemic also underscored the role of local governments, especially in deploying social

      programs. However, the risks of political uncertainty, jurisdictional overlap between the three tiers of

      government, and lack of clarity and coherence between policies and devolved powers will remain in the coming

      period. The World Bank is closely monitoring the country situation and maintains regular dialogue on the

      development agenda with all key stakeholders to mitigate any risks that may be influenced by political change.

      While the extent to which political risks can be mitigated through Project-specific measures is limited, the Project

      design includes specific actions to build public support through broader stakeholder participation, strategic

      communication, and outreach. Other measures include implementing the institutional strengthening activities

      under the Project, dialogue and close collaboration with GoN entities, and proactive engagement by the World

      Bank team and management as issues arise.

             Macroeconomic risk is Substantial. One major uncertainty is related to vaccine deployment and

      effectiveness to stop transmission of a new COVID-19 variant. A second is stronger import control measures to

      mitigate pressures on international reserves, which could affect growth through lower trade-related tax revenue,

      depressed private consumption and production, and lower capital expenditures. The ongoing war in Ukraine, if

      it deepens further, could lower travel demand and may threaten the recovery of tourism and tourism-related

    sectors. While downside risks are significant, Nepal’s strong record of sustained macroeconomic stability during

    periods of large exogenous shocks demonstrates a growing capacity to navigate future macroeconomic risks.

           Technical risk is Substantial. The targeted road section runs through sections of dense linear settlements.

    Effective stakeholder engagement and careful relocation of potential structures that are encroaching the RoW

    will be required. The road also passes through sections that are prone to landslides and flooding. The project will

    associations, and development agencies. These stakeholders hold interest, influence and/or are concerned about

    returns of project investment, and the need for compliance with key regulations. An SEP was thus prepared to

    guide consultation processes and disclosure of relevant project information to stakeholders throughout the

    entire project duration.

         Grievance redress mechanism (GRM). The project-level GRM addresses overall project-related grievances

  and will include grievance redress committees (GRCs) at the field and PCU levels. The GRCs will respond to

  complaints received from affected people and provide timely, transparent, and fair resolutions. Details of the

  GRM will be widely disseminated, the GRM registers will be accessible to communities and other stakeholders,

  and information on the corrective actions taken in response to the grievances will be shared with stakeholders. In

  addition to onsite GRMs, the project will consider developing an interactive voice response system to receive

  complaints and suggestions from citizens. The GRM will be gender responsive, culturally appropriate, and readily

  accessible to the PAPs at no cost and without retribution. The system will be accessible to women and girls; female

  PAP representatives will be included in the GRC to help redress grievances specific to women. In addition, a GRM

  will be established for worker-related grievances.

          A SEA/SH GRM will be established locally at the project site as a corresponding body to the overall project

  GRM using the existing framework. As recognized by the 2019 “Grievance Redress for Sexual Exploitation and

  Abuse in World Bank Projects” note, GBV/SEA/SH requires specific attention to allow for prompt, safe, and ethical

  handling of and response to GBV/SEA/SH cases in a survivor centric approach. A GBV focal point at the project

  level will be identified within the GRC, who will respond to all GBV/SEA/SH cases incurred by the project. The focal

  point will link with and refer cases to existing local GBV services or can also coordinate and link with the 24-hour

  toll-free helpline “Khabar Garau-1145” to provide support to survivors.

  Financial Management

         Institutional Arrangements and Financial Regulations. Each PCU will be responsible to ensure sound FM

  arrangements of their component under the project. Given the number of cost centers, it is crucial to ensure

  strong coordination and collaboration amongst all the implementing agencies during the preparation of respective

  agency’s AWPB. The GoN Financial Procedure and Fiscal Accountability Act (FPFAA), 2019, Financial Procedure

  and Fiscal Accountability Regulations, 2021 and the respective Provincial Financial Procedure Acts will govern the

  project for programming, budgeting, accounting, funds flow, reporting, internal control, internal audit, external

  audit, and other financial management procedures—these will also be reflected in the PIM. The draft PIM was

  submitted to the World Bank prior to project negotiations. The final PIM will be shared with the World Bank for

  review prior to project effectiveness, be endorsed by the PSC, and will be subject to periodic updating, based on

  implementation experience.

         AWPB and Budget Authorization. Budget will be released upon approval of the Appropriation Act to the

  Secretaries of MoPIT, MoICS and MoFE based on the approved AWPB. Each PCU will be responsible to prepare

  the AWPB. The budget will accordingly be reflected in the LMBIS as per the approved AWPB for each PCU and PIU.

  The budget for activities to be implemented by the Division Forest Offices of the Provinces will be included in

  MoFE’s budget and it will be provided as conditional grants to the respective provinces. The respective provinces

  will enter the budget for such activities in the Provincial LMBIS. The PCUs will ensure: (i) before project

  effectiveness, that a separate budget head is entered in the ‘Appropriation Book’ (Red Book) for the budget

  proposed in the FY23 LMBIS; (ii) availability of budget for the first year of implementation if the loan agreement

  is signed after presentation of the budget in the Parliament; and (iii) the preparation and submission of the AWPB

  together with disbursement projections to the World Bank before the start of each FY.

         Accounting and Financial Reporting. The Nepal Public Sector Accounting Standards will be used as the

  accounting standard. All PCUs, PIUs and cost centers will maintain their project accounts in the Computerized

  Government Accounting System. All project offices will maintain a separate sub-ledger to record and report

  budget and expenditures by category, component, subcomponent, and activity as prescribed in the PIM. The PIUs

  will consolidate and provide financial reports required for IUFRs to respective PCUs within 30 days from the end

  of the quarter. Each PCU will maintain the IDA credit register and the Withdrawal Monitoring Register. Each PCU

  will submit the IUFR to the World Bank separately. The PCUs will consolidate reports from PIUs and submit

  separate quarterly implementation progress reports, which will include IUFRs, and the procurement management

  reports, within 45 days from the end of the quarter. Project implementation progress reports will be prepared

  from the date of credit effectiveness, showing the sources and uses of funds, statement of GoN funds and GoN

  reimbursable funds, statement of expenses by category and component, statement of IDA disbursement, output

  monitoring report, procurement management report, and narrative progress report in the format agreed with the

  World Bank.

         Internal Control. The GoN has clear legal provisions and procedures in place to ensure budget commitment

  control as depicted in the Public Expenditure and Financial Accountability (PEFA) assessment conducted in

  FY13/14. The District Treasury Comptroller Offices (DTCOs) and Province Treasury Comptroller Offices (PTCOs)

  will make payments only after ensuring the adequate budget and sources of funds in conformity with the budget

  release procedures. Expenditures are deemed to be incurred only if there is an approved budget and balance to

  cover the expenditure. However, the internal control weakness, provided in the external audit, is not addressed

  effectively. The project will maintain a log of the Office of Auditor General (OAG) recommendation and ensure

  resolutions/actions taken on the findings reported by the OAG in the succeeding year’s audit.

         Internal Audit. The Financial Comptroller General Office (FCGO), through the respective DTCOs for the

  federal government and PTCOs for the provincial governments, is responsible for internal audits. The internal

  audit coverage is 100 percent; however, as highlighted in the PEFA 2014, the internal audit has minimal focus on

  systems monitoring and is more focused on the pre-audit of transactions and preparation of treasury reports

  (PEFA, FY13/14). As per Section 33 (4) of the FPFAA, the internal audit of the GoN’s offices should be conducted

  quarterly and internal audit of the provincial governments on periodic basis as specified in the respective Province

  Financial Procedure Acts, and reports should be prepared. However, in practice, reports are issued annually or

  semi-annually for the implementing agencies. The FCGO has provisioned a separate account sub-group in the civil

  service for internal auditors who work at DTCOs and PTCOs. The respective PCU/PIU will be responsible for

  quarterly audits and resolving any audit observations as per the prevailing Law. Government has strengthened

  internal audit by creating independent internal audit cadres, updating internal audit guidelines, and internal audit

  standards are under development. While internal audit is still transactional, it is recommended to further

  strengthen fiduciary management through reviews conducted by the FM Consultant in each PCU. The World Bank

  team will review the internal control of the project throughout implementation.

         External Audit. The OAG of Nepal, as mandated by the Constitution of Nepal, will conduct audits of all PCUs

  and PIUs under the project. Each PCU will coordinate with PIUs and OAG to ensure the timely completion of project

  audits. The annual audited project financial statements and the audit reports will be submitted to the World Bank

  separately by each PCU. These statements and reports will be disclosed on the website of each PCU as well as the

  World Bank website. Given the number of IAs and the need for OAG to conduct and consolidate reports for each,

  the external audit report of the project for each year of project implementation will be submitted to the World

  Bank within nine months from the end of the fiscal year. There are no overdue audits from the implementing

  ministries as a portfolio-wise extension has been provided by the World Bank and audit reports are due on June

  15, 2022.

        Funds Flow Modality and Arrangement. DTCOs and PTCOs will make payments for the expenditures

  incurred for the project, based on the AWPB of: (i) DoR-DCID; (ii) MoICS; and (iii) DoFSC and two provincial PIUs

  under DoFSC component. The IDA share of expenditure will also be pre-financed through GoN’s consolidated fund.

  Direct payments or direct reimbursements could also be made from the World Bank. Special commitment

  payments could also be provided from the World Bank.

         Disbursement. The disbursement from the World Bank will be report-based for each PCU. All PCUs have

  requisite experience in implementing IDA-financed projects thus providing a proven base for financial

  management and reporting. Each PCU will be responsible for the disbursements and submission of withdrawal

  applications under their respective components. For reported eligible expenditures from PCUs, reimbursement or

  direct payment for the amounts above the threshold, or special commitment payment, can also be made as

  specified in the Disbursement Letter issued by the World Bank.
