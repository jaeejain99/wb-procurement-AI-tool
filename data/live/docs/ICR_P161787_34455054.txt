    I.       PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES



    A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL

1.       By 2018, Ghana had experienced nearly two decades of strong economic growth, reaching lower-middle-income

status in 2011. High export prices and domestic and foreign investments, especially in oil, drove growth above 10 percent

during peak production years. However, fiscal mismanagement and external shocks, such as fluctuating oil prices, slowed

growth to 3.5 percent by 2016, with rising fiscal deficit and public debt straining the economy.



2.       In 2017, the Government implemented fiscal measures that cut fiscal deficit to 5.9 percent of gross domestic

product (GDP), lowered debt-to-GDP ratio to 69.2 percent, and spurred annual growth to 8.5 percent, led by the mining

and oil sectors. Inflation eased, allowing interest rate cuts, but risks from commodity price volatility, energy sector debt,

and regional poverty disparities persisted, with poverty rates rising in areas such as Upper West, Northern, and Volta

region, despite declines elsewhere.



3.       Ghana’s financial sector had grown significantly, but the banking sector was facing vulnerabilities. Total financial

sector assets rose from 48 percent to 68 percent of GDP between 2010 and 2016. However, the banking sector, which

was the primary holder of financial sector assets, recorded high nonperforming loans (NPLs), which stood at 23.4 percent

by April 2018. Though the average capital adequacy ratio (CAR) remained high at 18 percent, several banks fell below the

regulatory minimum and needed recapitalization. Profitability declined due to low credit growth and increased NPL

provisions.



4.      At the time of appraisal, Ghana's specialized deposit-taking institutions (SDIs) sector, which includes

microfinance institutions (MFIs),1 rural and community banks (RCBs), savings and loans companies (S&Ls), and finance

houses, faced critical challenges. Despite being vital for financial inclusion, regulatory oversight was under significant

strain. Many SDIs operated unsafely, with undercapitalization, liquidity issues, and frequent violations of prudential

standards. By 2017, 39 percent of MFIs and RCBs were distressed or closed, affecting hundreds of thousands of depositors.

Oversight by Bank of Ghana (BoG), the Association of Rural Banks (ARB) Apex Bank, and the Ghana Co-operative Credit

Unions Association (CUA) was hindered by the rapid licensing of diverse, often undercapitalized institutions managed by

unqualified owners, which strained supervisory capacity.



5.      Ghana’s financial inclusion had improved notably, with formal account ownership rising from 41 percent in 2010

to 58 percent in 2017, supported by mobile money expansion. Mobile money accounts increased 529 percent between

2012 and 2017, reaching 23.9 million accounts and facilitating nearly a billion transactions. However, access remained low

among certain groups, particularly rural residents, women, and poor regions such as Upper West and Northern Ghana.

These groups are also less likely to own bank accounts due to limited bank penetration. From 2010 to 2017, bank account

ownership grew only slightly, rising from 34 percent to 36 percent.



6.      This project was designed to address several of these challenges and contribute to financial sector resilience

and improved access to financial services. It was designed in strong collaboration with the International Monetary Fund

(IMF) and other partners to ensure complementary efforts and avoid duplication during the project’s design phase. The

IMF played a significant role in supporting the cleanup and strengthening of the banking sector, focusing on banks, while

the World Bank targeted the SDIs. Coordination efforts involved leveraging studies and technical inputs from other

development partners, such as the German Development Agency.

7.       The implementation of this project faced extraordinary challenges. Just six months after it became effective, the

COVID-19 pandemic halted project implementation for several months because border closures prevented short-term

consultants and resident advisers from traveling to Ghana, which disrupted critical project activities, including the design

of the agency banking model for the ARB Apex Bank and the review of the legal and regulatory framework for the SDI

sector. Similarly, the disruption of supply chains due to COVID-19 delayed the delivery of IT infrastructure for the ARB

Apex Bank. Despite these obstacles, the project team swiftly adapted by leveraging virtual communication tools to conduct

the procurement of goods and consultants, but the disruption significantly slowed momentum. The situation worsened

with the onset of the debt and economic crisis that prompted the Ghanaian authorities to implement a Domestic Debt

Exchange Programme (DDEP). This was the biggest shock to the project implementation environment as it imposed

significant losses on financial institutions (including banks and SDIs), which held a significant amount of government debt.

Many institutions became undercapitalized, and there was a high risk that the financial system could collapse without

decisive action by the authorities. Therefore, and limited resources of the Ministry of Finance (MoF), which was the project

implementing agency (PIA), and BoG, one of the main project beneficiaries, had to redirect their attention and resources

from project implementation to preventing a full-blown financial sector crisis and stabilizing the financial system.

Recognizing the gravity of the situation, at the Government’s request, the World Bank team also shifted its attention and

resources toward the preparation of an emergency financial stability operation to support the recapitalization of the

banking sector.



8.      These cascading shocks compounded preexisting, long-standing vulnerabilities in the SDI sector. Additionally,

the project was already delayed by a nine-month effectiveness lag due to a protracted recruitment by the MoF of the

Project Coordinator and the Procurement Specialist. Considering the initial nine-month delay in achieving project

effectiveness and the disruption caused by COVID-19, the project’s effective implementation period was reduced to about

four years, even with a nine-month extension of the project closing date.



B. Theory of Change



9.      At appraisal, the project team developed a Theory of Change (TOC) which outlined the causal link between

project activities and expected outcomes. The TOC reflected how the project would increase financial sector soundness

and access to financial services by (a) strengthening regulatory and supervisory capacities of BoG and CUA; (b) expanding

financial access points through agent banking and e-banking and connecting Village Savings and Loans Association (VSLAs)

to formal banking channels; (c) enhancing financial literacy and consumer protection; and (d) building institutional

capacity of the MoF and other implementing agencies for sustained impact. Implicit assumptions underpinning the TOC

included rapid effectiveness, the full cleanup of the SDI sector, adequate implementation capacity of the PIA and

beneficiary institutions, strong commitment of beneficiary institutions (particularly the ARB Apex Bank) to lead the

implementation of their activities, absence of major disruptions such as COVID-19, and continued recovery of the Ghanian

economy to support strengthening of the financial sector.



10.      The project was restructured in June 2023 to adapt to an extraordinarily challenging implementation

environment marked by the delayed effectiveness, limited implementation capacity, COVID-19 disruptions, and the

shock to the banking sector of the DDEP. The key activity related to financial stability—overhauling the SDI regulatory

framework —was cancelled as BoG pivoted their financial stability expertise to manage the impact of the DDEP and

prevent a full-blown banking crisis. Instead, the project concentrated on expanding digital financial services through e-

banking and agent banking, upgrading supervisory and IT systems, and enhancing consumer awareness and protection. It

expanded support to building BoG resolution capacity amid heightened vulnerabilities in the banking sector. Several

outcome and intermediate-level targets were revised to account for the challenges. Consequently, the project’s TOC was

recalibrated to focus exclusively on the financial inclusion objective, as the dual objective of promoting both financial

stability and inclusion proved untenable in the face of the shift in policy priorities and vulnerabilities in the SDI sector.

Components



Component 1: Improving Financial Sector Regulatory Oversight and Market Transparency and Discipline (Original: US$8

million; Actual: US$3 million)



13.      This component aimed to enhance the regulatory framework, supervisory capacity, and market transparency

in the financial sector. Specifically, it aimed to support updating SDI regulations, optimizing institutional roles for SDI and

credit unions supervision, and building capacity within BoG’s Other Financial Institutions Supervision Department (OFISD)

through training and technical assistance (TA). The component aimed to support the CUA to strengthen its Supervision

Division, including the development of a supervisory framework, acquisition of a shared core management information

system (MIS) and supervision application, and professional development for credit union managers. Finally, the

component aimed to establish a Domestic Credit Rating Agency (DCRA) to improve market transparency by providing TA,

capacity building, and industry workshops to enhance local expertise and data availability in the credit rating sector.

Component 2: Increasing Outreach of RCBs and MFIs and Linking VSLAs to the Formal Financial Sector (Original: US$13

million; Actual: US$14.09 million)



14.      This component aimed to enhance financial inclusion by upgrading ARB Apex Bank’s data insfrastructure and

core banking applications (including an e-banking platform) and building capacity to support agent banking services. It

aimed to develop an agent banking network for RCBs, including the design of a business model and operational manuals,

procurement of an information and communication technology (ICT) system and point-of-sale (POS) devices, and training

for staff on network management. Moreover, the component aimed at upgrading the shared MIS of MFIs and training

them on its use to improve reporting and service delivery, and linking VSLAs to formal financial services by establishing

new groups, providing training on governance and financial education, and leveraging expanded access points from the

agent banking initiative to strengthen financial links.

Component 3: Bolstering Financial Capability and Consumer Protection (Original: US$3.6 million equivalent; Actual:

US$4.28 million).



15.     This component aimed to enhance financial literacy and strengthen consumer protection mechanisms. Financial

education campaigns utilized traditional methods such as radio and face-to-face interactions, alongside innovative

platforms like SMS/WhatsApp messaging, prerecorded phone lines, and mobile apps to teach concepts like savings,

budgeting, and digital financial services. Consumer protection efforts were designed to focus on building regulatory

capacity through resident advisers, training programs, and developing market conduct supervision strategies for BoG and

National Insurance Commission (NIC). The component also included procuring and implementing complaint management

systems for BoG, NIC, and the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), alongside staff training to ensure effective use

of these systems, improving the overall consumer protection framework.



Component 4: Enhancing the Capacity for the Implementation and Monitoring of Financial Sector Policies and

Supporting Project Implementation (Original: US$3.4 million; Actual: US$3.85 million)



16.     This component focused on strengthening the MoF’s Financial Sector Division (FSD) and support effective

project execution. It was meant to support training for FSD staff on financial inclusion, the development of a financial

inclusion database, and the publication of reports and briefs, alongside organizing events such as the annual financial

inclusion conference. Another objective was to support a financial inclusion survey, geospatial mapping of access points,

and the development of online databases and visual displays. To ensure smooth project implementation, this component

funded consultant salaries, operational costs, and TA for communication strategies, citizen engagement, and monitoring

and evaluation (M&E) systems. Biannual beneficiary surveys were also conducted to assess satisfaction with upgraded

systems, links, and financial education initiatives.



C.      SIGNIFICANT CHANGES DURING IMPLEMENTATION (IF APPLICABLE)



17.      Revised PDOs and outcome targets. The project underwent two restructurings. The first and most significant

restructuring occurred in June 2023 to DDEP’s impact on the soundness of the financial sector, which led to shifting

priorities and implementation challenges. The second, a minor restructuring, occurred in July 2024 at the project’s closure

to cancel undisbursed funds. The team intended to restructure the project immediately after the midterm review (MTR),

but BoG had shifted its attention toward managing the impact of the DDEP, which could have led to collapse of the

financial sector. As a result, BoG needed more time to finalize its plan for reforming the regulatory and institutional

framework for the SDI sector. This plan (never completed) was intended to inform the restructuring, by endorsing key

reforms that the project would support to improve the soundness of the SDI sector. Like BoG, the World Bank team shifted

its focus toward preparation of an emergency financial sector operation to support recapitalization of the banking sector.

The first restructuring revised the original PDO—to promote financial sector soundness and access to financial services by

individuals. In line with the shift in priorities and project scope, the emphasis on ‘financial sector soundness’ was dropped

from the PDO and the wording adjusted to focusing more narrowly on financial inclusion, specifically, ‘To increase access

to financial services by individuals.’ Activities were reprioritized in the DDEP context and the underperforming ones were

cancelled.



18.     Revised targets for PDO indicators. As shown in table 2, the first PDO indicator—the percentage of reporting SDIs

and CU in compliance with their respective minimum capital adequacy ratio or gearing ratio —was dropped in line with

the dropping of the soundness objective from the PDO, following the cancellation of associated activities amid the DDEP

and the deterioration of the overall financial sector solvency. The other two indicators were retained as follows:



(a)   Number of deposit accounts within the RCB network: This indicator was unchanged.

19.      Revised components. Changes to the components were also made in June 2023. Key changes included dropping

activities related to regulatory frameworks for SDIs and core banking application for credit unions, reallocating resources

to enhance digital banking, expand agent networks, and link VSLAs to formal financial services. Enrollment targets for

digital platforms were revised.

Component 1 (Improving Financial Sector Regulatory Oversight and Market Transparency and Discipline)



20.     Changes were made to prioritize enhancing BoG’s resolution capacity while dropping the strengthening of SDI

regulatory and supervisory frameworks. Support for credit unions shifted from digitization efforts to upgrading

supervisory tools and capacity building. Consequently, the component was renamed to ‘Improving Credit Unions’

Regulatory Framework, BoG’s Resolution Capacity, and Market Transparency and Discipline’. The budget for Component

1 was decreased by US$2.15 million to US$5.85 million, due to cost savings and cancellation of the procurement and

implementation of core banking and mobile banking applications for credit unions.

Component 2 (Increasing the Outreach of RCBs and MFIs and Linking VSLAs to Formal Financial Services)



21.      Component 2’s budget increased from US$13 million to US$14.35 million to cover cost overruns for ARB Apex

Bank activities—ICT upgrades (US$0.8 million) and agent banking component (US$2.25 million), including core IT

applications for agency banking and e-banking. Procurement of the micro-banking application for MFIs were canceled and

replaced by capacity-building initiatives for Ghana Microfinance Institutions Network (GHAMFIN). VSLA efforts shifted to

linking existing, mature groups rather than creating new ones, resulting in US$1.4 million savings.2

Component 3 (Bolstering Financial Capacity and Consumer Protection)



22.     Minor cost adjustments led to US$0.2 million savings in financial literacy campaigns and revised allocation reduced

to US$1.5 million. BoG’s market conduct support shifted from a resident adviser to short-term technical experts due to

BoG’s preference and difficulty in attracting resident advisers while maintaining all planned activities.

Component 4 (Enhancing Capacity for Policy Implementation and Project Support)



23.     Component 4’s budget increased from US$3.4 million to US$4.1 million. This included US$0.7 million saved by

canceling geospatial mapping, as well as US$1.4 million added to cover project implementation cost overruns and support

the extended project timeline.



24.       Other Changes. The project timeline was extended by nine months to June 2024 to ensure the completion of

critical activities.



Rationale for Changes and Their Implication on the Original Theory of Change (TOC)



25.     In June 2023, the project was restructured based on findings and recommendations of the MTR conducted in mid-

2022. The MTR found the PDO was still relevant but noted some of the original PDO indicators, particularly the financial

sector soundness indicator, were unlikely to be achieved in the prevailing financial sector context. The restructuring

altered the original TOC by canceling regulatory and supervisory reforms for SDIs. As a result, financial sector soundness

was removed from the Results Framework. Other outcome targets that were deemed overambitious were also revised.

The rationale for the changes is detailed in the following paragraphs.

26.     Shifting priorities. Between 2022 and 2023, the Government implemented the DDEP as a critical response to an

unsustainable debt burden that threatened macroeconomic stability. Domestic public debt, which was held largely by

commercial banks, was restructured and exacerbated vulnerabilities in the financial sector, straining liquidity, solvency,

and investor confidence while redirecting policy priorities toward immediate crisis management. Consequently, BoG

appropriately redirected its focus from long-term reforms to immediate crisis management and financial stability,

including by strengthening of its resolution capacity to deal with distressed financial institutions. This limited the capacity

and urgency to overhaul SDI’s regulatory and supervisory framework. Moreover, the SDI sector was still facing legacy

solvency challenges, which would have muted the impact of the planned reforms.



27.     Implementation and procurement challenges. The project’s effectiveness was delayed by nine months, limiting

the time available for execution. Limited technical and procurement capacity within the implementing agency and

beneficiary institutions caused delays in drafting terms of reference (TORs), often requiring the World Bank team’s

assistance, bidding processes, and approvals. The Central Tender Review Committee (CTRC), part of Ghana's procurement

process, caused significant delays by questioning some project activities already outlined in the Financing Agreement, cost

appropriateness, and procurement decisions by the implementing agency. Consequently, contracts such as those for

financial education and VSLA links were stuck in CTRC review, awaiting approval.



28.      Underestimation of needs. Assumptions made during project design underestimated the resources and

infrastructure required for upgrading core banking systems for credit unions. The actual cost of upgrading the core banking

systems was determined after a deeper assessment during implementation. However, in line with best practices, the

project had allocated a US$2 million contingency at appraisal for potential cost overruns related to deployment of IT

solutions, but this amount was significantly reduced due to appreciation of the US dollar against the SDR. If the deeper

assessment to estimate the cost of the core banking system had been completed sooner as planned using the funds set

aside in the Project Preparation Advance (PPA), the additional funding needs would have been identified and addressed

through additional financing.



29.     Highly ambitious design. The project’s initial objectives and indicators, such as improving financial sector

soundness and achieving high enrollment in digital banking platforms, were highly optimistic. This was warranted by the

strong commitment of the counterparts at project preparation, including to fully cleanup of the banking sector. While

headwinds such as the DDEP and COVID-19 could not have been anticipated, the project could have anticipated the time

lag between the implementation of reforms/rollout of IT solutions (for example, the e-banking platform) and the

achievement of the target results such as the number of customers enrolled in the e-banking platform.



30.     External factors. The project’s focus on TA, capacity building, and outputs requiring international consultants

made it highly susceptible to travel restrictions. COVID-19 had a dual effect on the financial sector and the project —it

delayed project activities due to logistical issues and the Government’s focus on addressing the pandemic and weakened

borrowers’ loan repayment capacity, which compounded vulnerabilities in the SDI sector. Coupled with lingering

vulnerabilities in the SDI sector, implementing the planned reforms would not have led to the achievement of project

goals related to SDI soundness.

 C.      EFFICIENCY



Assessment of Efficiency and Rating



43.     The efficiency of the project is rated Substantial based on a comprehensive evaluation of the project’s design,

implementation, and resource utilization. The project’s design addressed the specific needs of Ghana’s financial sector,

with a focus on improving access to finance, institutional capacity building, and enhancing regulatory oversight. The

project design was informed by a World Bank TA program under way at the time. The team chose Investment Project

Financing (IPF) for its suitability and flexibility in supporting complex, long-term investments such as infrastructure and

capacity building, enabling a focus on completing essential foundational activities such as procurement, infrastructure

construction, and system development before measurable outcomes can materialize. Unlike PforR or IPF with PBCs, IPF

avoids rigid disbursement conditions, ensuring smoother implementation in contexts where institutional capacity and

monitoring systems are at the development stage.



44.     The project efficiently mobilized resources, ensuring that funds were allocated to priority areas such as financial

infrastructure development and institutional strengthening, thereby addressing critical bottlenecks in the sector. The

inclusion of financial services reforms and the introduction of modern technologies played a key role in improving the

operational efficiency of financial institutions, particularly rural banks. This targeted approach ensured that resources

were deployed where they were to generate the greatest impact, contributing to improved financial inclusion and the

development of a more robust financial sector.



45.      While an economic internal rate of return cannot be calculated for a TA project such as this, the project

demonstrated significant efficiency in terms of operational implementation. It surpassed its target for deposit accounts

and linking VSLA members to formal financial services by 2 and 73 percent, respectively. Upgrades to ARB Apex Bank’s

core banking software from T24 R16 to T24 R20 improved operational efficiency and system reliability, allowing ARB Apex

Bank to better serve over 130 RCBs nationwide and enhancing customer satisfaction. For example, the time required for

daily close-of-business activities was significantly reduced from 8 hours to approximately 4.5 hours. Many RCBs reported

a sharp reduction in client service turnaround times, from 20 minutes to just 3 minutes. Additionally, RCBs can now create

and customize their own products to meet specific needs of their customers, without the earlier reliance on standardized

offerings of ARB Apex Bank. ARB Apex Bank's core banking system also enabled susu deposit collectors to use POS devices,

eliminating manual errors and fraud through real-time transaction uploads and instant text confirmations to depositors.

Beyond 5,000 project-funded smart POS devices, RCBs have independently mobilized their own funds to purchase an

additional 688 POS devices. Furthermore, the implementation of a more reliable USSD platform facilitated significant

growth in e-banking transactions, increasing accessibility for underserved populations and strengthening the digital

financial ecosystem. These achievements demonstrate effective use of resources to achieve financial inclusion targets.

Despite these benefits, the project missed key systemic improvements—CUA’s core banking system, the MFI shared

application, and regulators’ complaints handling system.



46.     Project costs and budget management. The actual component costs of the project remained largely within the

estimates outlined during project appraisal. The total budget of US$30 million, fully funded by the World Bank, was

strategically allocated through detailed annual work plans to focus on priority areas of the financial sector. While there

were usual variances in some components, these were efficiently managed. It is noteworthy that during the restructuring,

restructuring, all beneficiary institutions were equipped with the necessary expertise to implement the project effectively.



49.      COVID-19 response and adaptations. The project efficiently adapted to COVID-19 challenges by making

operational adjustments to maintain continuity. While the pandemic delayed capacity-building activities and procurement

of goods and services, the team realigned resources and timelines to minimize disruptions. It also supported financial

institutions in adopting digital platforms, enabling continued services, and increasing sector resilience. Despite challenges

posed by procurement delays and the COVID-19 pandemic, the project successfully closed on schedule and achieved its

goals within the original financial envelope, demonstrating strong operational and financial efficiency.

Negative Outcomes



60.      Cancellation of the implementation of the core banking system for credit unions. The development and

model for credit unions. The need for detailed scoping and design of the core banking application was anticipated during

unmet expectations among credit unions as they had anticipated a more comprehensive platform as an alternative to the

clients to competing providers of core banking applications, which affected CUA’s reputation.

 III. BANK PERFORMANCE, COMPLIANCE ISSUES, AND RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME



A. QUALITY OF MONITORING AND EVALUATION (M&E)



70.     The M&E framework of the project demonstrated several strengths at both the design and implementation

stages, contributing to its ability to track progress and guide decision-making. The framework had a clear focus on

financial inclusion outcomes, incorporation of gender-disaggregated metrics, use of beneficiary feedback mechanisms,

and adaptability during implementation. However, the framework suffered from overly ambitious targets and gaps in

systemic indicators, among others.

M&E Design



71.     Focus on measurable outcomes. The framework included clear, measurable indicators, such as the number of

deposit accounts in the RCB network, e-banking customer enrollment, and financial literacy outreach. These were aligned

with the objectives of expanding financial inclusion and institutional strengthening, with VSLAs targets emphasizing

project’s benefits to underserved groups.



72.     Target setting. Some initial targets were highly ambitious. For example, 250,000 enrollments in ARB Apex Bank’s

e-banking platform were high given the baseline of 5,000 users and the complexity of procuring and implementing new

technology. Given the complex, phased nature of the project—particularly in its early stages when the focus was on

upgrading IT infrastructure and establishing the foundations for both financial inclusion and sector soundness —the

measurement of progress against the PDOs should have accounted for intermediate milestones as key indicators of

success. The M&E design should have better captured these incremental achievements, such as the installation of backend

systems or data center infrastructure. This approach would ensure that stakeholders can observe tangible progress before

outcome indicators are achieved. Additionally, such intermediate indicators would help avoid perceptions of stagnation,

particularly in cases where final PDO indicators—such as transaction volumes or number of e-banking accounts—can only

be measured after the requisite IT systems have been procured, configured, and are fully operational. Finally, the project

design should have considered installation of the e-banking platform as an outcome or PDO indicator, even if transactions

are not yet flowing, particularly within the typical five-year project implementation time frame.



73.      Early interventions aimed at improving regulatory and supervisory oversight in the financial sector often led to

an initial increase in the number of financial institutions reporting noncompliance with regulatory ratios. This was an

expected outcome given the legacy solvency issues in Ghana’s financial system and the challenges posed by the COVID-

19 pandemic. These results, while counterintuitive in the short term, reflect the nature of enhanced regulatory scrutiny,

which often uncovers weaknesses and leads to higher reporting of noncompliance before improvements can be realized.

This should have been considered during M&E design.

74.     Gender focus. The inclusion of gender-disaggregated indicators, such as the number of deposit accounts owned

stability and inclusion. Consequently, no environmental safeguard policies were triggered, and no environmental

instruments were prepared at either the design or implementation stages. Although e-waste management concerns arose

during the procurement and technology upgrades under Component 2, ARB Apex Bank (the beneficiary institution)

addressed these by engaging professional e-waste disposal contractors. The ARB Apex Bank waste management policy

was also reviewed to further enhance e-waste management practices.



80.      Social safeguard compliance. The project was assessed as low risk for social safeguards, with no social safeguard

policies triggered due to the absence of physical works, eliminating the risk of resettlement or livelihood displacement.

disruptions.

D. RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME



93.      The risk to the project’s development outcome remains substantial. Despite progress in expanding financial

access and strengthening rural financial institutions, macroeconomic instability—inflation and currency depreciation—

poses risks to the sustainability of reforms. The sustainability of the project’s interventions is contingent on strong

institutional capacity, continued financial and regulatory support, and ongoing investment in infrastructure and training.

The MoF, BoG, CUA, and other stakeholders would need to continue the momentum generated by the project, including

strengthening institutional capacity to oversee and manage the interventions effectively.

94.      Financial inclusion infrastructure. The project focused heavily on expanding digital financial services and

supporting agent banking networks as means of increasing financial inclusion. These interventions, such as the upgrading

of ARB Apex Bank's IT systems and the deployment of agent banking, have strong potential for sustainability, especially

given their scalability. The sustainability risk of the project’s digital financial services and agent banking network initiatives

is mitigated by ARB Apex Bank’s technical capacity and ongoing commitment to these interventions. Despite the project’s

closure, ARB Apex Bank successfully rolled out the agent banking network and continued expanding its reach by procuring

an additional 688 POS devices beyond the 5,000 project-funded devices. The bank’s core banking system plays a crucial

role in enabling susu deposit mobilizers to use POS devices, reducing manual errors and fraud through real-time

transaction uploads and instant text confirmations. Furthermore, RCBs, now equipped with upgraded systems, can

customize financial products to meet the specific needs of their customers, marking a shift from the earlier reliance on

standardized offerings from ARB Apex Bank. All these benefits are expected to continue and expand post project closure.

95.     A key element in ensuring the long-term sustainability of these infrastructure improvements is the fee model

97.     Complaints handling system and the DCRA. Initiatives, such as the complaints handling systems and the

operationalization and the broader benefits of the initiative will be fully realized in the future. The development of

complaints handling systems will also be supported under the GDFP.



 IV. LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS



98.      Tailoring project design to implementation capacity. Capacity-building projects, particularly supporting the

implementation of IT infrastructure and solutions, are intrinsically complex and risky. Future projects must ensure that

project scope and targets are both ambitious and achievable by conducting thorough assessments of baseline conditions

and implementation capacity during preparation. For example, under this project, although the Government secured a

US$2 million PPA for designing IT systems and developing technical specifications, low capacity within the PIA and

beneficiary institutions prevented the effective use of the PPA to advance implementation readiness. This underscores

the need for future projects to complete comprehensive assessment of beneficiaries’ capacity at preparation stage to

inform tailored interventions. Future projects, particularly those involving large-scale IT investments, procurement, and

institutional reforms, should also adopt more realistic timelines that align with the scope of activities and capacity

constraints. For such projects, the standard five-year implementation period is often inadequate; extending timelines to

six or seven years would provide the necessary time to address challenges and achieve sustainable outcomes.



99.     Flexibility in project design: Flexibility in project design is crucial for adapting to changing circumstances and

ensuring that critical outcomes remain a priority. In this project, the ability to restructure was instrumental in achieving

progress despite significant challenges. Future projects should incorporate mechanisms for flexibility from the outset,

enabling teams to respond effectively to unforeseen changes and risks.



                                                                                                                     Page 23

         The World Bank

         Ghana Financial Sector Development Project (P161787)                                                  ICR DOCUMENT







100.     The value of timely adjustments. Early identification of project limitations and swift restructuring decisions can

significantly enhance project effectiveness. While this project benefitted from restructuring, earlier recognition of

challenges and proactive adjustments could have further optimized outcomes. Future projects should prioritize robust

diagnostic tools and continuous monitoring to enable early course corrections when needed.



101.     Balancing financial stability and inclusion objectives. Future projects in the financial sector should carefully

balance the dual objectives of financial stability and inclusion, particularly in contexts with sector vulnerabilities. While

this project sought to integrate these goals seamlessly, it highlighted that trade-offs are often unavoidable. Projects should

establish clear priorities and realistic expectations about the extent to which both objectives can be pursued

simultaneously. Moreover, the MTR underscored that financial sector soundness is influenced by a wide range of systemic

factors beyond the scope of any single project. Future initiatives should adopt a holistic approach, recognizing the

limitations of stand-alone interventions and coordinating efforts with broader systemic reforms to enhance the likelihood

of achieving sustainable financial stability and inclusion outcomes.
