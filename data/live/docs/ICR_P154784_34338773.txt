I. PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES

A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL



     Context

1.   At the time of appraisal of the Kenya Climate Smart Agriculture Project (KCSAP), Kenya was the fifth largest

     economy in sub-Saharan Africa after Nigeria, South Africa, Angola, and Sudan, with a gross national income per

     capita of US$1,340 in 2015. Despite the poverty rate falling from 47 percent in 2005/06 to 39 percent in 2012/13,

     reducing poverty and increasing shared prosperity remained formidable challenges, particularly for the rural

     population. Poor agricultural performance had stymied poverty reduction even as the economy had grown. Based

     on the national household budget survey of 2005/06, the vast majority of the poor lived in rural areas and sustained

     themselves through income and consumption from crops and livestock.

2.   In 2010, the adoption of a new Constitution was a major step towards addressing disparities and historical

     patterns of marginalization through a two-tiered system of national and county government. Accordingly, the

     national government committed to devolve responsibility for multiple functions to 47 elected county governments,

     which would receive at least 15 percent of national revenues to perform them. County governments now play the

     primary on-the-ground role in delivering agricultural services (crop and animal husbandry, extension, agricultural

     marketing, and other services), and the national government retains a policymaking, regulatory, and research role,

     albeit some counties have struggled in delivering their mandated services.

3.   Agriculture—the dominant source of employment for roughly half of the nation—was a major force in the

     economy, with a leading role in poverty reduction and adaptation to climate change. However, the performance

     of Kenyan agriculture had been highly volatile, with the principal cause being extreme weather events which are

     increasing in frequency and intensity with climate change. The country was particularly vulnerable to increasing

     temperatures and droughts, as nearly all agriculture in Kenya was rain-fed. Consequently, negative growth had

     occurred in nine years between 1980 and 2012. Studies in Kenya1 had found that by 2030, under a business-as-usual

     scenario, climate change would reduce yields of staple crops (maize by 12 percent, rice by 23 percent, wheat by 13

     percent) as well as prospects for cropland to sustain maize and wheat production. Depending on the region and type

     of production system, water scarcity would result in less productive pastures, lower dairy yields, and higher risks that

     crop and livestock diseases would spread. Meeting this challenge would therefore require investments in building

     resilience to near-term shocks and in adapting to long-term climate change. In addition to resilience, agriculture is

     also important for mitigation in Kenya. The sector was the largest source of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the

     country, contributing about 58.6 percent to total emissions. As such, the agriculture sector in Kenya was central in

     the effort for reducing GHG emissions and become part of the solution to tackle climate change. In this context,

     climate-smart agriculture (CSA) offered an appropriate strategic framework for responding to and reducing the

     adverse effects of climate change.

4.   Kenya’s wide spectrum of CSA policies, strategies, and plans provided grounds to achieve the triple-wins. The 2010

     National Climate Change Response Strategy (NCCRS) provided a framework for integrating climate change into

     development priorities. The 2012 National Climate Change Action Plan (NCCAP) operationalized the NCCRS and

     emphasized a low carbon, climate-resilient development pathway for the economy critical for achieving the

   Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) of combating climate change and its impacts (SDG13). The NCCAP set out the

   Kenya Nationally Appropriate Mitigation Actions (NAMAs) pathway for six sectors: energy, transport, industry,

   agriculture, forestry, and waste management. The country also had a Kenya Climate Change Act (2016) and a

   Climate-Smart Agriculture Program (CSAP 2015–2030) that envisioned “a climate resilient and low carbon growth

   sustainable agriculture that ensures food security and contributes to national development goals in line with Kenya

   Vision 2030.”

5. KCSAP contributed to the Government of Kenya’s Vision 2030 development strategy, launched in 2008, and to its

   agriculture strategy. Vision 2030 reiterated the importance of transforming smallholder subsistence agriculture into

   a modern, innovative, commercially oriented sector. It also aligned with the objectives and goals set by the

   Agricultural Sector Development Strategy 2010–2020 (ASDS). In particular, KCSAP supported the ASDS priorities set

   with respect to adaptation to climate change in agriculture, livestock and in Arid and Semi-Arid Lands (ASALs). With

   respect to adaptation to climate change, ASDS prioritized investments in weather information systems, research on

   drought-tolerant varieties, soil and water conservation, water harvesting, and integrated pest management. For

   livestock, ASDS prioritized improved management of grazing systems, biogas, livestock diversification, and improved

   breeding. During its implementation and from 2020, efforts were also made to align the project with Agriculture

   Sector Transformation and Growth Strategy (ASTGS) for which the objective was to transition Kenyan agriculture

   from subsistence to commercialization while enhancing resilience and digitization.

6. Finally, KCSAP was closely aligned with the World Bank Group Kenya Country Partnership Strategy (CPS) FY 2014 –

   2018. The program was particularly relevant for Domain of Engagement 2, “Protection and Potential – Human

   Resource Development for Shared Prosperity” and specifically outcome 4: Greater Agricultural Productivity. It was

   also consistent with the Africa Climate Business Plan: Accelerating Climate Resilience and Low-Carbon Development.



   Theory of Change (Results Chain)

7. The KCSAP brought together components to enable the adoption by farmers of a selection of climate smart

   technology innovation management practices (TIMPs) and their access to public sector infrastructure and services

   to increase their productivity and resilience to climate change. The components under KCSAP can be organized under

   two groups of impacts: a direct impact on beneficiary households and an ecosystem impact. At its core, KCSAP

   adopted a Community Driven Development (CDD) approach. At the community level, the project interventions were

   carried out through Common Interest Groups (CIGs) and Vulnerable and Marginalized Groups (VMGs) which were

   linked to a Community Driven Development Committee (CDDC) at the ward level and to Farmer Producer

   Organizations (FPOs) at Ward/Subcounty level. These were vehicles for the delivery of the project. Households also

   benefitted directly from the sub- projects that were implemented by the County Governments. These sub projects

   ensured access to a range of services e.g., irrigation, animal vaccination or market infrastructure.

8. In addition, the project also put in place investments aimed at an ecosystem building approach, that enabled the

    scaling up of the delivery of services by the public sector to farmers. These investments include investments

    towards strengthening public research related to climate smart agriculture (CSA), setting Big Data architecture and

    establishment of agro-meteorological stations all contributing to delivery of services like climate smart extension

    practices and agro-weather information among others. The building of the ecosystem under KCSAP contributed to

    improvements in public services in the agriculture sector across Kenya which meant that the benefits accrued to

    beneficiaries beyond the 24 project counties. For example, elements under this ecosystem building have also

    supported the World Bank financed NARIGP which was targeting 21 other counties in Kenya.

9. The project was built around 5 components, including a component dedicated to project management and

    coordination (Component 4) and a component enabling a contingency emergency response (Component 5).

    Component 1 was the channel for the CDD approach and the direct household impacts. Components 2 and 3

    supported the development of the public sector ecosystem and the delivery of TIMPs and services to farmers. The

    three core components under the KCSAP s were:

    • Component 1: Scaling up Climate-Smart Agricultural Practices

    • Component 2: Strengthening Climate-Smart Agricultural Research and Seed Systems

    • Component 3: Supporting Agro-weather, Market, Climate, and Advisory Services

10. KCSAP implementation involved a three-tiered institutional arrangement, namely at the national, county, and

    community levels. At the national level, the Ministry of Agriculture, Livestock and Fisheries (MoALF) was the main

    implementing agency. County governments were the executing agencies at subnational level, while beneficiaries

    were responsible for implementing their CSA microproject interventions at the community level.

11. Those three components invested in the development of institutional infrastructure which could serve farmers

    beyond the lifetime and scope of the project. To further test how these different types of institutional and

    community infrastructure could be used in the longer term, a range of pilots were also integrated throughout the

    life of the project. This included the piloting of the use of Savings and Credit Cooperatives (SACCOs) for providing a

    conduit for farmers to access credit beyond the life of the WB investment program. It also included the testing of

    the use of a range of disruptive agricultural technologies (DATs) selected as service providers to CIGs. These features

    are now integrated into the recently effective NAVCDP.

12. These components and the related infrastructure also served as the basis for the pathways of the project’s impact

    on resilience. Given that the TIMPs used in the project had already been selected as technologies proven to increase

    the resilience of farmers based on the research conducted by KALRO and their validation based on the FAO

    methodology, access to information about TIMPS and their adoption was the main barrier to be addressed. As such,

    the institutional infrastructure created under the project provided farmers with access to key information for

    managing risk through the weather and market information system and for their adoption of TIMPs to directly

    address resilience of investments at field level, reinforced by farmer training and access to finance.

    Component 5, on Contingency Emergency Response was designed to finance eligible expenditures under the

    Immediate Response Mechanism (IRM) in case of natural or man-made crises or disasters, severe economic

    shocks, or other crises and emergencies in Kenya. In February 2020, Government of Kenya (GOK) developed a Pest

    Management Plan (PMP) that was used to activate a Contingency Emergency Recovery Implementation Plan (CERIP)

    to respond to the desert locust invasion emergency. The objective was to procure chemicals and associated spray

    equipment, surveillance, support operations in the control of the locust invasion and to deliver related capacity

    building.

    Project Development Objectives (PDOs)

13. The project development objective (PDO) in the project Appraisal Document (PAD, p. 6) aimed to: “increase

    agricultural productivity and build resilience to climate change risks in the targeted smallholder farming and pastoral

    communities in Kenya, and in the event of an Eligible Crisis or Emergency, to provide immediate and effective

    response”.



    Key Expected Outcomes and Outcome Indicators

14. The key outcomes based on the PDO were as follows:

               •       Increase productivity in the targeted smallholder farming and pastoral communities in Kenya

               •       Build resilience to climate change risks in the targeted smallholder farming and pastoral communities in Kenya

    • Provide immediate and effective response in the event of an Eligible Crisis or Emergency

15. KCSAP’s main focus was on the promotion of Climate-Smart Agriculture and the project aimed to reach about

    521,500 households as direct beneficiaries across the 24 participating counties,3 in which 6 wards were participating.

    KCSAP promoted 14 priority value chains namely - Sorghum, green gram, Irish potato, local poultry, apiculture, meats

    (beef), dairy (cow and camel milk), millet, pigeon pea, cassava, tomato, fish, bananas, and cotton. During the

    restructuring of the project in November 2020, the project incorporated support for other commodities through the

    Government of Kenya National Value Chains Support Program including the rehabilitation of coffee production in

    Nyeri, Tharaka Nithi and Machakos. The selection of these value chains was based on analysis with focus on delivering

    reallocating funds to better serve the more successful component elements. These changes and the

    restructuring did not fundamentally affect the core structure of the project, but they introduced some

    innovative solutions which both increased the beneficiary numbers and helped assure implementation

    success. These changes targeted increased agility in the flow of funds and in building capacity at all

    beneficiary groups in their capacity to apply for the different types of funding and linked both capacity

IV. BANK PERFORMANCE, COMPLIANCE ISSUES, AND RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME



 A. QUALITY OF MONITORING AND EVALUATION (M&E)

    M&E Design

57. A monitoring and evaluation (M&E) and management information system (MIS) was set up at the national and

    county levels to enforce a results-based project M&E and provide the basis for an evidence-based decision-making

    process. These systems were designed to provide concurrent feedback to key stakeholders about progress toward

    the project’s key results. The objective was real-time monitoring of activities, processes, inputs, and outputs to track

    achievements against targets through a web-based M&E and MIS system. In addition, the project planned for a series

    of quantitative impact evaluations.

58. The project implementation manual (PIM) and M&E manual were developed early in project implementation to

    guide the monitoring implementation activities and targets. The approach referenced a number of tools including

    the KCSAP results framework; annual work plan and budget; and monitoring and evaluation plan. Others are financial

    and procurement plan; data collection tools; reporting tools (status, surveys, case study, aide memoire, reports); web

    based PMIS; and project technical implementation guidelines.

59. The M&E manual was fairly comprehensive, identifying indicators to track progress in relation to the project theory

    of change, and explaining the conduct of evaluation in KCSAP, including baseline survey, mid-term review, project

    completion evaluation as well as more targeted evaluation studies. It further operationalized monitoring of the

    results chains to be implemented under 9 M&E work streams to track project progress and outcomes, ensure

    accountability and supporting learning among stakeholders. This included tracking progress of county, ward and

    community micro- and sub-project planning; adoption of TIMPs through CIG/VMG development and sub-project

    implementation; and improved functional performance of the national agricultural research, extension and input

    supply system. Others were tracking of increased use of/access to risk mitigation tools, effective project management,

    quality management of monitoring data, aggregation of monitoring data, review and evaluation, and reporting.



    M&E Implementation

60. The most important indicators were tracked monthly starting on an excel sheet and then through an extensive MIS

    developed for KCSAP and digital dashboard. Because of the three-tiered implementation arrangement (national,

    county, and community levels), the institutional arrangements for M&E involved:

    Quality at Entry

79. The Program had a robust design which was well informed by lessons learned from Brazil and Mexico and aligned

    with the Government’s strategy and the Bank priorities. Overall, the technical, E&S and Fiduciary Systems

80. However, the original design was somewhat complex with several elements which were not agile enough to

    respond to the reality of implementation. This includes for example the appointing of the Normalized Difference

    Vegetation Index (NDVI) agent which would be a feature of the development of the Big Data platform. While

    important, it was considered more efficient to supply these services through a private contractor. This also includes

    a range of features in relation to seed development which were not picked up in the project. Nevertheless, the

    program managed to increase the availability of quality seeds by training farmers as local level multipliers.

    mitigation measures, more could have been done to mobilize county governments at the beginning of the project.

    mechanism based on experience in India. Such a mechanism should be integrated in similar designs in the future.

 D. RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME



87. The Program introduced key institutions as well as good practices which are likely to sustain the Program

    development outcomes.

    • At the community level, the various layers of institutions created are likely to be sustained over time, even

       though some might require to be repurposed to survive well beyond the project life. The trained lead farmers

       will be able to continue providing services to their CIGs/VMGs, the FPOs will continue to provide access to markets

       and SACCOs access to finance. The CDDCs proved to be very important to support the CIGs/VMGs but they were

       created for the implementation of KCSAP and closely linked to the program and its micro-projects. Various options

       are considered for ensuring their continued support activities, including through their linkages to SACCOs. The

       objective under the new NAVCDP is to bring the model piloted under KCSAP to scale beyond the ward and counties

       which had been selected under KSCAP.

    • The adoptions of TIMPs should have a long-term impact on beneficiaries, and their user base is likely to continue

       to grow through permeation into communities. An important feature of KCSAP was to enable the adoption by

       farmers of a selection of climate smart TIMPs. Many of these TIMPs did not require capital to be adopted. This is

       the reason why, even some CIGs/VMGs which were not granted micro projects adopted them. These TIMPs have

       proven to increase the productivity of farmers adopting them. It is therefore likely that the impact will continue

       in the longer term. The number of beneficiaries (89%) satisfied with relevance, timeliness and effectiveness of

       TIMP advisory services and the extent of the adoption (595,366 hectares at completion) provides insights that

       these TIMPs are likely to be sustained.

    • The enhancement of capacity of government and county government officers will continue to support activities

       of the public sector. The extension of the network of agro-weather stations and the development of the KALRO

       Big Data platform also creates a core infrastructure which is now at the center of the digital transformation of

       agriculture supported by the government. The impact of these investments under KCSAP are therefore

       foundational and will impact the government’s activities in the long run. Some of these activities still need further

       investments to be used more efficiently for the delivery of actionable advice to farmers and governments. These

       investments are already integrated into the new World Bank agriculture portfolio which is taking over the building

       of this digital infrastructure.

88. However, some specific risks which might affect the Program development outcomes:

    • Capacity constraints might undermine the further development of the KALRO Big Data Platform. The digital

       transformation of government agencies requires accessing new types of competencies which can be difficult to

       procure for a government agency.

    • A sustainable model for sustaining the supply of services to farmers through DATs might be needed. The access

       to DATs was funded by the KCSAP program. It is not clear at this stage whether farmers would be ready to pay



                                                                                                               Page 22 of 69

        The World Bank

        Kenya Climate Smart Agriculture Project (P154784)







         fees to access these services. NAVCDP took over this for KCSAP, nevertheless, it would be important for the

         government and county governments to engage in a process of identifying the best ways to move forward with

         those services. At this stage, the government is interested in the model of Agripreneurs which could be a good

         conduit for the delivery of these services. But options can be identified around the world. Many countries are for

         example providing subsidies to registration fees, meaning that they subsidize a percentage of the registration fee

         to the digital service. The services could also be procured by extension service officers for the supply of public

         services.

    •    FPOs business models might not be sustainable at this stage. Margins for FPOs are small and they might need

         support developing business plans to ensure their activities can sustain any shocks.



V. LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

89. The success of the KCSAP project is considered along two dimensions, the shorter-term implementation

    success, and the longer-term impact on national strategy. First, KCSAP included a range of institutional

    innovations which amplified its reach and facilitated disbursement, allowing it to exceed targeted indicators,

    despite the triggering of the Emergence Response Mechanism and related reallocation. Second, the project

    impacted the agriculture sector trajectory in Kenya by creating foundational institutional infrastructure at

    community and national levels which will serve new programming and also help ensure sustainability.

      • At the community level, the new World Bank IPFs NAVCDP and Food Systems Resilience Project will

          continue to work with the infrastructure already created under the KCSAP project and will bring the

          model to scale by supporting development in the wards not previously covered.

      • At the national level, the program was instrumental in putting Kenya on the path to the digital

          transformation of its agriculture sector.

90. The project generated positive lessons regarding the value of community-based organizations but also how

    it can increase its reach and impact through the combining this traditional approach with digital

    innovations. Some of the most important lessons of the project are the following:



 Lesson 1: The use of community level organizations organized by different function and tier or level of association,

 allowed community members to acquire different skills, services or funds through their membership in one or more

 organizations, and with provision of grants/subsidized funding, tailored to the target group and purpose of the

 organization.

91. While a CDD approach is not new, the program tested a range of innovative approaches based on lessons

    from other regions which increased efficiency, accountability, and buy-in. This allowed farmers to be trained

    more efficiently, and contributed to the sustainability of the program. In particular, three community level

    organizations proved essential to the program’s success, supporting the CIGs and VMGs and the scaling up of

    their activities: CDDCs, FPOs, and SACCOs. This institutional infrastructure was then integrated into the

    NAVCDP program from 2022.



 Lesson 2: The adaptation of the program and of some of its features also demonstrated the value of

 institutional learning and flexibility during implementation and in response to lessons on the ground.

92. Some small changes in the way the program was implemented allowed it to vastly increase its reach and

    disbursement efficiency. For example:

     • the training of lead farmers was a key feature adopted during project implementation.

     • the team proved creative in addressing disbursement constraints in the context of the multiple layers

          created by the devolved nature of the government. The World Bank team created a flow of fund

          framework which is now used in other World Bank projects not only in agriculture but also in other



                                                                                                              Page 23 of 69

          The World Bank

          Kenya Climate Smart Agriculture Project (P154784)







             sectors. Similar approaches could also be relevant in other countries with devolved governments. See

             box 2 in section III for more details about the mechanism put in place.



     Lesson 3: The project leveraged data, digital technologies, and innovation for the delivery of last mile services

     to farmers with the creation of the Big Data Platform and its national farmer registry, and the One Million

     Farmer Platform for providing new service not only to CIGs/VMGs but potentially to all farmers in Kenya.

    93. The Big Data Platform and the Kenya Agricultural Observatory Platform (KAOP) at the Kenya Agriculture

        Livestock and Research Organization (KALRO) linked 5.5 million farmers to these platforms. They provided

        comprehensive data, information, and agronomic advisories to enable national and county-level agricultural

        institutions, farmers, and other stakeholders to access high-resolution geospatial agro-meteorological data

        and customized advice. This created the ground for enabling more data-driven decision making and policies

        for the government with the support to the Big Data Platform which is now a core asset for the government

        strategy.

    94. The One Million Farmers Platform (OMFP) brought together AgTech Start-ups, otherwise called DATs selected

        by innovation challenges and county governments and facilitated their partnership. It demonstrated the use

        of digital technologies to enhance productivity and profitability of farmers, through extension information

        and credit to farmers, leveraging tech platforms to enable access to markets, and use of apps and platforms

        to enable access to good quality inputs like seeds and fertilizers for farmers.



     Lesson 4: Critical to the integration of innovative approaches was the additional funding and technical

     partnerships, sourced through ancillary trust funds.

    95. These additional sources of financing enabled the World Bank to provide substantial technical assistance (TA)

        and technical support at multiple levels in the government.



      Lesson 5: The project design created successful connections between the community and national levels

      which is key to its success and longer-term impact.

    96. An important feature of the KCSAP design was the combination of the community level component

        (component 1) and the components serving the development of a national level ecosystem (component 2 and

        3). This approach allowed for an effective diffusion of nationally generated knowledge though local level

        networks, but also to reach beneficiaries beyond the selected ward and counties and the national strategy

        overall. It also created some foundational infrastructure at the national level which now feature in the

        continuing NARIG and FSRP and which enable a more rapid scale up of the activities already initiated under

        KCSAP. This includes the big data platform and the farmer registry.



      Lesson 6: While M&E overall was fairly strong, a more comprehensive set of indicators to better assess the

      multidimensionality of resilience building would increase understanding of impact pathways.

    97. While the M&E was based on large number of intermediate indicators which enabled the tracking of the

        progress of the implementation of the project, more attention could have been given to resilience indicators.

        The PDO outcome indicators rely on some core assumptions regarding the impact of adoption of TIMPs on

        resilience. It would have been useful to more thoroughly articulate the causality between the adoption of

        TIMPs and increase in resilience, and to explore if other proxy measures for resilience could usefully have

        been adopted. While an increase in productivity is one indicator of increased resilience, the project could have

        identified others to include so that there could be a more multidimensional approach to resilience building to

        help strengthen the monitoring/assessment of resilience.

                                      ANNEX 4. EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS



                       Kenya Climate-Smart Agriculture Project (KCSAP) (P 4784)

                           Implementation Completion and Results Review



 1.     This Efficiency Analysis of the Kenya Climate Smart Agricultural Project (KCSAP) assesses how

 economically resources and inputs of the Project were converted to results. The Analysis was prepared in

 February-March 2024, as contribution to the Implementation Completion and Results (ICR) Review.



 2.      Chapter I of this Analysis summarizes the Project background. Chapter II briefly discusses the

 methodology and assumptions of the Economic and Financial Analysis (EFA). Chapter III summarizes the

 Project achievements, benefits, and beneficiaries. This information is essential to determine the scope

 and outreach of the EFA. Chapters IV presents the financial analysis, Chapter V the economic analysis.

 Chapter VI assesses Project design and implementation efficiency.



    I.   Project Background

 3.      The Project Development Objective (PDO) of KCSAP was “to increase agricultural productivity and

 build resilience to climate change risks in the targeted smallholder farming and pastoral communities in

 Kenya, and in the event of an Eligible Crisis or Emergency, to provide immediate and effective response”.



 4.      KCSAP had 5 components. Component 1: Upscaling Climate-Smart Agricultural Practices,

 promoted the adoption of Technologies, Innovations and Management Practices (TIMPs) by farmers.

 Component 2: Strengthening Climate-Smart Agriculture Research and Seed Systems, supported the

 development and validation of context-specific CSA TIMPS. It also developed sustainable seed production

 and distribution systems. Component 3: Supporting Agro-weather, Market, Climate and Advisory Services,

 financed building institutional and technical capacity for agro-meteorological observation and forecasting,

 agricultural statistics collection and analyses, and market advisory services. Component 4: Project

 Coordination and Management supported Project coordination and management at both national and

 county levels. Component 5: Contingency Emergency Response, was triggered in 2020 and supported to

 the Emergency Locust Response Project (ELRP).



 5.      Component 1 was implemented by the National Project Coordination Unit (NPCU) and County

 Project Coordination Units (CPCU). Component 2 was implemented by the NPCU and Kenya Agricultural

 & Livestock Research Organization (KALRO), Component 3 by the NPCU, KALRO and Kenya Meteorological

 Department (KMD), Component 4 by NPCU and CPCUs.



 6.     The direct beneficiaries of the Project at design were estimated at 521,500 households of

 smallholder farmers, agro-pastoralists, and pastoralists.



 7.      KCSAP intervened in 24 Counties, of which six in Arid Areas (Marsabit, Isiolo, Tana River, Garissa,

 Wajir, and Mandera); nine in Semi-Arid Areas (West Pokot, Baringo, Laikipia, Machakos, Nyeri, Tharaka

 Nithi, Lamu, Taita Taveta, and Kajiado); and nine in Medium-to-High Rainfall Areas (Busia, Siaya,

 Nyandarua, Bomet, Kericho, Kakamega, Uasin Gishu, Elgeyo Marakwet, and Kisumu).

 8.      KCSAP promoted 14 priority value chains: Sorghum, green gram, Irish potato, local poultry,

 apiculture, meat (beef), dairy (cow and camel milk), millet, pigeon pea, cassava, tomato, aquaculture, and

 bananas.



 9.      The KCSAP was approved in February 2017, became effective in May 2017, and was scheduled to

 close on 31st January 2022. Three no-cost extensions for a total of 22 months were given and the Project

 was closed on November 30, 2023. The Mid-term Review (MTR) took place in September 2020.



 10.     At design, the total Project cost was estimated at US$279.75 million, financed through an

 International Development Association (IDA) grant of US$250 million, national government counterpart

 funds (US$15.9 million equivalent), county governments contributions (US$8.3 million), and beneficiary

 contributions (US$5.5 million equivalent). The beneficiaries’ contributions were essentially co-financing

 for matching grants.



     II.   Methodology and Assumptions of the Efficiency Analysis

 11.    The methodological approach of this EFA follows that of Gittinger (1982)5, Belli et al. (2001)6 and

 recent World Bank guidelines published on economic and financial analysis7.



 12.      Data Collection and Sources. This Efficiency Analysis is based on a broad range of qualitative and

 quantitative data sources. The main sources are: (i) the Project Appraisal Document (PAD) and MTR

 report; (ii) the Implementation Status & Results Reports (ISR) and mission Aide-memoires; (iii) the KCSAP

 Project Completion Report (December 2023)8, as well as the End of Project Evaluation Report for KCSAP9

 (December 6, 2023), and the Impact Evaluation Report for KCSAP (September 2023), all prepared at the

 request of the Borrower; (iv) impact stories and knowledge products published on the Project website. In

 addition, discussions with farmers and other stakeholders, as well as members of the NPCU during a field

 mission from 20 to 27 March 2024 were a much-valued and essential source of information.



 13.     Financial analysis. From the perspective of beneficiaries, positive financial returns on investment

 are important for the Project to convince farmers and other investors that sufficient incentive exists for

 their participation. The assumption is that sustainability of the investments requires a financial or

 commercial profitability. Fourteen (14) models are developed, one for each priority value chain, each of

 them with a ‘with’ and ‘without’ Project ( P and OP) situation to analy e the financial performance of

 the investments that were supported under Component 1. The financial discount rate used is 14.63

 percent; the duration is 10 years for the models. Core financial performance indicators used are the

 Internal Rate of Return (IRR), Benefit/Cost Ratio (B/C ratio), Net Present Value (NPV), and Return to Family

 Labour. The exchange rate is KES 137 for 1 US$ (15 March 2024).

 of economic costs and benefits. This economic analysis of KCSAP is based on (a) value chain investments

 Achievement of Intermediate Results Targets

 19.      Table 2 presents the Intermediate Results Indicators of KCSAP. Most indicators were achieved.



 20.     Component 1: Upscaling Climate-Smart Agricultural Practices. Component 1 aimed to scale up

 the implementation of CSA practices to achieve triple-wins of increased productivity; enhanced resilience

 (adaptation) and reduced GHG emissions (mitigation) per unit of output, as co-benefits. These objectives

 were largely achieved.



 21.      The entry point for scaling-up the use of TIMPs and good agricultural practices (GAPs) in the 14

 priority value chains was the mobilization of common interest groups (CIGs) and vulnerable and

 marginalized groups (VMGs). The CIGs reached 428,800 members, the VMGs 107,600 members. In

 addition, at value chain level, 134,200 beneficiaries were reached through PPPs with farmers’

 organizations (FPOs) and private sector, and 213,200 beneficiaries through sub-projects at county level.

 Some members of CIGs/VMGs were also involved in PPPs and sub-projects, and several CIGs/VMGs, which

 resulted in double counting.



 22.     Out of 23,525 micro-project proposals, 11,200 micro-projects for the CIGs11 and VMGs12,

 representing 251,240 beneficiaries13, were selected and financed. The CIGs and VMGs, who’s micro-

 projects did not receive funding, benefitted from extension support and other services. The micro-project

 grants were valued at KES 6.95 billion (approximately US$ 64.3 million), with a 100 percent absorption

 rate. The CIGs contributed 10 percent, which enhanced ownership.



 23.      At county level, the Project financed 190 sub-projects14 to address opportunities and bottlenecks

 along the value chains. Each sub-project reached at least 500 but up to 1,500 farmers. Around each sub-

 The sub-projects were distributed as follows: livestock vaccination (21) and breed improvement (5),

 livestock markets (3), water sources (boreholes, water pans, earth dams, spring protection) and farmer

 led irrigation projects (141) on 11,442 acres; rangeland rehabilitation (615 acres), agroforestry (395 acres)

   IV.   Financial Analysis

 38.     This financial analysis looks at the impact of the TIMPs on smallholder farming in the 14 priority

 value chains– Sorghum, green gram, Irish potato, millet, pigeon pea, cassava, tomato, bananas, dairy,

 aquaculture, poultry, shoats (sheep & goats), meat (beef), and apiculture. Additional models were

 developed for seed multiplication and fodder production.



 39.     The Results Framework, the KCSAP Impact report (p. 75) and KCSAP Project Completion Report

 (p. 87) confirmed that direct and tangible Project benefits resulted from significantly higher crop and

 livestock productivity, resulting in surplus production, higher sales and household incomes. These benefits

 in the With Project (WP) situation came from the adoption of CSA TIMPs. For crop production, these CSA

 technologies included the use of improved (certified) CSA seeds, improved agronomic and crop husbandry

 practices (line seeding, mulching, manure, additional chemical fertilizer, etc..). As non-organic fertilizer is

 quite expensive, farmers often preferred low doses of additional fertilizer. For the livestock value chains,

 the TIMPs were often based on improved breeds (also AI for dairy animals), improved feeding practices

 and fodder production, vaccination and disease control, and improved animal husbandry (herd

 management, animal health, ...), better linkages with dairy cooperatives, slaughterhouses, and markets.

   V.     Economic Analysis

 45.     This economic analysis of KCSAP is based on (a) value chain investments in productive goods; and

 (b) monetized environmental benefits expected to accrue from reduced GHG emissions and increased

 carbon sequestration. Conservative assumptions and parameters have been applied, to avoid

 overestimation of benefits and provide realistic results.

 Investments in productive goods

 46.      The core economic benefit flow is based on farmers that benefitted from the 11,200 micro-

 projects and adopted TIMPs under Component 1. According to the KCSAP Completion Report,

 approximately 33 percent of these households were involved in crop value chains, while 66 percent in

 livestock. In addition, sub-projects, PPPs, Inception Grants, focused on solving bottlenecks along the

 priority value chains, which was required for the development of the priority value chains and often a

 precondition to maintain attractive farmgate prices and market access. The investments under

 Component 2, such as validation of TIMPs, training of lead farmers, and multiplication of seed, is a

 precondition for efficient and effective investments under Component 1. An adoption rate of 80 percent

 is assumed. The typical spill-over or leverage effect of economic public goods under Component 3 was

 considered using a multiplier factor of 1.1.

 Calculation of environmental benefits

 47.     The carbon balance is defined as the net balance across all GHGs expressed in CO2 equivalents

 (CO2e) that will be emitted or sequestered due to the Projects (WP), as compared to a business-as-usual

 scenario (WOP). A 20-year period is being considered.



 48.     The environmental externalities were calculated by using EX-ACT, a tool developed by the FAO,

 to quantify the volume GHGs mitigated by the Project. This allowed to estimate its economic value of this

 GHG mitigation, which was included in the economic analysis. EX-ACT is a land-based accounting system,

 estimating CO2e stock changes (i.e. emissions or sinks of CO2) expressed in equivalent tons of CO2 per





                                                                                                       Page 55 of 69

The World Bank

Kenya Climate Smart Agriculture Project (P154784)







 hectare and year. The result shows a marginal carbon emission savings for the Project of 9,803,397 tCO2eq

 over a period of 20 years, equivalent to 490,170 tCO2eq per year.



 49.     The monetary value of the GHG balance has been estimated and considered as economic benefit

 of the Project in the EFA. The orld Bank’s Guidance Note on Shadow Price of Carbon in Economic

 Analysis (September 2021) recommends “projects’ economic analysis use a low and high estimate for

 carbon pricing. In the case of KCSAP, carbon prices increase from 39 US$/tCO2eq in 2019 to 65

 US$/tCO2eq in 2042, and high estimate of the carbon price increasing from 78 US$/tCO2eq in 2019 to 131

 US$/tCO2e in 2042.

 valuation of environmental benefits. The scenario without the valuation of environmental benefits is

   VI.    Project Efficiency

 52.     Efficiency is a measure of how economically project resources are converted into results. It is

 therefore related directly to economic analysis.



 53.       Overall, the KCSAP implementation structures were largely effective in carrying out their intended

 functions. Also, the Project implementation strategies were largely followed, although putting in place

 the required county-level implementation capacity and building their capacity took more time than

 initially planned. Also, the COVID-19 pandemic and national elections caused additional delays. On the

 other hand, the magnitude of CIG, VMG and FPO mobilization, preparation of proposals and speed of

 disbursement, once the implementation mechanism was operational, shows the efficiency of the

 approach at county and community level.



 54.      The expenditures for sub-projects and micro-projects were as per budgetary plans. The

 disbursements of funds for both sub-projects and micro-projects were generally delayed, largely because

 of the fund management structures at the counties, and the change in leadership in some counties and at

 the national government level. After capacity building of these institutions, disbursements were fast.

 Detail on KSCAP components and pathways to households and ecosystem impact



 1. Component 1 was the channel for the CDD approach and the direct household impacts. This

    component was implemented by the National Project Coordination Unit (NPCU) and County Project

    Coordination Units (CPCU) and established the implementing and oversight structures at the

    community levels. CIGs and VMGs activities were facilitated by CDDCs which undertook procurement

    of inputs, training materials along with supporting monitoring implementation of micro projects. To

    help CDDCs’ perform their role and mandate, an MoU was signed and effected between the county

    governments and these community institutions. By creating organized community institutions and

    providing them grants and management training through service providers, the project enabled the

    creation of a functional institutional infrastructure for the delivery of the investment program. This

    included the combination of training for a selection of TIMPs and grants for micro-projects to finance

    their implementation. Investments at the county level with larger infrastructure investments through

    sub-projects, such as irrigation investments, also directly impacted farmers’ productivity. Finally,

    given the partnership with the private sector, and later with the integration of support to Farmer

    Producer Organizations (FPOs) after the restructuring of the program in 2020 and their support

    including through inclusion grants and enterprise development grants (EDG), the supported CIGs and

    VMGs were also impacted through better access to markets and better prices for their products. As

    such the development of this community infrastructure empowered county governments and

    government agencies in the delivery of services to their farmers. The capacity of the community and

    the county government to organize around these groups was critical to deliver services and for the

    program’s disbursement capacity. Since 2020, this component also enabled access to a range of

    digitally enabled services (inputs, credit, market access, soil testing, extension advice) through the

    partnerships that the project facilitated between the County Governments and Digital Startups.



 2. Components 2 and 3 support the development of the public sector ecosystem and the delivery of

    TIMPs and services to farmers. Component 2 was implemented by the NPCU and the Kenya

    Agricultural and Livestock Research Organization (KALRO) and Component 3 by these agencies and

    the Kenya Meteorological Department (KMD). These components included elements of building

    infrastructure (Agro-weather stations and the Big Data Platform), research and analytics capacity for

    the various institutions involved, but also focused on the delivery capacity to farmers. This includes

    the selection and training of farmers on TIMPs, targeted to their needs, facilitated by the community

    infrastructure developed under component 1. This component also supported the development of

    good quality seed systems through support to both KALRO and by training CIGs to produce good

    quality seeds.

 Innovative implementation mechanisms - creation of an operational account to enable disbursement

 of the World Bank funds at the county and community level.



 The flow of funds arrangement under the KCSAP program was defined as follows: the World Bank would

 allocate funds to two different designated accounts: DA1 for county level activities, meaning Component

 1 and part of Component 4, representing 65% of the funds, and DA2 for national level activities, meaning

 Components 2, 3 and part of Component 4, representing the remaining 35% of the funds.



 The fiscal year in Kenya starts in July and the budget for DA2 for counties had to be negotiated at the

 National Assembly. The process leading to the disbursement of the DA2 budget was the following: On

 each September 30, the budget figures proposed for the next fiscal year are frozen. The budget approval

 process then takes 9 months until the beginning of the fiscal year. However, the budget approval process

 is separate from the disbursement process, which requires another round of discussions at the National

 Assembly and the Senate. As a consequence, the funds were not reaching counties in July (the start of the

 FY), but usually only by December 1st, in other words 6 months after the beginning of the fiscal year.

 Moreover, only a first tranche of 50% would then be released. The next tranche of 25 % would be released

 in March, only if the first tranche of 50% had been fully disbursed. Finally, the last tranche is released in

 May or June irrespective of the full disbursement of the second tranche, but the funds had to be used

 before the end of the fiscal year et end June.



 From DA1, funds are transferred to the County Revenue Funds (CRF), which transfers them to a Special

 Purpose Account (SPA). Both the CRFs and the SPAs are managed by the Integrated Financial Management

 Information System (IFMIS). The IFMIS is an Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) used at both the National

 and County levels of government for enhancing accountability and transparency. It is a core tool for the





                                                                                                          Page 65 of 69

    The World Bank

    Kenya Climate Smart Agriculture Project (P154784)







     reform of the Public Finance Management system in Kenya for budget formulation and execution, public

     procurement, and financial reporting. However, the use of IFMIS created further constraints in the funds

     disbursement as the IFMIS system typically had multiple down times during the year which meant that

     expenditures had to take place only during the periods IFMIS was fully functional.



     To allow more agility in the system, the World Bank team discussed with the governors the creation of an

     additional operational account in each county which was linked to the SPA but would allow for

     expenditures to be undertaken throughout the fiscal year. The mechanism used County score cards and

     a ranking based on the level of disbursement of the program, under which ‘better’ Counties were

     rewarded with a higher fund allocation. By ensuring that project expenditures were spread out

     throughout the year, the counties were able to absorb the funds that were released in the initial tranche

     (typically released in December) and receive the subsequent second and third tranches





     Description of the role of three essential institutions in KSCAP supporting CIGs/VMGs



•    CDDCs were essential for managing procurement and for supporting the development of proposals by

     CIGs and VMGs. These CDDCs were the link between the governors and community, acting as lobbyist for

     specific projects. These CDDCs are made of one representative of each CIG with one CDDC per ward (group

     of 40 to 60 CIG) responsible for collecting and evaluating the CIG microproject proposals, prior to

     to support their creation, access to basic hardware such as computers to enable the digitalization of the

     management of their activities and the training of a bookkeeper.

 County Performance Ranking- Data driven boost to implementation efficiency



 KCSAP introduced an innovative system of ranking project counties based on their relative performance

 on a set of overall and component level performance indicators. The performance ranking approach first

 tested during the mid-term review of KCSAP, combined visual analytics that clearly communicated relative

 performance of project counties through scatter graphs with tabulated scoring on all indicators to arrive

 at top performing counties as well as laggards. The scatter graphs called performance quadrants were

 utilized to place counties across four quadrants of varied performance levels, providing a visual snapshot

 of all counties on key indicators. The example given below maps various counties on two indicators viz.

 Number of Micro-projects disbursed and Number of CIGs linked to POs. For these indicators, benchmark

 thresholds are pegged at 350 Micro-projects and 500 CIGs respectively, effectively creating 4 different

 performance quadrants.

 The performance quadrants became immensely popular among project stakeholders for their

 effectiveness in showcasing county performance and a position in the right upper quadrant (high

 performance) became an aspirational goal for CPCUs. The county performance ranking was undertaken

 every six months in conjunction with implementation support missions and the final ranking along with

 performance quadrants were presented in follow up meetings with county Governors, thus creating

 awareness and buy-in at the highest level. The best performing counties awarded Gold, Silver and Bronze
