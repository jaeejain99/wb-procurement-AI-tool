I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT



A. Country Context



1.      Since the introduction of economic reforms in 1986, Vietnam has been a development success story . Growth in

real gross domestic product (GDP) per capita has averaged 5.5 percent annually since 1990, leading to the quadrupling

of real GDP per capita. External trade has been the major driver of growth, much of it powered by strong foreign direct

investment. This rapid development has resulted in structural economic transformation, with agriculture falling from 40

percent of GDP in the late 1980s to less than 20 percent in recent years, with a related rise in services and industry.



2.     Economic growth has been inclusive, yielding shared prosperity and substantial gains in poverty reduction.

Evidence of inclusive growth is the relatively small increase in the Gini coefficient, a measure of income inequality, from

33 in 1993 to 35.7 in 2018.1 During this period, poverty fell dramatically, from 50 percent in the early 1990s to 1.9 percent

in 2018, based on the metric of US$1.90-a-day.2 Using the more precise General Statistics Office–World Bank standard

adopted by the Government of Vietnam (GOV), poverty incidence fell from about 58 percent in the early 1990s to 9.8

percent in 2016.3 At the same time, access to basic infrastructure has also improved substantially and electricity is

available to almost all households as of 2015, up from less than half of households in 1993. 4



3.     Vietnam’s rapid economic transformation, however, has led to uncoordinated and suboptimal urbanization .

Vietnam’s urban population has grown at 3.4 percent annually since the late 1980s, from less than 13 million urban

residents in 1986 to more than 33 million today.5 Urban areas now contribute about 70 percent of the country’s GDP,

according to the most recent estimate by the Ministry of Construction (MOC). At the same time, Vietnam’s current

urbanization model is characterized by low and stagnant levels of urban density, with industrial zones developed ahead

of demand, and a proliferation of small, fragmented, and poorly connected urban centers.



4.       Vietnam is exposed to weather-related hazards such as typhoons, floods, and drought, exacerbated by poorly

controlled development and climate change. The average annual disaster damage and losses are estimated at US$11

billion, or 4.5 percent of GDP, making Vietnam’s disaster losses relative to GDP among the highest in the world. 6 Disaster

risk is especially high along the 3,300-kilometer coastline, where there is high prevalence of flooding, storm surge, and

typhoons. Inadequate policy responses to climate-related risks, inefficient coordination of planning and investments

across sectors, and a lack of coordinated management drive the high losses. With the majority of cities situated along

the coast or in the two main river deltas, urban areas are especially exposed to flood risk. This exposure results in elevated

climate and disaster risks because of the lack of risk-informed urban planning and insufficient development of basic

B. Sectoral and Institutional Context



Sectoral Context



5.       The central government has identified Vinh City as an important growth center given its strategic location. The

city lies on the main national highway and railway networks connecting the northern and southern provinces of Vietnam

and serves as the political, economic, and cultural center of the North Central Coast region. According to the city’s

Department of Planning and Investment, Vinh City’s GDP has grown by 8.5 percent per year since 2016 due to a highly

industrialized economy, with agriculture accounting for only 1.3 percent of the local economy. The number of visitors

has been increasing by over 4 percent per year, and annual tourism revenues have grown by 15-16 percent since 2015.

The Master Plan of Vinh City to 2030, and Vision to 2050, approved by the Prime Minister in 2015, envisages rapid

population growth, from 520,000 inhabitants today to one million by 2030.



6.      Despite ambitious development plans, Vinh City is struggling to keep up with the rising demand for basic urban

infrastructure, both in the established urban centers and in urban expansion areas . To varying degrees, the city’s

sewage and sanitation systems are inadequate, resulting in frequent direct discharge of untreated domestic wastewater

into rivers, generating water pollution and serious health risks to the surrounding populations. Increasing levels of solid

waste generation and uncontrolled dumping add to the environmental pollution, clog drains, and worsen public health

conditions. Roads, particularly in the older parts of the city, are too narrow and degraded to enable efficient intra-city

mobility. Addressing these shortcomings has been a top priority for Vinh City, as evidenced in both the central

government’s Directive for Vinh City Development No. 2468/QD-TTg (2015) and the Politburo’s Resolution No. 26 –

NQ/TW for Nghe An Province (2013).



7.     The uncoordinated nature of recent infrastructure development has resulted in rising flood risk, with

substantial social and economic consequences. The city is exposed to fluvial flooding caused by rainfall runoff from the

inland hills, pluvial flooding in the city itself, and coastal flooding and winds from typhoons. Rapid housing and industrial

development, along with rural to urban land conversion, has led to increased runoff, while the urbanization of historical

retention areas (for example, Hung Hoa Commune) has reduced storage capacity during extreme flood events.

Consequently, the city has experienced a greater number of flood events over the past decade, affecting the regional

economy and disrupting the lives of residents. A flooding event in 2019 damaged or destroyed more than 5,000 houses. 8

and Investment (DPI) plays the leading role in the development of master plans and ensuring that investments proposed

by line departments are aligned with those plans. The Department of Construction (DOC) is responsible for drainage

C. Relevance to Higher Level Objectives



10.     Nghe An Province’s Vinh City Priority Infrastructure and Urban Resilience Development Project (VPIUR) is

aligned with the World Bank Group’s (WBG) Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for the Socialist Republic of Vietnam

for the period FY18-22 (Report No. 111771-VN). The operation will support the achievement of the CPF Focus Area 1,

“Enabling Inclusive Growth and Private Sector Participation,” particularly Objective 4, “Improve planning, management,

and delivery of infrastructure and land in cities,” by investing in drainage, wastewater, and road infrastructure. The

project will also contribute to the achievement of Focus Area 3, “Ensuring Environmental Sustainability and Resilience,”

particularly Objective 10, “Increase climate resilience and strengthen disaster risk management,” by investing in

integrated flood protection measures, flood modeling, and an early-warning system. The operation will also contribute

to the WBG’s Twin Goals of eliminating extreme poverty and boosting shared prosperity by increasing the access of the

city’s bottom 40 percent income group to improved basic services, public spaces, and connectivity.



11.     The project is aligned with Vietnam’s long-term climate mitigation and adaptation strategies . On adaptation,

the project directly supports the adaptation priorities identified in Vietnam’s Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC,

updated in October 2022), as detailed in the National Adaptation Plan (NAP) 2021-2023 (December 2022), on “enhancing

resilience and adaptive capacity of natural, social, and economic systems for sustainable livelihoods” and “reducing

disaster risk, damage from natural disasters and increasingly extreme climate events”. Specifically, the project addresses

“tasks and solutions” outlined in the NAP, including the construction and upgrading of water drainage systems to prevent

flooding caused by heavy rains, floods, and storm surges; investment, upgrading and modernization of climate change

monitoring, hydrometeorological monitoring, and specialized disaster monitoring system; building capacity in prevention

and control of flash flooding, landslides, storm prevention, prevention of heavy floods and extreme floods. On mitigation,

the project’s integrated investments will promote lower carbon urban growth through increased densification of the

urban core, which will require less energy to manage over the long-term. The project will also promote non-motorized,

low-carbon transportation options by constructing pedestrian and bike lanes that will be developed alongside

investments in resilient roads, river improvements, and the regulation lake. The project is thus consistent with Vietnam’s

National Strategy on Climate Change by 2050 (approved on July 26, 2022) and Action Plan on Methane Emissions

Reduction by 2030 (approved on August 5, 2022), which outline the country’s pathway for a low carbon future. As such,

the project will support Vietnam’s NDC in which the country committed a conditional of 43.5 percent reduction of total

II. PROJECT DESCRIPTION



13.     The VPIUR reflects an integrated, spatial approach to investments in cities to strengthen their resilience while

improving services and livability. The project design is informed by lessons and experience of previous and ongoing

World Bank interventions in urban resilience and disaster risk management in Vietnam.11 The following five principles

guide project design: i) integrating investments across sectors, including flood protection, sanitation, transport, and

public spaces; ii) leveraging affordable new technologies for better risk management, with a focus on incorporating flood

risk into development planning, sharing information across administrative units, and engaging communities; iii)

integrating remedial and preventive measures to increase connectivity that guides future development into low-risk

areas, while improving the resilience of the urban core; iv) harmonizing nature-based solutions with gray infrastructure

to promote attractive public spaces; and v) enhancing infrastructure quality to withstand the impacts of disasters and

climate change.



14.     The project will build upon flood risk reduction and sanitation infrastructure investments supported by the

World Bank-financed Medium Cities Development Project (MCDP). The MCDP, implemented between 2012 and 2018,

aimed to enhance access to improved sanitation infrastructure by constructing wastewater pipelines, closing open

drainage channels, and constructing primary and secondary sewers and two wastewater pumping stations. 12 The VPIUR

will further expand the combined sewer and road infrastructure system to delay the pluvial runoff from the

tertiary/secondary system to the primary drainage system through improved drainage and wastewater collection.



15.    Despite public investments and the MCDP, the city lacks the capacity to cope with severe rainfall events,

resulting in large ponding at various locations throughout the city . This results from a combination of factors: i) elevated

water levels in the river system during such events; ii) the inadequate scale of the secondary sewer system along the

main roads, which limits rainwater discharging; iii) the insufficient capacity of the drainage pumping stations; iv)

infrequent maintenance and dredging of the primary and secondary canals of the drainage system, which restrict

drainage flows; and v) extensive sludge build-up in the Vinh-Ke Gai and Rao Dung River systems.

16.    The project aims to reduce flood risks and will support a resilience strategy of “delay, store, discharge.” The city

has catchment basins that are connected to the Rao Dung, Ke Gai, Vinh, and Lam rivers. To manage the water, a sewer

system of tertiary and secondary drains collects stormwater and wastewater and delivers it toward primary drains that

discharge, under gravity or through pump stations, directly into the rivers. A special feature of the drainage system is the

Hung Hoa 1 retention lake on the eastern side of the city, which temporarily stores water before draining it into the Rao

Dung River. Investments will be made to improve retention and storage capacity while increasing drainage capacity.



17.    A key feature of the project to improve the functionality of the drainage system is the creation of the Hung Hoa

2 regulation lake. The new regulation lake will store excess stormwater runoff in an expanded flood basin within the city.

Concurrently, investments will be made in the urban core to increase discharge capacity by augmenting the existing flood

protection system and drainage network. This strategy will address flood risks in a manner that is environment-friendly,

with dependence on nature-based solutions and without negative impacts on areas outside of the protected city core

(see project map in Annex 6). About 1,500 hectares of Vinh City’s urban core will benefit from increased protection

against floods, with a 10-year return period for rainfall and 100-year return period for the Lam/Ca River basin.



18.     The project will also invest in environmental remediation measures to reduce waste discharge into the Vinh

River while improving and protecting public spaces. Technical Assistance (TA) activities funded by a Global Facility for

Disaster Reduction and Recovery (GFDRR) grant during project preparation in 2019-20 supported development of a

spatial planning framework for resilient public spaces in Vinh City. In alignment with the project’s investments in hydraulic

capacity and green public spaces along the Vinh River, land use requirements were agreed with Vinh City and with the

provincial DOC and DONRE, based on the framework’s key principles. During implementation, the project will finance the

improvement and development of pedestrian and bicycle lanes in green public spaces, while supporting the adoption of

guidelines for residential development in the surrounding areas as stipulated in the current city Master Plan. Finally, the

project will support the city’s strategy to convert vacant land on both sides of the river, most of which is currently used

for informal/uncollected waste disposal, into green public spaces, while improving waste collection and separation to

reduce the quantity of plastic waste disposed into the rivers.

21.    This component will implement the “delay” and “discharge” parts of Vinh City’s resilience strategy by expanding

and improving the secondary and tertiary combined sewer system in all urban wards, while constructing transport links

with improved drainage and wastewater collection capacity. These investments will reduce flood-related risks, improve

environmental sanitation, improve the quality of surface water, and enhance connectivity. Investments will consist of

gray and green (or nature-based solutions) infrastructure. The following activities will be financed:



22.     Subcomponent 1.1—Integrated secondary and tertiary sewer improvement (IBRD: US$36.5 million; Counterpart

Funds: US$4.7 million). This subcomponent will improve Vinh City’s combined system of drainage and wastewater

collection services in the urban core and in selected fast-growing areas. These investments will better protect the urban

core against flooding. A total of 75 kilometers of sewers, including 30 kilometers of combined sewers and 45 kilometers

of sewerage sewers, will be upgraded or newly constructed. New combined sewers will be added to Le Mao extension

road, while the existing Dong Vinh channel will be upgraded in combination with the augmentation of the Tran Nguyen

Han road (see subcomponent 1.2). The new tertiary sewerage sewers will expand the existing wastewater collection

sewer system by approximately 10 kilometers.



23.    New and upgraded sewers will enhance the city’s existing stormwater drainage system, which currently consists

of approximately 20.3 kilometers of main channels and 194 kilometers of secondary and tertiary sewers. These

investments will improve the system’s stormwater conveyance capacity from 1,800,000 m 3/hour (cubic meters an hour)

to 6,500,000 m3/hour. Green infrastructure interventions will be prioritized to reduce surface runoff for the existing and

new drainage system. The stormwater conveyance system will also contribute to removing or rechanneling accumulated

sediment in existing water reservoirs, thus increasing the reservoir’s capacity. Although the existing drainage pumping

stations are assessed to have sufficient capacity, the associated facilities will be upgraded to improve their effectiveness,

including the relocation and upgrade of the Southwest pumping station.



24.     This subcomponent will also finance technical systems for better urban management. This includes the installation

of real-time monitoring equipment in the Hung Hoa Wastewater Treatment Plant (WWTP) to enable the collection of

real-time data on the WWTP influent and effluent across system structures. This also includes the installation of

equipment for an early-warning system that will be linked to an existing province-wide system managed by the DARD.

The investments in equipment will be complemented by activities to develop operating and decision-making procedures.

be the construction of the 1.2 kilometers Le Mao extension, including a bridge crossing the Vinh River, which will provide

a shorter route from the city center to National Highway 1. Sewers will be constructed along this road to improve its

flood protection and to transmit wastewater to the existing WWTP. The second subproject will be the upgrading of 0.55

kilometers of the Cao Xuan Huy road and replacement of an existing deteriorated “monkey bridge” by a motorized

permanent bridge, which will improve the drainage and wastewater services to this unserved area. The third subproject

will be the upgrading and extension of the Tran Nguyen Han road, totaling 1.95 kilometers, and improvement of the

associated degraded drain, the Dong Vinh channel, which will increase the discharge capacity of the channel to the Ke

Gai River, the upstream section of the Vinh River. This link will reduce local flood risks and increase the east-west

E. Rationale for World Bank Involvement and the Role of Partners



37.    Rationale for World Bank involvement. Although the economic benefits of the investments to be financed by the

VPIUR are expected to exceed their economic costs, the services that the project will enhance cannot be delivered by the

private sector profitably because of:







                                                                                                                 Page 16 of 62

       The World Bank

       Nghe An Province’s Vinh City Priority Infrastructure and Urban Resilience Development Project (P174157)







    (a) Missing markets. The market for a service is deemed “missing” if the service is non-rival and non-excludable. In

        Vietnam, markets are missing for road connectivity, drainage, and flood control systems. The private sector is

        unlikely to invest profitably in such services because it will not be able to prevent non-paying consumers from

        using the services.

    (b) Positive externalities. Wastewater collection can, in principle, be provided to individual customers. However, it

        is unlikely that customers will be willing to pay a price for these services that is sufficient to cover the full cost of

        construction and subsequent O&M, including a risk-adjusted profit margin. Therefore, the investments will not

        be financially profitable to private owners, even though they generate substantial benefits (positive externalities)

        to other parties. Furthermore, positive externalities from public investments in flood control systems are

        expected to be greater than if left completely to the private sector.



38.     Without some form of public intervention, the market is thus likely to provide a suboptimal level of urban

infrastructure services from a societal point of view. To address such market failures, government intervention is

proposed in the form of public investments in critical municipal infrastructure and sustained funding of O&M budgets at

adequate levels. The World Bank is well positioned to support the GOV to address these specific issues through the

provision of TA and financing in the design and implementation of the proposed project.



39.    Role of Partners. The World Bank coordinates extensively with development partners engaged in urban

development projects in Vietnam (for example, the Swiss State Secretariat for Economic Affairs, Asian Development Bank,

Japan International Cooperation Agency, Export-Import Bank of Korea, French development agency [Agence Française

de Développement], the German development bank [KfW], and the European Union) to advance national and local-level

dialogue on the importance of investment in climate-smart cities that are able to adapt to the impacts of climate change.

The project design was informed by the World Bank’s ongoing dialogue with the MOC and other prominent partners. To

increase the effectiveness of the capacity-building activities of Component 4, the World Bank will work closely with Vinh

City and relevant development partners to ensure synergies and to leverage additional development financing.



F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design



40.     The design of the VPIUR is informed by lessons from other urban operations in the country, such as the Da Nang

Sustainable Development Project (P123384), Vietnam Urban Upgrading Projects (P070197 and P159397), the Can Tho

Urban Development and Resilience Project (P152851), and the recently closed MCDP, which supported activities in Vinh

City as noted.13 The key lessons are:



    (a) The importance of securing adequate O&M budgets and sustaining political commitment . The experience of the

        MCDP in Vinh City has highlighted the importance of allocating enough financing to fund the O&M of project-

        financed infrastructure. Component 4 of the VPIUR will therefore support Nghe An Province and Vinh City in

        developing an integrated flood risk management system and operating procedures for the Hung Hoa WWTP,

        both integrating O&M plans and budget requirements for sustaining the long-term operation of the flood control,

        wastewater, and drainage systems (as part of the urban road system) and wastewater treatment investments

        financed by the project. The integration of these plans with the day-to-day operation of the City People’s

        Committee (CPC) and WWTP will help to ensure that O&M budgets are secured on an ongoing basis. Recognizing

    the critical need for resilient infrastructure investments in Vinh City, the VPIUR benefited from strong, high-level

    support from the central, provincial, and city governments from the earliest stages of project identification. To

    ensure continued political commitment, a high-level Project Steering Committee will be established and chaired

(b) The importance of timely and adequate allocation of counterpart funding for land acquisition and resettlement .

    The experience across the lending portfolio in Vietnam over the past several years is one of consistently long

    delays in project implementation because of lengthy and expensive land acquisition and resettlement processes.

    These delays typically stem from cost underestimations and delays in the central government’s provision of

    counterpart funding. This underscores the need for strong PPC commitment to work proactively with the central

    government to ensure timely and full allocation of counterpart funds to avoid construction delays. The full

    allocation of the required counterpart funds has been a consistent point of emphasis in the World Bank’s

    engagement with the PPC and CPC since project identification, and will continue throughout project

    implementation. As discussed in Section IV.D, the land acquisition costs and schedule included in the project’s

    Resettlement Plan (RP) were conservatively estimated to ensure that the PPC and CPC can prepare their financial

    mobilization plans for the required counterpart funds based on a realistic scale and timeline.

(c) The value of effective community participation and consultation throughout the planning, design, and

    implementation process to ensure sustainability. When developing community-level infrastructure, experience

    has shown that involving the community in the planning, design, and implementation processes can help improve

    the sustainability of infrastructure investments because, in that way, the investments tend to be tailored to suit

    local needs and priorities. Accordingly, the project will ensure community participation and consultation

    throughout project implementation and help instill a sense of ownership over the infrastructure within the

    community through extensive consultations that will be conducted at every stage of investment preparation,

    implementation, and operationalization. Further, the project will establish a grievance redress mechanism (GRM)

    to ensure that the concerns of the community are effectively addressed in a timely manner. The project Results

    Framework includes an indicator to monitor the share of grievances received that are addressed.

(d) The need for a strong empirical base for the identification and design of flood risk management investments .

    During the project identification stage, Vinh City assessed the limitations of its existing municipal flood

    management mechanism, which did not encompass the river basins in the broader area surrounding the city.

    Recognizing this shortcoming from past experiences and building on the lessons from the implementation of the

    MCDP, the city developed a comprehensive stormwater and wastewater hydraulic model that served as the basis

    for identifying and designing the flood control and environmental sanitation investments of this project. During

    project preparation, the hydraulic model was developed for the city’s drainage and wastewater collection system

    and was integrated with existing hydraulic models of the more expansive Lam/Ca River basin network. In addition

    to facilitating the planning and validation of the structural investments, the model will also be an important part

    of the development of the integrated flood risk management system under subcomponent 4.1, which will

    develop an early-warning system that will enable city officials to better predict and respond to future flood

    events, and to guide future development.

(e) The need for a comprehensive approach to resilient urban planning. Following lessons from earlier urban

    development projects in Vietnam that primarily focused on infrastructure investments but did not substantively

    address relevant planning and management issues in an integrated manner, the VPIUR will support a more

    comprehensive approach to urban and flood risk management and planning. As highlighted by a 2020 self-

    evaluation of Vinh City’s CPC, improper land use enforcement and unclear frameworks for spatial regulation have

    contributed to unconstrained development, leading to increased vulnerability of open spaces, agricultural land,

    and other natural resources to the impacts of disasters and climate change. Further, local government officials

        in Vinh City lack the tools and systems necessary to manage integrated urban development and to enforce spatial

        regulation in the current city Master Plan. As a result, Vinh City’s urban development pattern is characterized by

        low-density sprawl that has raised the costs of providing public services. While the objective of the structural

C. Sustainability



45.     The project investments were identified through a demand-driven approach and in close consultation with the

local government and project beneficiaries to enhance their sustainability potential. Moreover, considering the multi-

sectoral nature of these investments, and to ensure that all line departments continue to support the investments after

project closing, the PMU will coordinate with relevant line ministries and agencies to ensure that the investments i) are

fully consistent with other ongoing and/or upcoming sectoral programs and projects financed by both the GOV and other

development partners; and ii) follow relevant national sector guidelines and design criteria.



46.     The sustainability of the infrastructure will be enhanced by ensuring that technical designs are based on thorough

and comprehensive studies of past patterns and trends, as well as future projections of use relevant to the infrastructure

being developed. For example, the design of drainage interventions will be informed by the hydrologic-hydraulic

modeling and long-term projections for flooding and climate change vulnerability at the city level (including all drainage

basins). Drainage and wastewater facilities will be planned and implemented alongside the three urban roads to avoid

future costly retrofits and to improve the investments’ physical sustainability. Nature-based solutions will be applied to

investments in river embankment and construction of the regulation lake. Consultations with communities on the

investments’ design, as required by Vietnamese law, will further ensure that designs best meet local needs, which will

likely enhance their sense of ownership and maintenance of the infrastructure. Solid waste collection will also be

upgraded in the public spaces along the Vinh River and the surrounding Hung Hoa 2 regulation lake as part of a

comprehensive infrastructure service improvement package to enhance the environmental sustainability of these spaces.



47.    Ownership of the proposed infrastructure investments will be assumed by Vinh City following government

regulations. To ensure sustainability, the city will be required to establish adequate institutional arrangements and O&M

plans as part of the integrated flood risk management system and the operating procedures for the Hung Hoa WWTP.

Most of the project assets will be operated under the integrated flood risk management system that will be developed

under the project, which will enhance efficiency and sustainability. Relevant provincial departments, under the

leadership of the PPC, will also be involved at various stages of project implementation, including the development and

adoption of the O&M procedures for the integrated flood risk management system and the Hung Hoa WWTP. As

mechanisms that are embedded at local administrative levels (for example, one-stop shops at the ward and community

levels, inspectorates at the city level, and people’s courts). These mechanisms function well in dealing with issues such

safety impacts. However, they can be less effective in addressing concerns related to compensation for land acquisition

required by the project, or the allocation of resettlement plots. The project GRM will build on, and coordinate, these

mechanisms to ensure that all possible concerns are captured and addressed in a satisfactory and timely manner. The

project has developed a SEP to ensure that transparent and meaningful consultations are conducted with the affected

and interested parties throughout the project cycle. Details on the GRM and its implementation arrangements are

provided in the Detailed Environmental and Social Assessment and Management in Annex 5.

    (ESF) and on fiduciary requirements. In preparation for the implementation of the VPIUR, the World Bank provided TA to

    develop the hydraulic model and to support integrated infrastructure and land use planning. Nevertheless, the

    implementation of household connections to the tertiary sewer system, and the development of operating procedures

    for the WWTP and the integrated flood risk management system, are new areas that were not included in the MCDP. In

    addition, all capacity-building activities now follow a new financing arrangement in which the Bank’s loan will fund

    equipment, and the city’s budget will finance associated consulting services. The Bank will also mobilize additional

    technical specialists as necessary to support such complex assignments. The strong commitment of the city and provincial

    governments is also needed to ensure the effective operation and financial sustainability of the infrastructure systems

    that will be developed under the project, such as the pumping station and retention lake. Timely consultations with

    central government agencies will be carried out throughout the course of implementation to proactively ensure that

    capacities and budgets are in place to keep systems and infrastructure functioning as planned after the project closes. As

    a result, adequate funding allocation has already been included since April 2023 in the medium-term investment plans

    of the central government (for IBRD loan) and Nghe An province (with regard to counterpart budget), respectively.

    permanent land acquisition and resettlement, risks related to labor and working conditions, and temporary disruption of

    urban livelihoods because of construction activities. An ESMP, as part of the ESIA, was prepared that details the extensive

    E&S risk mitigation measures that ensure compliance with applicable provincial and national laws as well as World Bank

Economic Analysis



1.     Assumptions. Annual project costs and benefits were evaluated for a 25-year period of benefit, allowing

for a two- to five-year construction period starting in 2022. A discount rate of 9 percent was employed to convert

annual cost and benefit streams into present value, according to the 2016 World Bank guidelines. The unit prices

used for estimating project costs and benefits were based on prices in Vietnam in 2020. The evaluation was

conducted using the Vietnamese Dong (VND) as the unit of currency. An exchange rate of VND23,147:US$1 as of

January 2022 was adopted as consistent with both the market rate used in the Investment Policy Report (Pre-

Feasibility Study) approved by the Prime Minister and the rate used in the Feasibility Study approved by Nghe An

PPC in February 2023.



2.    Costs and benefits were calculated from economic prices rather than market or financial prices. Each

category of capital costs and O&M costs was disaggregated into subcategories such as traded material, non-traded

material, skilled labor, unskilled labor, transfer cost (taxes, subsidies, levies), and others. These financial costs

were converted from VND to US$ and were multiplied with their respective conversion factors to arrive at

economic prices. The economic costs were converted from financial costs using a conversion factor of 0.9 for

investment costs and 0.95 for O&M costs, which are based on international border prices.



3.    Project costs. The analysis took into account costs that occur over a project cycle, including capital

investments and O&M costs, which are converted into economic prices. Capital investments include construction

costs and other costs during preparation and implementation of construction, such as design, supervision

consulting, resettlement, and environmental management. Annual O&M costs were expressed as a percentage of

the economic investment cost. For urban transport infrastructure subprojects, the percentages were estimated

as 0.2 percent for construction maintenance, 1 percent for routine maintenance, 5 percent for periodic

maintenance, and 42 percent for major repairs. In addition to regular maintenance costs, the operating costs of

pumping stations and wastewater treatment plants are mainly the cost of electricity. Other costs, including

chemical costs, labor, management, and so on, are calculated as 30 percent of the electricity cost.



4.    Benefits from urban transport subprojects. The evaluation quantified two main conventional economic

benefits: savings in vehicle operating costs (VOC) and savings in vehicle operating time (VOT). Traffic demand was

estimated using the System for Traffic Demand Analysis, a package for transport forecasting that is widely used in

Vietnam. The system was used to calibrate current and projected passenger traffic and to assign traffic to each of

the project roads in “with project” and “without project” networks. VOC units were estimated using the World

Bank’s Highway Development and Management Model Four VOC model, with inputs collected in project areas for

gasoline, lubricant costs, and vehicle prices in 2020. Vehicle fleet characteristics were drawn from recent relevant

studies and similar projects, adjusted using survey data in project areas. VOC units were calculated based on

topographic characteristics, climate features, and road conditions. The estimate of VOT follows the standard

methodology employed in the other World Bank projects. VOT units were calculated for the city based on average

personal incomes, number of residents per household, number of income earners, and percentages of work and

business trips. To calculate VOT per vehicle, the average number of passengers for various categories of vehicles

was collected through surveys in project areas.

PPSD Summary



9.     Through the PMU, Vinh City’s CPC prepared an acceptable PPSD to inform fit-for-purpose procurement

arrangements in support of the PDO. Based on the PPSD findings, the PMU recommended a Procurement Plan

that was agreed with the World Bank. During the project period, the PPSD and Procurement Plan will be regularly

updated as appropriate. All procurement plans and their updates or modifications will be subject to the World

Bank’s prior review and no-objection in STEP. After the Bank’s clearance, the PMU will have the Procurement Plan

(and its updates) published on Vietnam’s national e-procurement system and the project owner’s website. They

will also be automatically posted on the World Bank’s external website. To deliver the best value for money and

to achieve the PDO, the PPSD recommends the following procurement approaches for key activities:



(a) Civil works. The civil works to be implemented under the VPIUR have a non-complex, technical nature and

    do not require any specialized technical knowhow. They are standard and common in Vietnam. Similar works

    have been successfully implemented in the country over the last two decades by national contractors. The

    national market has high capacity, with many potential bidders. Approaching the national market is

    appropriate because these packages are not likely to attract competition from abroad because of the long-

    term, labor-intensive nature of the works, the high capacity of the national market, the complicated

    coordination efforts required for urban infrastructure works, and the challenges of land acquisition. The

    experiences of urban sector projects in Vietnam have shown that, for international bidding operations, only

    national bidders typically participate. In the Da Nang Priority Infrastructure Project (P086508), the

    environmental improvement works for the Phu Loc River were packaged in a single contract, and since the

    contractor could not manage to complete the works within the original contract period, the contract was

    extended by 2.5 times its original scheduled length. If international bidders participate and win a contract,

    they will likely subcontract the majority of the works to local contractors, an arrangement that would

    complicate contract management and affect implementation timelines and construction quality. Therefore,

    international bidding does not demonstrate any clear advantages or added value over national bidding in this

    project. The single-stage, one-envelope Request for Bids (RFB) method, with an open and national market

    approach, would therefore be a reasonable choice.



(b) Goods. The project will procure real-time monitoring equipment for the existing Hung Hoa WWTP and

    facilities for transferring real-time data to the oversight agencies at both city and province levels. According

    to the PPSD, these types of equipment are off-the-shelf available in the Vietnamese market, which supports

    the PMU’s choice of a national, open, single-stage, one-envelope RFB method. In the past, similar equipment

    and institutional organization for equipment O&M were successfully implemented in Da Nang City, using the

    NCB method. The Vinh City PMU should consult the Da Nang PMU to learn from its experience. It is important

    to include appropriate training requirements in the contract to ensure smooth equipment O&M by the

    government beneficiary agencies.



(c) Consulting services. The PPSD has clarified that all consulting services (detailed design, construction

Environmental Safeguards Issues



1.       The ESIA finds that the environmental baseline of the project areas is characterized by low sensitivity with

varying degrees of modified habitats, ranging from complete surface sealing and the absence of vegetation to

highly modified patches of intra-urban habitats and agro-ecosystems. The site survey during the ESIA process

found that some sections of the proposed road alignment would run through existing agricultural or vacant land

in the city. Canal and riverbank upgrading and rehabilitation may disturb existing embankments where there may

be some patches of vegetation cover. The project’s excavation and dredging works may affect existing green

space, vegetation cover, and trees, birds, and aquatic life with low biodiversity value.



2.      The project is expected to use substantial resources and materials to construct urban roads, river

embankments, secondary and tertiary stormwater and wastewater collection systems, and a pumping station.

The ESIA identified and assessed in detail the risks and impacts related to the release of pollutants, waste

generation, the management of disposal materials and hazardous wastes, impacts on adjacent communities, and

resource use efficiency. The risks and impacts of generating non-hazardous as well as hazardous wastes were

addressed during the preparation of the ESIA, taking into account the standard measures in the WBG

Environment, Health, and Safety Guidelines (WBG ESHG) and in the WBG ESHG for Water and Sanitation.



3.      Dredging risks. Dredging activities in the Vinh River will have temporary adverse impacts on water quality

and aquatic species in the river itself, owing to sediment disturbance and increased turbidity and sedimentation.

The ESIA indicates that the dredging of the Vinh River will have minimal impact on water quality in the Lam River

due to increased turbidity and sedimentation. To mitigate the impacts on river water quality and aquatic species,

the contractors should prepare and implement a dredged material management plan. Dredging activities in the

river and canals must be carefully scheduled to avoid the rainy and spawning seasons. The dredged materials

should be reused for the leveling needs of other construction activities. Opportunities for upgrading and

expanding modified habitats and for “greening” infrastructure through ecologically friendly urban design and

landscape design were considered and included in the project’s technical design.



4.      Material supplier risks. The project will involve primary suppliers especially of stone, aggregates, and

sand. The ESIA indicates that all the construction materials will be obtained from licensed sources. During

implementation, a construction supervision consultant and the PMU will monitor and verify the contractor

sourcing materials to ensure that the supply is from licensed sources and to limit procurement to suppliers that

can demonstrate that they are not contributing to the significant degradation of rivers or other natural or critical

habitats.



5.     Cultural heritage risks. There is no intangible cultural heritage within the project area of influence, nor

any recognized national and international physical resources found in proximity to the project areas.

Approximately 407 individual burial graves will need to be relocated for project construction. The ESIA includes

appropriate measures to mitigate these impacts, including impacts due to such grave relocation. A chance finds

procedure is included in the ESMP for addressing unexpected physical resources found during construction.

Social Safeguards Issues



6.      Labor risks. The project workforce will include direct workers (directly employed by the PMU), contracted

workers, and primary supply workers. The project workforce, estimated to be approximately 657 people, consists

of 42 direct workers, 515 contracted workers, and 100 primary supply workers. The borrower will develop and,

where relevant, include in the bidding documents the requirements for i) a workers’ GRM, which will address all

workers’ complaints, and ii) sensitization related to the administration of the workers’ GRM and a code of conduct

to prevent and address potential incidents of harassment, child labor, forced labor, gender or SEA/SH issues, and

intimidation and/or exploitation during the implementation of activities financed under this project. The majority

of the estimated 515–520 workers during peak periods would be mobilized by the contractors and may include

workers from other localities. Potential risks related to labor and working conditions—such as labor influx, child

labor, forced labor, work-related discrimination and occupational health and safety (OHS) risks, sexual

exploitation, abuse, and harassment, contamination during infectious disease outbreaks, and limited access to

safe drinking water, power supply, and sanitation facilities—were assessed during the preparation of the project

and will be addressed in the implementation of the LMP. Key OHS risks relate to moving equipment, noise,

vibration, welding, chemical hazards, working environment temperatures, working at heights, and safety and

hygiene in worker camps (where established). The LMP, as part of the ESMP, provides mitigation measures to

address such risks and will be incorporated into procurement documents.



7.        Health and safety risks. The aspects of community health and safety that were taken into consideration

included waterway and roadway traffic safety in impacted areas, community health issues and safety risks at the

construction sites, hazardous substances generated during construction processes, and public safety, including

concerns about SEA/SH related to the influx of workers and security personnel. These risks and issues were

assessed during the ESIA process, and mitigation measures were developed and incorporated into the ESMP for

application during the detailed design, bidding, construction, and operational phases. The design of flood control

measures (which may include retention ponds) will incorporate design features to minimize the presence of

waterfowl and mosquitoes, which can function as disease vectors, as well as incorporate safety features, especially

for vulnerable populations such as children, older people, and people with disabilities. Regarding traffic safety,

the ESIA/ESMP identified, assessed, and will monitor risks related to traffic and traffic safety that might affect

local communities and travelers. Mitigation and risk management measures were incorporated into the design of

roads and the intersections, and the preparation of cost estimates. Measures to enhance traffic safety in relation

to the vehicles used by the project during the construction and operation phases were proposed. Other measures

to minimize safety risks for traffic, including drivers and local residents, were proposed for cases where the road

surface is occupied or disturbed by project activities. The ESIA/ESMP considered the incremental safety risks that

arise when members of the public are using footpaths, and the application of universal access in the design of the

footpaths along the new roads, including those within the RS. Regarding community health and safety risks related

to plant operation, although the use of highly toxic substances in the WWTP operation is not likely, the ESIA

identified and assessed the health risks of pollutants emitted from, and chemicals used for, wastewater treatment.

The ESMP includes recommendations for the size of a buffer zone, and an Emergency Response Plan will be

prepared as part of the WWTP’s operational manual. The ESIA assessed risks posed by security arrangements to

those within and outside the project sites, and the ESMP will include measures to avoid and mitigate the identified

risks in accordance with ESS4 (Community Health and Safety).

relates to the acquisition of land from 3,001 households, of which about 86 need to be relocated. The scope of

line with the World Bank’s ESS5 requirements on land acquisition and involuntary resettlement, an RP was

prepared that accounted for the socioeconomic vulnerability of the affected population to avoid the risk of

impoverishment, particularly among people who do not hold property title to the land they occupy. The RP will

ensure that the mitigation measures are adequate to restore their living conditions and include an analysis of

compensation options beyond cash compensation.



9.      Ethnic minorities. The ESIA demonstrates that the majority Kinh people are dominant (98 percent) in the

project area, with small numbers of Thai, Tho, and Mong people. The minority ethnic populations are composed

of individual households, residing in mixed neighborhoods with the Kinh majority and have no collective

attachment to the project area. ESS7 (Indigenous Peoples/Sub-Saharan African Historically Underserved

Traditional Local Communities) is therefore not relevant.



10.      Temporary project-driven labor influx. During the construction of the various infrastructure projects,

contractors may temporarily mobilize workers from outside the project areas. This may generate potential social

risks for communities living in the project area, such as violence among the local youth, gambling, drug

proliferation, and the risk of communicable diseases, particularly among local women. These impacts will be

mitigated through measures proposed in the ESMP and RP, such as the training of workers and construction

supervision teams on required lawful conduct in the host community and on Human Immunodeficiency

Virus/Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (HIV/AIDS) awareness, strict enforcement of drug abuse and

trafficking laws, and the prompt payment of adequate salaries to workers to reduce their incentives toward theft

or gambling. The PMU and independent E&S-monitoring agencies will be responsible for the implementation of

these mitigation measures. The measures will be contractually enforced through the inclusion of codes of conduct

in the bidding documents and the inclusion of the necessary requirements in the C-ESMP.

E&S implementation arrangements



13.     Vinh City PMU will be responsible for implementing this project. The PMU was provided with safeguards

training courses and has experience in managing E&S risks under previous World Bank safeguards policies.

However, the PMU lacks experience executing World Bank-financed projects under the new ESF. The PMU will be

responsible for implementing and monitoring compliance with the ESIA, RP, SEP, LMP, and ESCP through its

dedicated E&S focal points during project implementation. The PMU will also be responsible for incorporating the

ESMP into the standard tender documents for contractors to implement the E&S requirements during the

construction phase. The implementation of the ESMP will be supervised and monitored by a construction

supervision consultant and an independent E&S monitoring consultant. The E&S performance and compliance

with ESSs will also be subject to regular supervision by the World Bank. Capacity building on the implementation

of the ESF and ESSs, including training, is included in the ESMP for implementation. PMU staff, contractors,

supervision consultants, and local community representatives will receive training on the implementation of the

E&S instruments prepared for the project.



14.     A GRM focal point will be appointed within the PMU to coordinate the different administrative levels for

handling complaints in a timely and satisfactory manner. In addition to the already appointed staff for E&S risk

management, the PMU will mobilize other specialists (for example, community engagement specialists) as and

when required. The responsibility for land acquisition and resettlement lies principally with city government units,

such as the Land Fund Development Center. Given the various stakeholders involved in E&S implementation, the

World Bank expects a significant increase in the borrower’s demand for continuous support in E&S risk

management during implementation, as compared to the previous safeguards policies.
