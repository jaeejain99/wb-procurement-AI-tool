    I. PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES1





      A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL





      Context



      1.       Country context. At appraisal, Viet Nam had gained remarkable achievements in social-economic

      development and poverty reduction during the previous decade. The country sustained high economic growth

      between 2005 and 2015, with its Gross Domestic Product (GDP) expanding at an average annual rate of about 6

      percent. By 2016, Viet Nam's GDP reached US$205.3 billion, and GDP per capita climbed to US$2,202. As per the

      government’s standard for poverty (period 2011-2015) 2, the average rate of households living below the poverty line

      decreased from 14.2 percent in 2010 to 5.8 percent in 2016. 3 Viet Nam joined the World Trade Organization (WTO) in

      2007, and trade and foreign direct investment (FDI) increased significantly during the past ten years. These

      achievements positioned the country for graduation from International Development Association (IDA) eligibility after

      fiscal year 2017 ended by June 30, 20174. Despite these achievements, Viet Nam still faced various challenges, including

      welfare disparities among ethnic minorities, slow productivity growth, and environmental vulnerabilities. To sustain

      its development, the country needed to implement comprehensive reforms focused on economic governance,

      infrastructure development (especially energy, transport and urban infrastructure), agricultural modernization, public

      service improvement, and climate change resilience5.



      2.       Sectoral and institutional context. The transport sector of Viet Nam comprises the full range of transport

      modes and its sub-sectors, including road, railway, inland waterway, maritime, and air transport. The Ministry of

      Transport (MOT) was assigned as a line governmental agency to perform the functionality of the state management

      of all transport sub-sectors nationwide, as well as the role of state management for sectoral public services as

      stipulated by law 6 . Under MOT, different functional departments, specified sub-sectoral authorities, project

      management units (PMUs), and other related agencies were institutionalized. Among these, the PMUs were assigned

      as focal agencies to manage the preparation and implementation of MOT’s projects. Transport infrastructure received

      noticeable investment from the 1990s, when the country began receiving large amounts of Official Development

      Assistance (ODA) financing from the World Bank (WB), the Asian Development Bank (ADB), the Japan International

      Cooperation Agency (JICA), and other donors. The government considered investment in transport infrastructure to

      be one of the key factors and driving forces for Viet Nam’s socio-economic development trajectory. On average, about

      23 percent of the total state investment budget was annually allocated to the transport sector during the period 2006-

      20147. As reported by MOT, from 2009 to 2011, the annual investment in the transport sector was recorded at 3.1

      percent of GDP. Out of this total budget, 91.9 percent was invested in road transport, with the remaining 8.1 percent

      allocated to rail, inland waterway, maritime, and air transport. This level of investment was considered significant

3.       Project context. The National Highway 19 (NH19),10 with a total length of 243 km, was a crucial East-West

corridor for transport connections between Central Highland provinces, Central Coastal provinces, Quy Nhon Seaport,

and Le Thanh (in Viet Nam)/ O'Yadav (in Cambodia) international border gate. At appraisal, the traffic characteristics

on NH19 were highly mixed, including a large number of heavy trucks, high speed vehicles, motorcycles, and

considerable non-motorized traffic and local pedestrians. However, most of its sections still suffered from poor

connectivity, inadequate safety, and insufficient capacity to handle the increasing traffic demand. They were also

vulnerable to climate impacts, such as flooding and instability under extreme weather events. Specifically, there were

still many narrow sections with their carriageway ranging from 6 to 7 meters wide with degraded road pavements,

unfavorable geometric elements (such as small curves, high gradients, short sight distances, etc.) and insufficient road

safety facilities, especially the road section at An Khe mountain pass, where heavy trucks were able to travel at only

walking speeds and traffic congestions occurred more frequently due to landslides during rainy seasons. During the

implementation of the World Bank-financed Viet Nam Road Safety Project (P085080) in 2012, the International Road

Assessment Programme (iRAP) 11 consultant assessed most of NH19 sections ranked as Star 1 and 2 ratings in safety

standards, which revealed that NH19 was one of the critically unsafe NHs. As needed, the project was requested by

the Government for the World Bank financing in rehabilitating/upgrading the three key road sections of NH19 to

improve its connectivity, safety and climate-resilience, including: Section 1: Binh Lien - Dak Po (Km50-Km90); Section

2: Chau Khe-Pleiku (Km131-Km167); and Section 3: Ham Rong - Le Thanh Border Gate (Km180-Km243).



4.      Rationale for Bank involvement. The rationale for the Bank's involvement was primarily rooted in the project's

direct contribution to the poverty reduction and economic growth objectives outlined in the Viet Nam Systematic

Country Diagnostic (SCD). It was also fully aligned with the Government’s development strategies, notably the Socio-

Economic Development Plan (SEDP) and the Socio-Economic Development Strategy (SEDS). The project aimed to

improve connectivity, road safety, and climate resilience for NH19, which was expected to enhance access to jobs,

markets, and social services for local people and communities. This, in turn, would reduce transport costs and promote

socioeconomic development for the region. This project held particular importance for the Central Highlands region,

an area with a high poverty rate and a large number of ethnic minority groups. Consequently, it was closely aligned

with the World Bank Group’s mission of ending extreme poverty and boosting shared prosperity on a livable planet.

     Theory of Change (Results Chain)



     5.       At appraisal, the Project Appraisal Document (PAD, Report No: PAD2239) did not include a Theory of Change

     (ToC). For assessing project outcomes at closing, a retrospective ToC was developed in the Implementation Completion

     and Results Report (ICR) based on the Project Development Objectives (PDOs), Project Description, and Results

     Framework provided in the PAD. The developed ToC is given in Figure 1, which presents the causal relationships

     between the activities, outputs, outcomes, PDO, and impacts of the project. Given that all key assumptions are met,

     the project leads to its outcomes of reduced travel time, increased traffic demand, reduced risks of road crashes, and

     improved stability for roads and structures to contribute to its PDOs. Consequently, a number of positive impacts will

     be generated in terms of benefiting people and communities with improved transport services (safer, faster, more

     reliable and efficient services), reducing transport and logistics costs, promoting regional and trans-regional economic

     development for the project areas and the Central Highlands region, enhancing access to jobs and social services, and

     boosting shared prosperity and poverty reduction. Additionally, an increased opportunity for safer and climate-

     resilient transport infrastructures to be built in the future is also an impact of the project as well.

     Project Development Objectives (PDOs)



     6.     The PDO was to improve the connectivity, safety and climate-resilience of the National Highway 19. There

     were no changes to the PDO during implementation.



     Key Expected Outcomes and Outcome Indicators



     7.       The four PDO indicators12 of the project at appraisal were:



          1) Annual average daily traffic (AADT)

          2) Average travel time on the NH19 Corridor

          3) Number of kilometers of International Road Assessment Program (iRAP) 3-star rating or above for both cars

             and motorcycles on the NH19 Corridor

          4) Kilometers of roads that are upgraded in compliance with climate/disaster resilient design standards



     Components



     8.       The project was designed to achieve the PDO through the following two components.



     9.       Component 1: Road Improvements – US$ 145 million13 (US$ 141.3 million IDA Financing). This component

     consists of: (i) Supporting physical improvements of selected sections of the NH19, through, inter alia, pavement

     rehabilitation, road/lane widening, improvement of intersections and bridges, construction of bypasses,

     reinforcement of road slopes, and other road safety features; and (ii) Supporting resettlement activities through

     assistance in financing compensation and assistance to displaced person(s).



     10.     Component 2: Implementation Support - US$ 8.7 million14 (US$ 8.7 million IDA Financing). This component is

     to provide technical support for project implementation, including overall detailed engineering design, construction

     supervision, financial audits, environmental and social management, and monitoring and evaluation.



     11.     During the implementation, technical assistance (TA) to enhance implementation support was provided

     alongside the two project’s components through the following Bank-executed activities:



           i. Bank- executed activities related to road safety supported by the Global Road Safety Facility (GRSF) to conduct

              a Road Safety Audit on NH19, to assess impacts of motorcycle lanes in Viet Nam for updating the draft manual

              for motorcycle lane design and specifications and to strengthen the institutional capacity of the implementing

              agency for managing road safety and physical works; and

          ii. Bank-executed activities related to climate resilience supported by the Global Facility for Disaster Reduction

              and Recovery (GFDRR) to conduct a landslide risk assessment of the project area to inform the identification

              of suitable climate resilience measures and design options for NH19 and provide cross-over recommendations

              on the current technical standards for their improvements in road resilient design.

     C. EFFICIENCY





     Assessment of Efficiency and Rating



     34.      Economic analysis. The ex-post economic analysis showed that the project achieved an Economic Internal

     Rate of Return (EIRR) of 17.71 percent at completion, compared to 15.72 percent at appraisal. The higher EIRR at

     completion indicates that the project had a more efficient generation of benefits compared to the costs incurred

     during its overall lifecycle. In addition, the analysis showed that the project gained a Net Present Value (NPV) of

     US$64,388,683 at completion, compared to US$64,773,916 at appraisal, using a 10 percent discount rate and updated

     data. The main reason for the updated NPV at completion being slightly lower than the original one at appraisal is

     due to prolonged delays in project implementation. Nevertheless, the economic analysis demonstrated the sound

     economic viability and robustness of the project at completion. In particular, the increased EIRR indicates that the

     project will continue to generate high economic benefits, even exceeding its initial estimations. The sensitivity analysis

     also confirmed the project’s robustness by showing it will still provide a positive EIRR even with a 20 percent increase

     in O&M costs and a 20 percent reduction in benefits (see more details in Annex 4).



     35.        Implementation efficiency. The project was found to be implemented efficiently. The total cost of the

     project was reduced from US$153.70 million estimated at appraisal to US$123.14 million at completion. The saving

     was a result of procurement savings, favorable changes in the exchange rate, and unused contingencies. The project

     substantially achieved its PDO with an 18-month extension to the original closing date, which was mainly due to delays

     in budget allocation, difficulties in land acquisition and resettlement (LAR), and unexpected events such as the COVID-

     19 pandemic and adverse weather conditions. During the COVID-19 pandemic, travel restrictions and lockdowns

     critically delayed and complicated the implementation of survey works, detailed design, procurement, LAR, and

     construction. Despite these considerable challenges, the project was successfully completed with an 18-month

     extension.



     36.     Based on the above analyses, the efficiency is rated as Substantial. This rating reflected the project's efficient

     performance despite its initial delay stemming from an approximately two-year delay in budget allocation, as well as

     subsequent delays in LAR and construction, which all restricted the achievement of a higher NPV at completion.

   transportation and logistics costs. As a result, the project contributed in the medium to long term to agricultural

   were subsequently transferred to PMU2 and relevant stakeholders for adoption in other upcoming projects.

   A. KEY FACTORS DURING PREPARATION



   41.     The Project Development Objectives (PDOs) were set clearly and realistically focused on improving the

   connectivity, safety, and climate-resilience of NH19, directly addressing the urgent needs identified during appraisal.

   The project components were also clearly designed and structured to achieve these PDOs.



   42.      The project's design was grounded in the World Bank's global experience and best practices, integrating

   key lessons on connectivity, safety, and climate resilience for NH19. This included applying international road safety

   standards, climate-resilient designs, and an optimized alignment for the bypasses. Comprehensive alternative

   analyses were conducted to select the best technical, economic, environmental, and social options, while clear

   stakeholder engagement and institutional arrangements were defined during the preparation phase.



   43.       Risk identification and mitigation measures. At appraisal, the overall risk to the PDO was rated as

   ‘Moderate’. The key risks stem from fiduciary, technical, environmental and social, and institutional aspects. Overall,

   the initial risk ratings and proposed mitigation measures were adequate, except for the environmental and social risk.

   This particular risk was initially underestimated due to the increasing complexity of land acquisition and resettlement

        surveys and public consultations as well as finalizing the detailed designs and safeguards documents. LAR and civil

        work activities were critically affected by the pandemic. This was particularly evident during suspensions of those

        activities from March to June 2020 and from May to July 2021 due to pandemic lockdown regulations in the affected

        provinces. Consequently, LAR progress suffered significant disruptions, only resuming at the beginning of 2022 and

        facing substantial delays as compensation payments to affected households could not commence by 2021. For civil

        work construction, the pandemic caused the most significant delays for packages CW3 and CW4A (which started in

        June 2021) to commence civil works construction, primarily due to disruptions in mobilizing workers and equipment

        to construction sites.



        48.      Rising prices and scarcity of construction materials. There was a sharp rise in prices of construction materials

        during project implementation, particularly in 2022. According to statistics, the prices of bitumen, sand, cement, and

        stone increased by about 23%, 18%, 13% and 7% respectively in 2022, compared to 2021 30 . Consequently, the

        contractors tended to slow down construction activities while waiting for the price stabilization to reduce their

        economic loss, leading to delays of project implementation during 2021 and 2022. Moreover, there was a scarcity in

        qualified construction stones for pavements and filling soils for embankments, particularly for civil work packages

        CW1, CW2 and CW3 due to legal restrictions on licensing or extending construction material quarries in the related

        provinces between 2023 and 2024. The shortage in construction materials disrupted construction activities during

        that period and led to further delays for the completion of those civil work packages.



        49.      Unfavorable weather conditions during implementation. Rainy seasons in the Central Highlands region

        normally occur for 6 months annually, from May to the end of October within a year. However, during the year 2022,

        the rainy season prolonged abnormally up to 7 months with high rainfall intensity, causing more disruptions for civil

        work construction activities, especially after the long-restricted periods due to the COVID-19 pandemic. These

        challenging weather conditions hampered the continuity of road construction, particularly earthworks, pavement

        foundation works, and asphalt paving, all of which could not be conducted under intense rainfall conditions.

Environmental Assessment (OP/BP 4.01); (b) Natural Habitats (OP/BP 4.04); and (c) Physical Cultural Resources

(OP/BP 4.11). The project was classified as Category B due to the potential moderate to substantial adverse

environmental and social impacts associated with the physical investments under Component 1. These policies were

V. LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS



   73.       Strong commitment and engagement from relevant stakeholders are a key factor for the successful

   implementation and completion of a project. Given the project's broad scope and extensive stakeholder

   involvement, the robust commitment and collaboration from all relevant stakeholders such as central governmental

   authorities, the Ministry of Transport (MOT), the World Bank, the Australian Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade

   (DFAT), ministry-level agencies, the project implementing agency, LAR implementing agencies, local authorities,

   contractors, and affected/beneficiary communities were essential. This collective effort helped to address a series of

   significant challenges faced during implementation, including delayed budget allocation, impacts from the COVID-19

   pandemic, difficulties in LAR, scarcity of construction materials, obstacles in securing project-related permissions,

   adverse weather conditions, delayed issues in several contracts, and other emerging issues. A notable instance of the

   strong commitment and engagement among key stakeholders within this project was the mobilization of the DFAT

   grant for detailed design and documentation (DDD) in early 2019, which occurred during a period of delayed budget

   allocation until the end of 2020. The collaborative partnership between DFAT and the World Bank, aimed at providing

   better support to the counterparts, was clearly demonstrated within this project. The successful implementation of

   the DDD ultimately reduced project delays by over 1.5 years. This underscores that with stronger commitment,

   engagement, and coordination, similar challenges can be resolved more quickly and efficiently, leading to more

   successful project implementation and completion.



   74.       Leveraging global knowledge, experience, and best practices are important to improve the achievement

   of Project Development Objectives. The project adopted and implemented numerous road safety and climate-

   resilient interventions, many of which were recommended by the technical assistance (TA) activities. These included:

   applying internationally recognized standards for road safety assessments and audits (such as iRAP), deploying

   dedicated motorcycle lanes, arranging bus stops at convenient locations, enhancing school zone safety, utilizing the

   Observational Method (OM) for slope design and stabilization, and retrofitting roads, bridges, and their structures

   with climate-resilient features. These interventions directly contributed to achieving the Project Development

   Objectives (PDOs). The project achieved major impacts by effectively integrating global good practices into road

   rehabilitation of Viet Nam. This was enabled because the TA activities were fully blended with the physical

   construction activities of the project. This integrated approach created a lasting impact that a standalone TA activity

   could hardly achieve on its own.

75.       A streamlined project design with few, well-structured components, focused technical assistance (TA)

activities on its core development objectives, and adaptive management helped improve implementation

efficiency and effectively leverage global best practices in the client country. The streamlined design of this project,

with its two well-structured components and supported TA activities (focused on road safety and climate-resilience)

as milestone-driven activities linked to PDO-level outcomes, was critical for its successful completion despite

numerous unforeseen challenges during implementation. This strategic approach simplified logistics and supervision

across a challenging landscape of mountainous terrain and remote areas of the Central Highlands region. Instead of

managing a multitude of small, fragmented contracts for individual road sections or structures, this project focused

resources on a few larger civil works packages, for instance, packages CW1, CW2, and CW4B. This helped reduce not

only administrative overhead but also made the project more resilient to disruptions. During the COVID-19 pandemic,

fewer, well-defined components and larger civil works packages simplified the supply chain for key materials like

asphalt, stone, filling soil, cement, and steel. Furthermore, it allowed for easier implementation of safety protocols,

such as social distancing, and on-site health checks. With more adaptive management, the project would maintain its

implementation momentum better during external shocks, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, through flexible contract

management, budget reallocation, and restructuring without compromising quality. This adaptive capacity is

instructive for fragility, conflict, and violence (FCV), post-disaster, or pandemic-prone contexts.



76.       Land Acquisition and Resettlement (LAR) has critical impacts on implementation progress of a road project.

Given the rising complexity and difficulties of LAR in the country, it's crucial to thoroughly scrutinize LAR risks,

mitigation measures, and projected progress during project preparation and design. This will help minimize potentially

substantial project delays. Furthermore, more advanced design options and budgeting contingencies related to LAR

should be carefully considered upfront. This proactive approach aims to use the best design solutions for minimizing

the land acquired for the project and its individual structures, while also allowing for better adaptation to changes or

new requirements that emerge during implementation. To enhance LAR effectiveness, coordination must be

strengthened among central governmental agencies, local authorities, the project owner, implementing agencies, and

affected communities. To achieve this, a dedicated Steering Committee for LAR should be established within the

project. This committee should be co-chaired by high-level leaders from the line ministry (e.g., a Vice Minister of MOT)

and from the relevant provinces (e.g., two Vice Chairmen from Binh Dinh and Gia Lai provinces). Its members should

include management from the PMU, relevant provincial departments, LAR implementing agencies, and

district/commune authorities. Additionally, an intermediate results indicator like "Number of kilometers of Right of

Way secured for the project" should be added to the Results Framework. This indicator will allow all relevant

stakeholders to closely monitor the total length of roads available for construction and take timely remedial actions

when needed to improve LAR implementation progress.



77.      Detailed work schedules and rigorous oversight of contractors’ performance are essential to ensure

adequate project’s overall performance. Road construction in the Central Highlands faced significant challenges due

to adverse weather conditions, with the rainy season often lasting six months annually. The project also encountered

numerous unexpected events and difficulties during its implementation. To enhance overall performance, it's crucial

to develop detailed, advanced implementation schedules that include time contingencies, particularly for earthwork

and pavement construction, given their high susceptibility to weather conditions. All construction and consultant

contractors must decisively mobilize in a sufficient and timely manner their technical, financial, and human resources

to meet committed schedules and contract provisions. Furthermore, the PMU needs to closely manage and frequently

monitor contractors' performance according to their signed contracts, taking prompt remedial actions when

necessary. This approach ensures the effective performance of individual contractors, which, in turn, significantly

improves the project’s overall performance.

ANNEX 4. EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS





Introduction

1.      This annex provides an ex-post economic analysis of the project to justify its efficiency. An updated economic

analysis was carried out at the time of project completion, using the same cost-benefit analysis methodology as applied

during the project appraisal stage (PAD). The analysis was conducted to reassess the economic returns of the project based

on actual implementation data. At the time of appraisal, three bypass options were analyzed. At project closure, the

analysis focused on the completed activities, which included: (i) construction of 13.7 km of An Khe Town bypass; (ii)

construction of 13.2 km Pleiku City bypass; and (iii) improvement of totaling approximately 117 km under the three road

sections of NH19.

2.      The evaluation applied a standard discounted cash flow approach, computing annual costs and benefits over the

project life cycle until 2045. All economic values were converted to constant 2025 US dollars, using updated unit prices

based on Vietnamese market data. The analysis incorporated quantified benefits such as vehicle operating cost (VOC)

savings, travel time reductions, accident cost savings, and potential CO₂ emission reductions. These were calculated using

internationally recognized tools and parameters adapted to Viet Nam’s context.

Updated economic costs

3.      The total investment cost of the project was updated based on actual disbursement data covering the period from

2018 to the time of evaluation. These figures accurately reflect the implementation progress across all project components

and provide a reliable basis for adjusting and finalizing the cost-benefit analysis, thereby ensuring that the project's

economic performance is properly assessed at completion. The analysis applied economic costs, excluding taxes, price

contingencies, and financial transfers, in accordance with standard economic evaluation practices.

Assessment of Economic Impacts on BOT Sections

15.     Given the proximity between the project route and two existing BOT sections on NH19, the completion stage

analysis revisited how these segments might interact in practice. Specifically, the analysis focused on two main aspects: (i)

the extent to which the project could contribute to increased toll revenues for BOT operators because of additional traffic

generated after completion; and (ii) the degree to which the existing BOT sections may affect traffic volumes and

operational performance along the project road. This assessment followed a similar approach to that used at the appraisal

stage (PAD), ensuring consistency in evaluating the economic interactions between the public and private investments

along the corridor.

16.      On the one hand, improved road conditions under the project could result in modest gains in toll revenue for the

BOT operators, due to increased traffic volumes. Updated traffic counts from 2024 and 2025 by PMU2 suggest that this

impact will be limited. The projected revenue increase reaches a peak of around 7.48% in 2025, gradually tapering off to

about 4.31% by 2040, indicating that induced demand effects are relatively contained. On the other hand, tolling may act

as a cost deterrent for certain users, particularly local traffic making short trips. In such cases, drivers might choose nearby

free roads rather than continue through tolled sections. However, this potential diversion is unlikely to affect the project's

broader traffic flows, as most vehicles are engaged in longer distance travel. For these users, the improved travel time and

road quality outweigh the toll cost, and their route choices are expected to remain largely unchanged. Overall, the

interaction between the project and the existing BOT sections is acknowledged but considered limited in scale. These

effects do not alter the fundamental rationale or diminish the overall economic efficiency of the project at completion.

The GHG evaluation results

17.     At project completion, total carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions over the entire evaluation period were estimated at

approximately 4.376 million tons with the project, and 4.284 million tons without the project. This results in a net increase

of about 0.92 million tons of CO₂, equivalent to a 2.1 percent rise attributable to the project. The emission estimates at

completion reflect a substantial departure from those prepared during the appraisal phase. While total CO₂ emissions with

the project declined from 4.954 million tons to 4.376 million tons, the reduction in CO₂ emissions without the project

scenario was even more pronounced, dropping from 4.936 million tons to 4.284 million tons. As a result, the net increase

in emissions rose to 2.1%, compared to just 0.4% projected at appraisal. This marginal increase in CO₂ emissions with the

project has negligible impact on the economic justification of the project.

18.      Compared to the appraisal stage (PAD), the total CO₂ emissions estimated at project completion were significantly

lower. This difference is primarily due to the incorporation of updated input data reflecting actual conditions, including

observed traffic volumes, vehicle composition on the corridor, and actual travel distances following the project's

implementation. In addition, technical parameters in the emissions model were adjusted to better reflect post completion

operating conditions. As a result, the updated analysis provides a more accurate representation of the project's actual

emissions, rather than relying on the more conservative assumptions used at the time of appraisal. Overall, the updated

GHG assessment at completion provides a more grounded and evidence-based understanding of the project’s

environmental performance. While the project results in a modest increase in emissions relative to the without-project

case, this trade-off is considered acceptable considering the broader transport efficiency gains, improved connectivity, and

alignment with long-term infrastructure development objectives. The analysis also highlights the importance of

continuously updating technical assumptions with real-world data to ensure accurate and credible evaluation of

environmental impacts over the project lifecycle.
