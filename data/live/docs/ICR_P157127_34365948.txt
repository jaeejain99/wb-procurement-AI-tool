     I.     PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES



     A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL



 Context



 1.       At appraisal, the Socialist Republic of Viet Nam had achieved substantial poverty reduction and

 growth over the previous 30 years, but environmental stresses, including climate change, threaten the

 sustainability of its growth. Historic growth was reached at the cost of significant depletion of the

 country’s natural resources, compromising its ecosystem services. Viet Nam is also one of the most

 hazard-prone countries in the region, with sea level rise and extreme weather events posing severe

 threats. These threats predominantly affect poor rural households, the majority of whose income comes

 from climate-sensitive activities, such as crop cultivation, livestock, forestry, and fishing. Viet Nam’s

 exposure to climate change is intensified by the high proportion of the country’s population and economic

 assets located in coastal lowlands and deltas.



 2.       The Government of Viet Nam (GoV) had invested in physical structures to increase the resilience

 of coastal areas, but coastal communes also needed enhanced support to adapt to projected increases

 in sea level rise, climate variability, and severe storms. Approximately 1,400 km of dikes were built in

 northern and central Viet Nam to protect 630,000 ha of agricultural land and about 8.7 million people,

 but their effectiveness declined over the years. Inaction would have severely affected several sectors,

 from energy to agriculture, as well as the livelihoods and climate vulnerability of poor households.



 3.      Afforestation and reforestation of coastal forests, such as mangroves, had been shown to offer

 sustainable alternatives to physical structures to improve coastal resilience and livelihoods, but Viet

 Nam’s coastal forests were severely degraded. Mangrove forests had decreased by nearly one-third,

 from 408,500 ha in 1943 to 270,000 ha in 2015, mainly from unsustainable land use conversions, including

 the development of infrastructure and industrial-scale aquaculture, harvest for fuelwood, encroachment

 from agriculture, and damage from severe weather events.



 4.      The GoV had taken several measures to improve coastal planning, but implementation was not

 effective. An Integrated Coastal Zone Management (ICZM) Program was adopted under several

 government decisions between 2007 and 20161 and supported by a National Coastal Zone Action Plan

 (2016–2020) to help implement ICZM priorities. However, the program’s implementation was limited

 because of lack of data on coastal forest plans and monitoring, weak provincial capacity to develop well-

 integrated plans, and insufficient technical capacity to apply recommendations, including for effective

 restoration of coastal forests. In addition, local stakeholders2 (the local government, communities, and

 private stakeholders) often lacked incentives to protect and restore coastal forests as long-term public

 goods as they often gained short-term tangible benefits from converting coastal forest areas for

 cultivation or infrastructure such as, ports or fishing piers, or from illegal exploitation. Therefore, Viet Nam

 had few examples of successful mangrove and sandy soil forest restoration, mostly at small scales.3



 5.       Against this background, the Government recognized the need for an integrated approach to

 coastal forest restoration efforts that considered institutional, biophysical/environmental, economic,

 and social realities in the long run. The World Bank has provided long-standing assistance to Viet Nam’s

 forestry sector since 2004,4 so it was approached to assist the GoV through an investment project with

 three central elements: (a) establishing modern systems for improving planning and providing quality

 inputs for and financing restoration of coastal forests; (b) investing in more effective management

 arrangements and in silvicultural practices and structures needed to extend and manage coastal forests

 and augment their survival; and (c) diversifying the long-term benefits from protecting and maintaining

 coastal forests.



 6.       Increasing investments in a sustainable, more holistic approach to coastal forest plantation and

 reforestation was aligned with the Government’s National Socio-Economic Development Plan (2016),

 Targeted Program for Sustainable Forest Development (TP-SFD, 2017), Green Growth Strategy (2012), and

 Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC, 2016), as well as the World Bank’s Viet Nam Country

 Partnership Framework (CPF) FY18–FY22.5 Coastal forest protection, plantation, and restoration were

 identified as a priority through 2030 in Viet Nam’s first NDC. In October 2016, the GoV approved the

 Implementation Plan of the Paris Agreement, which laid out specific actions to achieve its NDC targets.

 The Government-issued Resolution No. 73/NQ-CP dated August 2016 approved the investment policy of

 targeted programs in the period 2016–20, including the TP-SFD, which had the objective of contributing

 to disaster risk reduction, ecological environmental protection, effective response to climate change and

 sea level rise, and improvement of livelihoods for people working in forestry. The project was also aligned

 with the priorities of the CPF, specifically with Focus Area 3, Ensure Environmental Sustainability and

 Resilience, and its objective ‘Increase Climate Resilience and Strengthen Disaster Risk Management’,

 including to increase household-level resilience to disasters. The project was designed to implement the

 CPF objective to “support regional planning and decision-making and climate-resilient livelihoods and

 Components



 11.      The project components were as follows:



 12.      Component 1: Enabling Effective Coastal Forest Management (original allocation: GoV US$3

 million; actual cost: GoV US$0.73 million). This component provided technical capacity building to the

 Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development (MARD) to coordinate spatial plans at different levels and

 financed the purchase and dissemination of replicable technologies to map and visualize coastal forest

 plans. It also planned to modernize the technology and structures required to produce and certify quality

 seedlings and propagate trees and supported the transfer of skills to manage nurseries and the links

 between smallholder nurseries and buyers. The component also supported the valuation of regulatory

 ecosystem services from coastal forests and the operationalization of Payments for Ecosystem Services

 (PFES) for aquaculture, spawning, and carbon sequestration.



 13.      Component 2: Coastal Forest Development and Rehabilitation (original allocation: IDA

 US$104.86 million, GoV US$7.22 million; actual cost: IDA US$22.80 million, GoV US$2.30 million). This

 component financed afforestation and coastal protection forest restoration activities, including detailed

 site assessments and designs for planting, purchase of seedlings and supervision of planting, and training

 on community-based forest management. Physical supporting structures were also to be established and

 officials and communities on management, protection, and development of coastal forests.

 Revised Components

 number of provinces, and coastal cliff forests were added as eligible for project financing. The IDA cost of

 Component 3 was reduced to US$24.42 million, and the IDA cost of Component 4 was reduced to US$3.22

 million.

   C. EFFICIENCY



 Assessment of Efficiency and Rating

 Rating: Modest



 Economic Analysis



 56.      The primary measurable project benefits stem from activities financed under Components 2

 and 3. These components financed investments in the protection and rehabilitation of coastal forest

 systems and the strengthening of coastal economies and livelihoods. The economic analysis at closing

 considered scenarios with and without the project, applying key assumptions, such as a 6 percent discount

 rate, a 31-year investment lifespan, a social value of carbon at US$40 per tCO2e, an 80 percent success

 rate for coastal terrestrial and mangrove forest conservation, and a 30 percent success rate for livelihood

 investments. The analysis also incorporated potential CO2 emission reductions using a World Bank-

 recommended model.



 57.      The analysis shows that the project’s financial and economic viability depends on the success

 of plantation, forest restoration, and protection efforts. The project generates positive net benefits if

 the social values of carbon are included but not if they are excluded. The project’s economic net present

 value (ENPV) is estimated to be US$159.4 million if the social value of carbon is included and −US$20.7

 million otherwise. The corresponding estimates of economic internal rate of return (EIRR) are 18.2 percent

 with carbon and 3.6 percent without. These estimates are significantly lower than the ENPV estimated at

 appraisal (US$905.8 million) because forest plantation, forest restoration, and forest protection were

 implemented more slowly and on smaller areas than planned at appraisal and the analysis at appraisal

 had erroneously omitted important costs, including the opportunity costs of land use alternatives and

 post-project costs of forest maintenance.



 58.     Sensitivity analyses confirm the project’s financial and economic efficiency across all scenarios.

 They were conducted for several key assumptions and concluded that the project generates positive net

 benefits as long as the social value of carbon included is robust to changes in key assumptions, while the

 net benefits are negative if the social value of carbon excluded is also unaffected by changes in key

 assumptions. The analyses highlight the significant value of carbon sequestration by mangroves and







                                                                                                           Page 20 of 66

The World Bank

Forest Sector Modernization and Coastal Resilience Enhancement Project (P157127)









 coastal forests, as well as the coastal protection they provide, contributing to the project’s economic

 efficiency.



 Implementation Efficiency



 59.      Project implementation was hindered by slow GoV processes and was heavily affected by major

 flood damages caused by a typhoon in 2020, as well as by the COVID-19 pandemic and the resulting

 restrictions on movement of people and goods. These reduced the actual project implementation period

 to under four years compared to the planned project time frame and made several activities unfeasible

 to complete within the closing date.



 60.      The fact that the estimated ENPV for this project’s activities is negative unless the social value of

 carbon is included does not mean that investments in mangroves are not justified. But it does mean that

 those investments must be carefully considered. The role of mangroves and their high carbon

 sequestration capacity could have been further emphasized in the analysis. The analysis uses average

 values for benefits, but benefits are known to be highly heterogeneous, with mangroves and coastal

 forests providing high benefits in some areas and much lower benefits in others. The value of mangroves

 for coastal protection, for example, is extremely high when nearby inland areas are densely populated,

 under high-value agricultural or aquacultural production, or have valuable infrastructure, but much lower

 in other areas. By identifying such high-value areas and targeting investments in them, the net present

 value (NPV) can be increased substantially. Indeed, it is quite possible that if sufficient data were available

 to measure the actual benefits of the mangroves and coastal forests planted under this project, rather

 than using average values, this project’s ENPV might be strongly positive even without carbon benefits.

 These results also emphasize the need to contain the costs of interventions as much as possible—by

 relying on natural regeneration rather than plantation where possible, for example. It also emphasizes

 the need to collect data during project implementation on actual project benefits rather than continuing

 to rely on benefits transfer.

 72.     The preparation of the project drew on a substantial number of extensive background studies,

 Factors Subject to the Control of the Government



 75.      Start-up delays. Although the project was approved in June 2017, the Loan Agreement was only

 signed in August 2018, and effectiveness was only declared in March 2019, almost two years after

 approval. These delays were the result of slow GoV processes at the central level, such as budget

 allocation for the project and signing of on-lending agreements. Implementation was also affected by

 cumbersome internal approval procedures at the province level, such as 4–5 rounds of consultations to

 integrate opinions from different departments for an official decision regarding livelihood packages. These

 reduced the implementation time frame, which affected the completion of some of the activities and their

 downscaling.



 76.      Changes in the policy environment. Viet Nam’s policy and legal environment, including laws on

 public investment, debt management, budget, forest protection, environmental protection, and fisheries,

 evolved significantly during implementation start-up, delaying start-up by another year. These included a

 2019 amendment to the Law on Public Investment and subsequent changes in decrees and circulars.

 Consequently, implementation only began in early 2020, nearly three years after project approval.



 77.     Delays in investment preparation activities and land availability. Once implementation began,

 the completion of investment preparation activities, including confirmation of the eligibility and suitability

 of planting areas, was delayed22 by a lack of standards for some coastal forest species and silvicultural

 practices. Difficulties also occurred in land clearance and handover for mangrove planting due to the

 the necessary province-level approvals in time for the short time frame of coastal forest planting seasons

 in some of the provinces, which delayed the afforestation activities and did not allow the required five-

 year care period as per GoV regulations. Some provinces had also approved additional planning of

 mooring ports, and wind power projects) reducing the project area by 6,753 ha. Quang Tri province had

 the largest project area overlapping with the Southeast Quang Tri Economic Zone, which was reduced by

 the Prime Minister (Decision No. 308/QD-TTg dated March 5, 2021).

   V.      LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS



 96.       For coastal afforestation to succeed, it is not only crucial to choose appropriate sites and

 seedlings that can withstand coastal microclimates and harsh weather but also to consult with and/or

 hire local communities to optimize site selection and carry out the planting and protection activities.

 Even with the shortened implementation period, the survival rate of coastal forest plantation and

 restoration was significantly higher under FMCR compared to previous similar projects in Viet Nam, which

 did not incorporate local knowledge. Under FMCR, local experts conducted thorough assessments of site

 conditions before implementing planting activities, which were time-consuming and led to a reduction in

 the scope in some areas due to unsuitable environments being identified, but the survival rates were high

 in all verified locations. Another reason for the successful afforestation outcome was the key roles played

 by local communities, most often women, in planting and tending the plantations. This emphasizes the

 importance of properly engaging local communities in forest development projects, incorporating their

 knowledge and experience into project activities, and providing training when necessary to ensure their

 active involvement and ownership of the project in the long term.



 97.      Supporting coastal communities’ transition to sustainable livelihoods and empowering women

 in this transition are key to their effective engagement in coastal forest management and protection.

 This requires studying the link between healthy coastal forests and livelihoods, forming groups that foster

 and disseminate forest protection awareness, and providing livelihood training to help communities

 generate nondestructive sustainable incomes from coastal forests. Ensuring strong female participation

 in forest-related activities and training and enabling local women to engage in sustainable production

 models help empower women by providing them with new skills for additional income and often more

 time for other activities including caring for their family. Criteria for improved management arrangements

 should also be defined during preparation.



 98.      Viet Nam’s lengthy approval processes and political economy should be factored into the design

 of operations. Implementation was also challenged by cumbersome central and province-level internal

 approval procedures, which affected key results. This highlights the need to closely analyze the political

 economy and procedural issues during preparation and factor mitigation measures into operations’

 design, including implementation time frames, targets, and incentives. Coordination and communication

 should be strengthened between technical experts and decision-makers to align technical solutions with

 broader political and economic considerations.

                                        ANNEX 4. EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS



  1.      Methods. A cost-benefit analysis (CBA) was carried out with benefits derived from Components

  2 and 3. For Component 2, evaluation methods for forest ecosystem services were used to estimate the

  benefits derived from forest plantation, restoration, and protection. For Component 3, an agricultural

  economic analysis was used to calculate the benefits and costs of agricultural livelihood models

  implemented in this component.



  2.       Costs and benefits. The costs of the project are the total cost of project implementation including

  costs of preparation, investment, operation, maintenance, and opportunity costs. The benefits include

  the following:



          (a)   Component 1 and Component 4. The benefits can be considered as intermediate benefits

                that contribute to the benefits of Components 2 and 3.

          (b)   Component 2. The mangrove and coastal forests planted, restored, and/or protected under

                this component include coastal protection, erosion control, improved aquaculture values,

                fishery and maintenance of fish nursery, carbon sequestration and other benefits from

                collecting and harvesting raw materials, food provided by coastal forest ecosystems,

                recreation, and tourism. Some of these benefits were valued based on studies conducted in

                Viet Nam. For benefits where no such studies were available, benefit transfer methods were

                used, taking the smallest value from the studies in Southeast Asia that most closely match

                the conditions found in the project area. Table 4.1 summarizes the available evidence on

                each kind of benefit and the specific values used in the economic analysis. The costs used in

                the analysis are based on project implementation costs, which include both project

                investment cost and cost paid by local communities taking part in C3 livelihood packages,

                and opportunity costs (benefits of the most possible land use alternatives that are shrimp

                farming in mangrove forest areas and maize crops in coastal terrestrial forest areas).

          (c)   Component 3. The expected benefits will mainly result from the increased income of

                beneficiary households benefiting from the livelihood activities. Particularly, the benefits are

                the mixed income obtained from implementing livelihood models funded by the project. The

                costs include the investment costs from the project and the costs contributed by local people

                participating in implementing the livelihood models. Data were synthesized from the results

                of field surveys conducted by PPMUs.



  3.       Carbon benefits. Carbon benefits are not usually included in the economic benefits estimated

  from the perspective of a particular country, as they are global benefits. In this case, however, it is

  appropriate to include some carbon benefits, as Viet Nam has an emissions reduction purchase agreement

  with the Forest Carbon Partnership Facility (FCPF) for carbon credits from forestry activities in six of the

  project’s provinces (Thanh Hoa, Nghe An, Ha Tinh, Quang Binh, Quang Tri, and Thue Thien Hue). The FCPF

  will buy up to 10.3 million tons, in 2018–2024 at a price of US$5/tCO2e. Carbon sequestration in the

  eligible provinces is thus valued at that price (minus 20 percent to cover the certification and other costs).

  Viet Nam is free to sell any additional credits; the potential value of such credits is estimated separately.

  The global benefits arising from the project forest activities are also estimated separately, using the social

  value of carbon based on the World Bank guidance of 2017 (US$40/tCO2e in 2020, increased by 2.25

  percent per year).



  4.      Data. Secondary data were collected to serve as a reference to calculate the benefits of forest

  environmental services and the effectiveness of livelihood model projects. Documents and reports from

  the project provided data on costs and outputs for each year.



  5.      Assumptions. Key assumptions underlying the economic and financial analysis were as follows:



          (a)   Discount rate: 6 percent which is proposed by the World Bank.

          (b)   Project life-span: 31 years (life-span of the credit).

          (c)   Only 50 percent of the project areas of mangroves can provide fishery service and 30 percent

                of the project coastal mangrove areas can be utilized for recreation and tourism.

          (d)   An 80 percent success rate of maintaining coastal and terrestrial forest and mangrove forest

                plantation and restoration.

          (e)   A 30 percent rate of success of maintaining livelihood models after the support from the

                project ended.

          (f)   The decay proportion of forests in the project area after the project ends is 1–2 percent per

                year (Anger et al.).



  6.       The economic analysis examined two scenarios: a without-project scenario, which assumes no

  interventions to project areas, and a with-project scenario. The economic analysis considers both direct

  and indirect benefits brought to society by the project. Two main scenarios were analyzed: the social value

  of carbon is not accounted for in the project benefits and the social value of carbon is accounted for in

  the project benefits. In addition, sensitivity analyses were applied for examining the project’s efficiency

  against risks that may occur for the two main scenarios. Furthermore, the changes in the project efficiency

  by applying the decay rate of forest areas after the project ended were also examined. Particularly, two

  extreme forest decay rates of 1 percent/year (the lowest rate) and 2 percent/year (the highest rate) were

  applied.



  7.       Results. The results of the CBA are presented in Table 4.3. They indicate that the project generates

  positive net benefits if the social values of carbon are included, but not if they are excluded. The project’s

  ENPV is estimated to be US$159.4 million if the social value of carbon is included and −US$20.7 million

  otherwise. The corresponding estimates of EIRR are 18.2 percent with carbon and 3.6 percent without.

  These estimates are significantly lower than the ENPV estimated at appraisal (US$905.8 million) because

  forest plantation, forest restoration, and forest protection were implemented more slowly and on smaller

  areas than planned at appraisal and the analysis at appraisal had erroneously omitted important costs

  from the analysis, including the opportunity costs of land use alternatives and post-project costs of forest

  maintenance.

  8.       Sensitivity analysis. Sensitivity analysis was conducted for several key assumptions (Table 4.4).

  The conclusion that the project generates positive net benefits as long as the social value of carbon is

  included is robust to changes in key assumption, while the conclusion that the net benefits are negative

  if the social value of carbon is excluded is also unaffected by changes in key assumptions.



  9.       The fact that the estimated ENPV for this project’s activities is negative unless the social value

  of carbon is included does not mean that investments in mangroves are not justified. But it does mean

  that those investments must be carefully considered. The analysis uses average values for benefits, but

  benefits are known to be highly heterogeneous, with mangroves and coastal forests providing very high

  benefits in some areas and much lower benefits in others. The value of mangroves for coastal protection,

  for example, is extremely high when nearby inland areas are densely populated, under high-value

  agricultural or aquacultural production, or have valuable infrastructure, but much lower in other areas.

  By identifying such high-value areas and targeting investments in them, the NPV can be increased

  substantially. Indeed, it is quite possible that if sufficient data were available to measure the actual

  benefits of the mangroves and coastal forests planted under this project, rather than using average values,

  this project’s ENPV might be strongly positive even without carbon benefits. These results also emphasize

  the need to contain the costs of interventions as much as possible—by relying on natural regeneration

  rather than plantation where possible, for example. It also emphasizes the need to collect data during

  project implementation on actual project benefits rather than continuing to rely on benefits transfer.
