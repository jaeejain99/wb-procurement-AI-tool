       I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT



       A. Country Context



   1.      The comprehensive 1986 reforms (known as ‘Ðổi Mới’) in Vietnam have led to stable and

   sustained economic growth and inclusive development—transforming the country from a low- to

   middle-income economy in one generation. Vietnam’s rapid gross domestic product (GDP) per capita

   growth rates have averaged 5.5 percent a year since the early 1990s, yielding a three-and-a-half-fold

   increase in average income. The US$1.90-a-day poverty rate fell from 50 percent in the early 1990s to 3

   percent today. Economic growth has brought dramatic structural transformations, with the agricultural

   sector’s share in GDP falling from more than 40 percent in the late 1980s to less than 20 percent in recent

   years. Access to basic infrastructure has improved substantially. Electricity is now available to almost all

   households, up from less than half in 1993. By the World Bank’s measure of shared prosperity (that is, the

   income growth of the bottom 40 percent of the population), Vietnam is one of the most noteworthy cases

   of long-term shared prosperity, globally.



   2.      Vietnam’s rapid economic development and structural transformation over the past three

   decades has led to extensive urban transformation, with urban areas now contributing more than half

   of the country’s GDP. Vietnam has a low level of urbanization (37.5 percent of the population in 2017)

   compared to most of the countries in the East Asia region, but its urban population has grown from fewer

   than 13 million urban residents in the late 1980s to more than 30 million today. The urbanization process

   has accelerated in recent years, with half the country’s population expected to live in urban areas by

   2035.1 The Government of Vietnam (GoV) understands the strategic role and importance of sustainable

   urbanization in achieving its development aspirations as stipulated and updated in 2009 in the Framework

   Master Plan for Urban Development in Vietnam to 2025 and Vision to 2050. The Master Plan focuses on

   achieving balanced and sustainable strategic growth across the country.



   3.      Vietnam has been ranked among the five countries likely to be most affected by climate

   change,2 due to the concentration of a high proportion of its population and economic assets in

   vulnerable coastal lowlands and deltas. It is estimated that Vietnam’s average annual disaster-related

   losses are approximately US$2.4 billion, or almost 1.5 percent of GDP.3 Sea level rise of 0.22 meter (m)

   and an increase in rainfall between 12.4 percent and 33.3 percent are expected by 2030, based on

   scenario Representative Concentration Pathway (RCP) 4.5,4 which will further increase flood levels.

   Vietnam’s urban transformation has also led to a growth in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, which has

   been faster than any other country in the region.



   4.       The Mekong Delta is specifically vulnerable to climate change with sea-level rise and weather-

   related hazards. These events include typhoons, floods, and drought, which are expected to become more

   frequent and intense with climate change and have significantly affected the socioeconomic development

   of the region. Approximately half of the delta is flooded to a depth of 1 m to 3 m annually, and the situation

    B. Sectoral and Institutional Context



   8.       Vietnam’s secondary cities are recognized as a strategic part of its urban system to achieve

   balanced growth. However, their spatially dispersed urbanization pattern limits their potential for

   agglomeration economies. Vietnam’s urbanization is dominated by the twin economic engines of Hanoi

   and Ho Chi Minh City (HCMC) and their respective economic regions, which together accounted for 38.9

   percent of Vietnam’s overall population and more than 70 percent of national nonagricultural

   employment in 2016.10 In contrast, secondary cities outside these two regions have relatively low

   concentrations of urban population and nonagricultural jobs. These secondary cities have experienced

   spatially dispersed urbanization since 2010, largely driven by their desire to generate more land-related

   revenues and move up the Government’s urban hierarchy.11 Vietnam’s urban areas, as detected using

   nighttime light data, expanded four times more over the seven years period from 2010 to 2017 than they

   did during the previous 14 years from 1996 to 2010.12 As a result, between 2000 and 2015, Vietnam’s

   urban population density remained at 1,890 residents per square kilometer,13 which is low compared with

   other comparable countries in the region and globally. The low urban densities, coupled with poor

   connectivity, not only impede labor mobility, agglomeration economies, and regional integration but also

   contribute to increased vulnerability to climate risks.



   9.      The Mekong Delta Region (MDR), located in proximity to the metropolitan region of HCMC, is

   one of the most populated regions and the agricultural base of Vietnam. The region makes up 12 percent

   of the country’s territory and 19 percent of total population. Despite average economic growth rate of 13

   - 20 percent (2006 to 2010), MDR cities are typically small or medium in size with limited fiscal resources

   and relatively high poverty rates. In 2016, between 14 and 30 percent of the population consisted of low-

   income households, including a large proportion of ethnic minority groups and people with disabilities.14

   Access to basic services, such as sanitation, drainage, and quality water supply, remains as low as 15

   percent in MDR cities compared to 80 percent in large cities such as HCMC and Hanoi. Less than 8 percent

   of MDR cities have sanitary wastewater collection and treatment systems, with the majority lacking such

   services.15 Most MDR cities frequently suffer from flooding and other negative climate change impacts

   because of their low elevation and infrastructure deficits.

   water levels in the Mekong river. Large parts of the city were inundated by flooding in September 2019,

   causing severe disruption to traffic flows and the livelihoods of many low-income people living in these

   areas. The Southern Institute for Water Resources Research (SIWRR)20 calculated that without any

   protection measures, the estimated annual damage to the city is VND 626 billion (US$27 million). The city

   lacks a comprehensive approach to managing flood risk, which is exacerbated by the lack of flood

   defenses, an aging drainage system which is in poor condition, and a canal network which suffers from

   sedimentation and human encroachment. Untreated wastewater quality in the city is reported21 to

   exceed the national standard by 1.3–11.0 times.22 An estimated 12,000 m3 of predominantly untreated

   wastewater is being discharged directly to rivers and canals each day, leading to serious public health

   impacts. The city’s priorities are urban wastewater system upgrades, flood risk mitigation infrastructures,

   and nature-based solutions to increase water retention.



   12.      The severe flood risk in the core urban area, together with the lack of integrated planning, has

   led the city to sprawl outward along the national roads and main waterways in different directions, while

   large pockets of land in the core urban area remain undeveloped. This fragmented development pattern

   results in high and unsustainable costs for infrastructure provision and limits the ability of the Government

   to provide adequate water and sanitation services to the public. This in turn results in greater vulnerability

   to water and vector-borne diseases and the spread of disease. The sprawl also causes increased GHG

   emissions and high transport costs, along with the loss of productive water-retaining agricultural land. An

   integrated approach to flood risk management and urban planning will promote compact development,

   lower the cost of basic service provision, and reduce vulnerability to climate, disaster, and health risks.

   24.     The objective of this component is to reduce flood-related risks and improve environmental

   sanitation in the urban core of Vinh Long City through investments in drainage, flood protection

   structures, sewage networks, and wastewater treatment. In addition to improving the environmental

   sanitation conditions in the city, the upgrading of wastewater collection and treatment in Vinh Long will

   Subcomponent 1.1: Flood risk mitigation and urban drainage



   25.      This subcomponent will finance flood risk mitigation structures such as embankments and tidal

   sluice gates, rehabilitation, and improvement of the canal and drainage system in the city core areas, and

   investments in green infrastructure to retain and infiltrate rainwater. A polder approach will be used for

   flood risk mitigation, which can be expressed as a structural system consisting of (a) a closed ‘ring

   embankment with tidal sluice gates’ to protect areas on the edge of rivers from high water levels ( that is,

   river and tide floods) and (b) a drainage system including regulated sluice gates, open canals, sewers,

   stormwater retention, and pumps to facilitate runoff of rainwater.



   26.     In line with city planning,27 three small polders will be established along the small branches of the

   Co Chien river, to prioritize flood protection for the existing dense urban areas (1,788 ha across seven

   urban wards). The design of the polders will ensure that the navigation needs and the water flow in the

   main branches of the Co Chien river are preserved. The area to the south, which is predominantly

   agricultural land, is reserved for urbanization over the next 20 years, according to the city’s Master Plan.

   This area would be protected in the future as it becomes urbanized, either through an expansion of the

   polder structure or through elevation of the ground level for new development, combined with the

   creation of additional retention capacity in low-lying areas. A hydraulic modeling study was completed to

   assess the city’s flood risk with updated data, analyze the cost benefit of various investment options, and

   demonstrate how green solutions such as retention areas could play a more important role as part of the

   overall flood risk mitigation strategy. The stabilization of the polder embankment will consider green

   bioengineering methods. Where possible, amenities comprising green spaces with native and shade

   providing tree species and promenades with tracks and boardwalks along the embankment will be

   provided for both cyclists and pedestrians to turn the waterfront into an attractive recreational area.

    F. Rationale for Bank Involvement and Role of Partners



   42.     The project seeks to tackle a range of interrelated issues affecting the urban development of Vinh

   Long including the impact of climate change and disaster risks such as flooding, which are compounded

   by weak institutional capacity. Addressing these interrelated sectoral issues requires an integrated

   approach to ensure the project’s success and sustainability. This requires both knowledge and technical

   expertise, as well as the safeguarding of important social and environmental aspects relating to the

   project. With more than three decades of sustained engagement in urban development across multiple

   sectors in Vietnam and, in particular, the MDR, the World Bank is strongly positioned to support this

   project. The World Bank will be able to maximize the impact of the project investments by leveraging its

   global best practices and extensive cross-sectoral knowledge gained through similar projects in Vietnam,

   regionally and internationally.



   43.     The proposed interventions are complementary to and integrated with the existing government,

   World Bank, and development partners’ operations and technical assistance in the Mekong Delta. The

   World Bank has supported several GoV projects in the MDR including the Mekong Delta Region Urban

   Upgrading Project, concluded in 2018, which financed infrastructure improvements in 6 of the 13 cities in

   the MDR; the Scaling Up Urban Upgrading Project (SUUP) in the remaining seven cities in the MDR, which

   invests US$35 million for improving basic infrastructure in low-income areas in Vinh Long and

   strengthening urban planning capacity in the city; the Can Tho Urban Development and Resilience Project,

   focusing on the mitigation of flood risks and adaptation to climate change; and the Mekong Delta

   Integrated Climate Resilience and Sustainable Livelihood Project, under implementation since 2016,

   covering nine provinces in the MDR including two rural districts in Vinh Long. Vinh Long Province is also

   participating in the ongoing World Bank-financed Vietnam Improved Land Governance and Database

   Project to set up a Multipurpose Land Information System, which will expand on Vinh Long’s existing

   geodatabase by including datasets relating to land values and planning. The relevant lessons and synergies

   from these projects will be reflected in the proposed project.



   44.     The proposed interventions are based on several completed and ongoing studies financed by the

   World Bank and other donors including a number of World Bank studies related to climate resilience in

   the MDR, including studies for the MDR comprehensive development plan; an Asian Development Bank

   (ADB)-financed study on sanitation in Vinh Long,31 used to inform the design of the wastewater

   component of the project; and a GIZ study on land subsidence in Mekong Delta32 used for flood risk

   analysis. The World Bank is exploring the potential to collaborate with GIZ on providing vocational training

   for technical staff responsible for operating and maintaining the WWTP through GIZ’s Reform of Technical

   and Vocational Education and Training Programme.33

   53.      Institutional strengthening is vital to ensure project success. The design of the proposed project

   has been informed by learning from other similar World Bank projects and recognizes the importance of

   strong institutional arrangements. Involving local professional companies in preparation, planning,

   implementation, and O&M of facilities under the project reinforces the local capacity and is critical for

   sustainable service delivery. Given the complex cross-sectoral nature of flooding in the MDR and the

   interlinked roles and responsibilities of the numerous agencies involved (DARD, DOC, DOT, DONRE, and

   so on), the project will focus on improving coordination, monitoring, and data sharing. An integrated flood

   risk management information system will define clear roles and responsibilities of the key agencies that

   work together to coordinate flood control and drainage in the city. Monitoring and access to quality data

   for the agencies will be improved through smart Internet-based sensor systems and a geospatial data

   sharing platform that will be developed to integrate multiple data sources, from different departments in

   Vinh Long, for spatial planning and development.



   54.     Community engagement improves sustainability. International experience and learning from

   Vietnam show that projects with strong community participation in planning and prioritization of

   investments have produced much better results in beneficiary satisfaction and O&M during project

   implementation, promoting sustainability. Community engagement and mobilization are most effective

   when they take place from the design phase through to completion and when they include women. In the

   Zhejiang Rural Water Supply and Sanitation Project, broad public consultation was carried out, including

   on infrastructure designs and community representatives participated in construction supervision. These

   measures increased both beneficiary awareness and ownership, as well as connections to the sewer

   network and payment for wastewater services. The proposed project will dedicate considerable time and

   funds to engaging with citizens and community organizations throughout the design and implementation

   process to enhance their ownership and awareness of the project. The project will also ensure a strong

   focus on gender through gender-segregated consultations on project design and involvement of the

   Women’s Union in the IEC campaigns.

    C. Sustainability



   61.       Physical sustainability. A number of different investment options to protect the urban core of the

   city were considered for this project. The selection criteria used to identify the proposed option included

   application of a detailed hydraulic and exposure model, consideration of the economic rate of return, the

   resilience of the investments to climate change, and the social and environmental impact of the

   investments. The selected infrastructure investments are designed to be highly resilient to the forecast

   climate change impacts, including flooding, which will improve sustainability of the infrastructure. The use

   of green approaches will ensure environmental sustainability. Creating public space along canals,

   facilitating access to diverse services and facilities through increased connectivity, and creating a system

   for citizen engagement will contribute to social sustainability. The quality of work will be ensured by using

   international best practices for engineering designs, as well as through construction supervision, and

   technical audits. In addition, the designs will be informed by demographic, topographic, hydrologic, and

   land use/cover changes.



   62.      Financial sustainability. The investments will reduce the annual contingent liability posed by

   disasters and therefore reduce the fiscal burden on government accounts. A fiscal analysis found that the

   city has sufficient financial resources for resettlement and has the capacity to repay the debt incurred.

   The investment in the WWTP and household connections to the sewer network will be placed on a sound

   footing for sustainable operation, through involvement of the private sector and the application of cost-

   reflective tariffs.



   63.      Institutional sustainability. Institutional sustainability will be promoted through clear

   identification of the asset owners and managers, comprehensive capacity building, and technical

   assistance under the project. A key outcome of the project will be improved human resource capacity for

   flood risk management and infrastructure O&M. Technical assistance to improve data sharing and

   leveraging of ICT for improved urban and flood risk management will enable a data-driven decision-

   making approach for Vinh Long and improved transport management capacity for DOT will ensure the

   sustainable management of traffic infrastructure and flows in the city.

    IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY



    A. Technical, Economic, and Financial Analysis (if applicable)



   Technical



   64.     Flood risk mitigation. The flood risks in Vinh Long come from both the water level in the Co Chien

   river and from the local rainfall. The water level in the river is governed by the discharge of the Mekong

   river and the tides and storm surges of the East Sea. A study38 was carried out to determine what measures

   are necessary to protect Vinh Long against flooding for both threats under various scenarios of land use,

   including application of nature-based solutions. The results indicate that polders are an appropriate

   approach to protect the urban core area of Vinh Long against flooding from the Co Chien river.



   65.     The three smaller polders developed by the project prioritize flood protection for the existing

   dense urban areas (1,788 ha across seven urban wards), while the planned new urban area to the south

   would be protected in the future, either through an expansion of the polder structure or through elevation

   of the ground level, combined with the creation of retention areas. During extreme high river levels, the

   embankments and the closed sluice gates will protect the core urban area of the city from inundation.



   66.     Given the vulnerability of pumping to poor maintenance and power supply interruptions, the use

   of pumps will be minimized through the provision of retention areas (ponds and lakes). At low tide, surface

   water will drain to the Co Chien river through the sewers, canals (being dredged and cleaned up under

   this project), and the opened sluice gates. During periods of very high discharge, water volumes will

   exceed the capacity of the system and some pumping will be necessary. Suitable retention areas, without

   negative consequences for the functioning of the city, are currently being identified by the city.



   67.      A study will also be conducted to analyze land subsidence rates and patterns in Vinh Long using

   satellite imagery, to better understand the implication of land subsidence in the city. Although the existing

   building code and design standards in Vietnam do not specify consideration on land subsidence, design of

   the flood mitigation structures will need to consider the need for future upgrade (that is, increasing the

   crest level) because of land subsidence to sustain their services in the long run.



   68.      Some nonstructural measures will be required to ensure proper O&M of the new flood risk

   mitigation infrastructure. The selection of one department with responsibility for management of the

   polders is recommended to avoid ambiguity in the responsibilities for O&M. Training on flood risk

   management and O&M for relevant staff of city departments will ensure proper O&M of infrastructure

   investments. The city will be connected to the database of the Southern Regional Hydrometeorological

   Center for predictions of water levels and rainfall, and a protocol for closing and opening the gates, based

   on these predictions, will be developed. An early warning system for the inhabitants of Vinh Long, in case

   of extreme events, will also be developed, together with raising public awareness on these issues.



   69.     Drainage. Separate systems of drainage and sewerage have been designed to protect project

   areas against 10-year return period rainfall events. This complies with the government’s regulations and

   70.       Wastewater collection and treatment. The urban wastewater management activities have been

   designed to maximize tertiary sewer coverage, household connections, and the associated environmental

   sanitation and public health benefits. Sewer separation will further improve sanitation and health by

   avoiding overflows of stormwater mixed with wastewater during periods of high rainfall. Moreover,

   separating stormwater and wastewater is a cost beneficial option as (a) wastewater leakage/discharge to

   the environment will be minimized and odor from box culverts will be eliminated, (b) the WWTP’s capacity

   will be lesser because lesser stormwater will be taken to the plant, (c) the drainage network will be shorter

   as stormwater is discharged to the closest water body, (d) operation of the WWTP will be improved

   because of more consistent quality of influents entering the plant, and (d) the O&M cost for the drainage

   network will be reduced because of lower pipe sedimentation.



   71.     Safely managed water, sanitation, and hygiene services are an essential part of preventing and

   protecting human health during infectious disease outbreaks, including the current COVID-19 pandemic.

   Improving sanitation through investing in wastewater systems is a cost-effective strategy for increasing

   pandemic preparedness. Good waste management practices that are consistently applied serve as

   barriers to human-to-human transmission of the COVID-19 virus.



   72.      The SBR technology was selected for the WWTP, based on a comparison of five common

   treatment technologies in Vietnam39 and consideration of their technical, economic, environmental, and

   social aspects. The analysis shows that the SBR has significantly lower construction costs compared to

   CAS, TF, and OD technologies. The SBR requires the smallest land area of the technologies and can be

   expanded easily in the future, which is well suited to the available area for the WWTP in Vinh Long. With

   the SBR, all treated wastewater effluent indicators can meet the criteria of category A. The SBR requires

   more technical O&M skills; however, the operation of the WWTP will be outsourced to a professional

   entity with experience in the SBR technology, through a competitive process (the first operation phase

   will be included in a DBO contract).



   73.     The design of the WWTP is also based on the thorough analysis of projected wastewater

   generation through water demand projections rather than relying solely on the water consumption norms

   suggested in the national standards. In line with Decree 80/2014/NĐ-CP, user fees will be collected from

   customers to cover a part of the O&M costs of the wastewater and drainage, depreciation of short-lived

   assets such as vehicles and equipment, and any loan interest charges. Collection of cost-reflective tariffs

   will promote sustainability. A road map for the increase of wastewater tariffs and cost recovery of O&M

   has been agreed with the local government. Tariffs will be gradually increased each year during project

   implementation, coupled with expansions in service coverage, to fully cover the O&M cost in the future.

   The increases in wastewater tariffs are expected to remain below 5 percent of poor households’ income.

   Most households are willing to pay a cost-reflective tariff for wastewater services.

   76.     Economic. An economic feasibility analysis was carried out for the proposed investments and

   yielded satisfactory overall findings. All investments in Components 1 and 2 were analyzed. The

   resettlement costs for people affected by the project, along with other project management support costs

   in Component 4, were allocated to Components 1 and 2 for the purposes of the analysis. The analysis

   implies that all the investments are economically viable with an economic internal rate of return (EIRR)

   higher than the discount rate of 10 percent, even though only basic benefits were quantified. For

   Component 1, the EIRR of investments in flood risk management and urban drainage is 29.0 percent while

   that of wastewater collection and treatment is 27.3 percent. For Component 2, the overall return is 21.2

   percent, highest (36.8 percent) for the Ward 8-Ward 9 Connection Road and lowest (16.3 percent) for

   Urban Main Road No.1. A sensitivity analysis, which combined impacts of a 10 percent increase in the

   economic investment cost and a 10 percent decrease in the benefits, also showed that the proposed

   investments remain sustainable in terms of economic benefits. In addition to the quantifiable benefits,

   the project is expected to bring in greater long-term positive impacts on public health and lifestyle

   behaviors, as well as on the city landscape, because of the proposed green and smart initiatives.

       103. The overall risk to the project is rated Substantial, given the Substantial risk ratings for several

       categories, including technical, institutional and implementation capacity, fiduciary, and ESF standards.



       104. Technical risk is assessed as Substantial. Although Vinh Long City has relevant experience

       implementing the ongoing World Bank financed SUUP, the scope of Component 1 is more complicated

       than the SUUP. Under this project there are a number of technical and coordination challenges relating

       to the nature of the flood issues affecting the city, coordination required for the effective operation of the

       flood prevention infrastructure, related flood risk management system, and nature-based solutions, as

       well as the management of the WWTP and the household connections and implementation of the tariff

       road map. In addition, the IEC activities under the project require a level of technical design, civil works,

       and institutional coordination that are more complicated compared to the SUUP. The transport

       interventions proposed under Component 2 are not complex, but the scope of urban planning and

       development control along these corridors presents a capacity challenge for the city. There is a further

       risk related to the degree to which the proposed investments will be sufficiently climate resilient from a

       technical perspective, given the inherent uncertainty in climate change. In addition, the existing building

       codes and construction standards do not specify how to take into account land subsidence in the design

       of structures. Finally, there is a risk to wastewater collection and treatment services during the recovery

       phase of a disease outbreak such as COVID-19. Impacts could include disruptions to O&M activities and

       the inability of customers to pay bills, which might affect the continuity and quality of wastewater services

       as well as the associated IEC activities. If not mitigated, these impacts could increase the risk of further

       disease spread and hamper recovery.

   114. Climate change screening. A rapid assessment of Vinh Long City’s exposure to climate change and

   disaster risks was conducted using satellite imagery, open-source tools, and various global data sets. Vinh

   Long has a relatively flat terrain with a slope of less than 2 degrees and the elevation is quite low compared

   to the sea level (absolute elevation from 0.6 m to 1.2 m accounts for 90 percent of the natural area). As

   such, flooding and monsoons are a regular threat, which is exacerbated by sea level rise and land

   subsidence. The preliminary results show that around 60 percent of the densest settlement areas (that is,

   between 85 and 155 persons per 100 m2) are exposed to both river and rainwater flooding. The greatest

   exposure is concentrated in areas along the Co Chien river and to the south of the city along major arterial

   roads. The proposed project aims to mitigate the flood risks of core urban areas by investing in flood

   protection structures, drainage systems, and nature-based solutions, the design of which will take into

   account climate change scenarios. Combining nature-based solutions is also expected to contribute to

   climate change mitigation by reducing GHGs associated with the production of concrete. The potential

   application of solar energy and energy efficiency measures will be considered in the design and operation

   of the WWTP to reduce the plant’s CO2 footprint.

                                         ANNEX 1: Detailed Project Description



   Project Components



   Component 1: Flood Risk Management and Environmental Sanitation (Total Cost: US$93.0 million; IDA

   Credit: US$73.8 million; DRIVE Grant: US$17.1 million; Counterpart Funds: US$2.1 million)



   1.      The objective of this component is to reduce flood-related risks and improve environmental

   sanitation in the urban core of Vinh Long City through investments in drainage, flood protection measures,

   sewage networks, and wastewater treatment. In addition to improving the environmental sanitation

   conditions in the city, the upgrading of wastewater collection and treatment in Vinh Long will contribute

   to improving surface water quality. The design of this component will be based on the existing national

   building codes and will be informed by the findings of the Urban Flood Risk Analysis study, carried out

   during project preparation by the SIWRR.44 The study assessed the flood risks and hazards affecting Vinh

   Long City, as well as the vulnerability of the affected community, and proposed an optimized balance

   between gray and green infrastructure (or nature-based solutions) for flood risk mitigation.



   Subcomponent 1.1: Flood risk mitigation and urban drainage



   2.       This subcomponent will finance flood risk mitigation structures such as embankments and tidal

   sluice gates, as well as the rehabilitation and improvement of the canal and drainage system in the city

   core areas, including construction of new drains, dredging of canal systems, and investments in green

   infrastructure to retain and infiltrate rainwater.



   3.       A polder approach will be used for flood risk mitigation, which can be expressed as a structural

   system consisting of a closed ‘ring embankment with tidal sluicegates/valves’ to protect areas on the edge

   of rivers from high water levels (river and tide floods). To validate the polder approach and optimize

   options for green, nature-based solutions, a flood risk assessment based on an expansion of the

   computerized hydraulic modeling of the core urban area of Vinh Long City, completed under the SUUP,

   was carried out through the SIWRR study using nine different scenarios (table 1.1). The nine scenarios

   were grouped by three different land uses: (a) existing land use, (b) Master Plan land use, and (c) Master

   Plan land use with optimized green solutions. Three scenarios were developed for each of the three land

   use groups: (a) existing structures only, (b) additional flood control structures for three polders according

   to the Master Plan, and (c) additional flood control structures in the form of embankments with sluice

   gates for three smaller polders. All nine scenarios were simulated with various frequencies of 1 percent,

   2 percent, 4 percent, 10 percent, and 20 percent for rainfall and river water levels for flood risk analysis.

   detention, and retention ponds. The SIWRR study identified that the required pumping capacity for the

   urban area will be drastically reduced when 112 ha of retention areas are used.

                              ANNEX 3: Economic, Financial, and Fiscal Analysis



   Economic Analysis



   1.      A cost-benefit analysis was performed to assess the economic viability of investments proposed

   under Components 1 and 2 of the project, which represent more than 90 percent of the project cost. The

   resettlement costs of households affected by the project have been fully included in the economic costs

   of investments for the purposes of the economic analysis. A separate cost-benefit evaluation for

   Component 3 (Resettlement Area Development) was therefore not performed, to avoid double counting.



   Approach



   2.       The evaluation follows the conventional economic appraisal methodology for infrastructure

   improvement schemes. It compares annual discounted costs and benefits of the project to society as a

   whole, over the project life cycle in ‘with’ and ‘without’ cases using the NPV and the IRR indicators to

   justify the economic viability.



   3.      The NPV is a principal measure of economic viability. The NPV is the difference between the

   present value of economic costs and the present value of economic benefits. If the NPV is greater than

   zero, the project is considered to be viable.



   4.      The IRR is the discount rate at which the present value of economic benefits equals the present

   value of economic costs. If it exceeds the discount rate, then the project is considered viable.



   5.      Annual project costs and benefits are evaluated for a 25-year period of benefit, allowing for a 5-

   year construction period starting in 2021.



   6.      A discount rate of 10 percent is applied to convert the annual cost/benefit streams into present

   value in 2019 according to the recent Guidelines of the World Bank in 2016.



   7.      The unit prices used for estimation of project costs and benefits are based on prices in Vietnam

   in 2019. The evaluation has been conducted using U.S. dollar as the unit of currency. The exchange rate

   used (VND 23,400 = US$1) is close to the market rate and so no shadow foreign exchange rate is

   considered necessary. The economic analysis is conducted on the basis of constant prices without taking

   into account the impact of inflation on prices.



   8.       For the economic evaluation, costs and benefits have been calculated from economic prices

   rather than market or financial prices. Economic prices reflect the resource cost or value of an item to the

   country. Economic costs are the actual parts of the results of financial cost conversion in which market

   factors are eliminated. Each category of capital costs and O&M costs is broken down into categories such

   as traded materials; non-traded materials; skilled labor; unskilled labor; transfer cost (taxes, subsidies,

   and levies); and other categories. These financial costs have been converted from domestic currency to

   U.S. dollars and are multiplied with the respective conversion factors to arrive at economic prices.



   9.     The economic costs are converted from the financial cost using a conversion factor of 0.90 for

   investment costs and 0.95 for O&M costs.

   savings of improved sanitation projects. As a result, the beneficiaries in the project area would save VND

   146 billion of health care costs annually.



   17.      Savings in productive time. Flood mitigation not only saves health care costs but also enables

   beneficiaries to work more productively. If residents in the project area are able to avoid spending three

   days per year on sick leave or carrying out post-flood clean-up, they would gain an additional VND 92

   value along canals proposed for embankment improvements under the project in the urban core area

   would increase by VND 170 billion. Investment in the wastewater collection and treatment system would
