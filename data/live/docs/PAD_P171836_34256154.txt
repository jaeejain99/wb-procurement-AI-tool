I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT



A. Country Context

1.     Over the past decade, Nepal’s economy has demonstrated impressive growth and resilience despite a variety

of economic shocks. COVID induced movement restrictions accompanied by a near shutdown of tourism resulted in

Nepal’s first economic contraction in almost 40 years in FY20 (-2.4 percent). Vaccine roll-out and border re-opening

supported the economy’s recovery. Growth reached 4.8 and 5.6 percent in FY21 and FY22 respectively.

2.     Nepal’s economy slowed significantly in FY23 due in part to monetary tightening, sluggish capital expenditure,

and import restriction measures. A 150 basis points increase in the monetary policy rate and import restriction measures

to stem foreign exchange losses under the fixed exchange rate regime muted growth in the industrial and services

sectors, which grew by 0.6 and 2.3 percent during the year, respectively. Manufacturing, construction, and the wholesale

and retail trade sectors all contracted.

3.      Despite the economic slowdown, average consumer price inflation accelerated to a seven-year high of 7.8

percent. Cereals, housing and utilities prices together account for over 30 percent of the consumer price index and their

prices rose. Inflation exceeded Nepal’s Central Bank’s policy ceiling of 7 percent despite tighter monetary policy,

reflecting in part India’s export ban on wheat and flour.

4.     The current account deficit narrowed from 12.6 percent to 1.3 percent of gross domestic product (GDP)

between FY22 and FY23, reflecting a significant decline in goods imports and a strong rebound in remittances. Exports

and foreign direct investment are small relative to remittance inflows and imports. In the context of relatively low

external debt repayments, foreign reserves rose to 10 months of import cover as of end-FY23, well above the policy floor

of 7 months of import cover.

5.       The fiscal deficit almost doubled from 3.2 percent to 6.1 percent of GDP between FY22 and FY23. Over half of

all fiscal revenues are trade related. As imports declined in FY23, fiscal revenue contracted in nominal terms for the first

time since FY21, falling from 23.1 percent to 19.2 percent of GDP between FY22 and FY23. Expenditures also declined

but by much less (1 percentage point of GDP), reflecting lower transfers to subnational governments. The deficit was

financed through external concessional borrowing, domestic borrowing, and the prior year’s cash balance. The May 2023

Joint World Bank-International Monetary Fund Debt Sustainability Analysis finds that the country’s risk of debt distress

remains low for both external and overall public debt.

6.      Economic growth is expected to rebound to 3.9 percent in FY24 and 5.0 percent in FY25 under the baseline

forecast. The baseline forecast assumes that (a) monetary policy will be gradually eased in FY24 and FY25 relative to

FY23; (b) lumpy skin disease affecting livestock – which has infected 1.3 million animals since April 2023 will be under

control by mid-January 2024; (c) India’s export ban on select cereals and vegetables will be lifted by FY25; and (d)

electricity exports to India and Bangladesh will reach 1,000 megawatts annually by FY25. The lagged impact of lifting

import restriction measures in January 2023 and gradual loosening of monetary policy are expected to support the

industrial and services sectors. Agricultural growth is projected to slow down in FY24, in part due to livestock losses, then

provide a boost in FY25 once the disease is under control.

7.      The fiscal deficit is expected to narrow from 6.1 percent to 3.3 percent of GDP by FY25. Revenue growth should

increase in FY24 with stronger GDP, import growth, and the removal of value added tax exemptions on select goods. The

FY24 federal budget includes lower capital spending, lower transfers to subnational governments, and higher debt

servicing costs. Public investment should rise in FY25 on the back of revenue growth and the implementation of a national

project bank for infrastructure investments. Public debt is expected to peak in FY24 and then recede slightly to 41.2

percent of GDP by FY25.

8.      Nepal’s political system is democratic and is a coalition-based government. Nepal has had two successive

coalition governments that took office since the federal and provincial elections held in November 2022. The first

coalition lasted for only two months, which was followed by the current coalition comprising eight political parties. This

has affected the provincial government set up as well. The Government’s 16th periodic development plan (2024/25-

2028/29) is expected to be finalized in April 2024. At the sub-national level, seven provinces and 753 local governments

have governing oversight for which legislation, institutions, and administrative procedures have been constitutionally

formalized while the task of enacting new laws and amending existing ones in line with the 2015 constitution is still in

progress.



B. Sectoral and Institutional Context

9.     For land-locked Nepal, which is largely hilly and mountainous, roads are lifelines. Roads carry 90 percent of

goods and passengers and are critical for accessing socio-economic opportunities; mitigating climate impacts; and for

increasing proximity to government services. Of the total road network in Nepal, of about 77,000 km,1 about 86 percent

(63,500 km) comprises the provincial and local road network (PLRN), which provides access and connectivity to nearly

three-fourths of rural populations that are mostly agrarian.

10.     Federalism under the 2015 constitution presents an opportunity to address spatial disparities in development,

alleviate poverty, and transform rural development through effective devolution This has progressively led to better

participation from the federal, provincial, and local levels and has facilitated fiscal and functional devolution to make

lower tier governments more resourceful. Making an effective transition to federalism requires sustained efforts toward

capacitating institutions, creating role clarity, and facilitating and empowering provincial and local governments (PLGs)

toward resource mobilization.

11.     With the transition of Nepal to a federal structure and decentralized form of governance in 2015, responsibility

for the development and maintenance of provincial and local roads has been vested with the respective PLGs with key

policy and institutional frameworks in flux. As a unitary state till 2015, the provincial and local roads used to be referred

to as the Local Road Network (LRN)2 and their development used to be carried out by the District Technical Offices, with

substantial financial and technical support from the Ministry of Federal Affairs and Local Development. However, post

federalism, the institutional and policy frameworks are in a flux and need additional time for adjustment and

reorientation: the hierarchy (that is, functional classification) of the road system within PLRN is yet to be adequately

defined, as approval of the National Public Road Bill (which defines the category of roads and their ownership) is still

pending. Further, recruitment of technical staff at PLGs is affected by the delay in the approval of provincial public service

commissions. The sector is in transition and fraught with several other challenges, including the lack of uniform standards

and specifications, ad-hoc planning and prioritization, investments that lag demand, lack of systematic maintenance,

inefficient processes such as numerous small contracts leading to significant time, cost overruns, and inadequate

implementation capacity. The shaping of nascent institutions and corresponding policies require long-term programmatic

engagement.

12.    Nepal’s rural population is highly vulnerable due to the poor condition of the rural road network and is far from

achieving the aspirations of federalism. About 47 percent of the rural population is estimated to be living at a distance

greater than 2 km from the nearest road in good condition. Currently, only about one-fourth of the PLRN provides all-

weather connectivity to the population (which drops during the rainy season3), while the remainder provides either low

service or is not serviceable at all due to continuous neglect of maintenance. These roads were constructed to provide

access to rural and remote areas without considering their operability and sustainability over the longer term. The roads

13.    Physical remoteness influences human and economic development in Nepal. There exist significant disparities

in access to services across local governments and distribution of challenges is uneven across provinces. While better

accessibility and connectivity correlate with poverty reduction, spatial inequalities remain prominent, with poverty

concentrated in rural and isolated regions. Among the 460 rural municipalities in Nepal, 319 representing 65 percent of

the rural population, are categorized as ‘very remote or ‘remote’ where access to essential services remain a challenge,

with significant variation in both across and within provinces. Remarkably, 21 of these rural municipalities lack any road

access, 16 of which are situated in the Karnali and Sudarpaschim Provinces. Furthermore, 200 of these rural municipalities

await paved roads and rely mainly on non-motorized transport. Nearly 40 percent of the population (exceeding the

government’s target of 10 percent) lives more than 30 minutes away from a financial institution and 20 percent must

undertake a two-hour journey to reach the nearest hospital. However, in Karnali Province, this percentage rises to more

than 80 percent on average. Service accessibility within Karnali Province also varies greatly between local governments.

For example, over 65 percent of the population in Adanchuli and Tanjakot municipalities must travel at least eight hours

to reach the nearest hospital, while residents in Chankheli face the same travel time4 showing the significant variations

in access both across and within provinces.

14.    The underdeveloped road network also hinders integration into larger markets and jeopardizes economic

opportunities and income levels. Research of rural Nepal indicates that, while agriculture provides employment to 72

percent of population, the increasing travel time to markets adversely affects income, cropping intensity, access to non-

farm employment, and education levels. One percent increase in travel times to paved roads decreases rural farmland

values and implied profits by 0.25 percent.5 It is further compounded by the lack of associated infrastructure facilities

such as warehouses and cold storage facilities and poor transport services for storage and handling of agricultural

produce.

15.     Investments in the PLRN are insufficient to meet sector needs, hence, available funds must be deployed in a

cost-effective manner for greater efficiency. According to government estimates, local governments allocated 54

percent of their budget for the construction and maintenance of roads, and the provincial governments allocated 19

percent to transport (roads, bridges, transport management etc.). Because of limited internal revenue generation, annual

allocations for road/infrastructure are mostly linked to fiscal transfers from the federal government. Post Federalism,

sector funding has increased substantially to US$4.54 billion,6 through which the recently established PLGs have

prioritized road construction and upgrading, resulting in the opening of 5,052 km of new earthen tracks to meet basic

connectivity needs. While the local governments are keen to demonstrate progress, they lack planning capacity and are

continuing the practice of ploughing roads into hillsides with little regard for environmental outcomes, investment

efficiency and basic engineering principles. Therefore, efforts to scale up investment in rural accessibility and better

connectivity must be complemented by a substantive increase in efficiency. Objective criteria for road selection, network

16.     Rural communities, industrial facilities, and infrastructure are highly vulnerable to extreme weather events and

natural disasters. Due to the mountainous terrain and complex geology, the road network is highly vulnerable to erratic

precipitation patterns (in both intensity and frequency) leading to flooding, landslides, flash floods, mass wasting, debris

flow, regional sedimentation, rise in groundwater levels, windstorms and increasing temperatures. Of the 488 landslides

reported in 2020, 59 occurred along the side of the road and 62 occurred on roads, in all cases obstructing vehicular flow7

and causing delays and disturbances in the transportation of necessities to the rural communities and jeopardizing

livelihood opportunities. The current construction practices of rural roads often do not consider climate and/or hazard

risk profiling-based design, construction, and maintenance. Consequently, landslides are triggered in Nepal due to the

prevailing practice of informal engineering of rural roads, where haphazard track opening, and road construction is being

done without due consideration to engineering design and construction standards. Experience in Nepal indicates that

since 2008, 87 percent of landslides (179 of 205 landslides) were triggered by the construction of poorly engineered

roads. Further, there is no focus on maintenance, leading to the progressive degradation of road infrastructure. This

makes communities and animal and plant life vulnerable to these disasters. Therefore, orientation is required not only

for building resilient and sustainable infrastructure but also for incorporating disaster response management to minimize

disruption to services in case of climate events. Investment plans, road prioritization/ selection, design, and construction

need to integrate climate and disaster vulnerability and risk assessments, climate monitoring and forecasting systems,

use of nature-based solutions and early warning systems, community-driven response mechanisms, and building of

community resilience.

17.     A significant gender gap in employment still exists in the rural roads sector. Women lack opportunities, skills,

training, and an enabling environment in the road/ transport sector, which is exacerbated by the prevalent social norms

and safety concerns. Female labour force participation in the construction sector is 4.2 percent, compared to 19.5 percent

for men. This low participation rate is due to limited opportunities but also prevalent social norms and lack of appropriate

skills and education. The lack of access to training opportunities to develop skills and network also limits women being

employed in the sector. Nepal has low levels of women in science, technology, engineering, and math. According to the

Nepal Labour Force Survey (2017/18), only 0.3 percent of women are in professional, scientific, and technical activities,

and only one in seven managers is a woman. Similarly, latest data from the Department of Local Infrastructure (DoLI)

shows that out of 89 technical positions (government staff), only 12 are women.8 Women’s voice and participation in

decision-making are limited at the community level, as very few women are involved in road user groups and in key

decision-making roles. Women-led micro, small and medium enterprises (MSMEs) also have fewer opportunities, limited

mobility, and access to services.

18.    Development partners have valuable experience working with district roads in Nepal and have introduced

sustainable good practices. Apart from the World Bank, the Asian Development Bank (ADB) (through the ongoing

investment and technical assistance (TA) support for the Rural Connectivity Improvement Program); the Swiss

Development Corporation (through Local Road Bridge Program-IV, TA for trail bridges); and the Foreign, Commonwealth,

and Development Office (FCDO) (through investment lending and TA) have contributed to several rural access and

connectivity programs. Post federalism, some development partners have also started engaging directly with provinces

and municipalities. The experience gained through these engagements has been valuable in introducing good practices

in maintenance, occupational, health, and safety (OHS), and the use of information technology. However, longer-term

engagement is required to replicate and mainstream these practices sector-wide in a coherent manner across the

country.

D. Series of Projects

24.     The Project is the first in a Series of Projects (SOP). There are three principal reasons for choosing the single-

borrower SOP modality over a stand-alone investment project financing approach: (i) addressing the extensive and

complex challenges associated with large-scale investments and varying capacities of PLGs; (ii) ongoing decentralization

efforts necessitates a flexible and phased strategy; and (iii) progressively shape emerging PLGs, cater to the distinct needs

of provinces and municipalities, and methodically tackle sector-specific challenges while simultaneously enhancing PLG

capacities. Such a partnership is dedicated to overcoming institutional, technical, and financial hurdles, gaining

momentum, and ensuring the successful execution of the decentralization initiative. The SOP's incremental and adaptive

methodology ensures that implementation aligns with federalism principles, improves risk management, fortifies policy

frameworks and execution capabilities, and fosters intergovernmental cooperation. Accordingly, the World Bank plans

to support this initiative through a SOP, with the current operation marking the commencement of the anticipated

program.

25.    Three-phased SOP. This SOP is being structured as a 10-year engagement through the currently estimated three

operations/phases at an estimated cost of US$840 million, with a proposed IDA financing envelope of US$700 million.

The development objective of this SOP is to enhance all-weather connectivity, efficiency, resilience and safety of the

PLRN, while strengthening the capacity of the participating governments. The lending instrument for Phase-I is

Investment Project Financing (IPF). Based on implementation experience and sectoral demands, other instruments such

as Program for Results or IPF with performance-based conditions will be considered for the subsequent phases. The SOP

provides the flexibility of adjusting the program framework, including phasing and their number, to stay on course to

achieve the development objective. The subsequent projects under SOP will be submitted to the Board for approval.

26.     The proposed three phases together form a comprehensive programmatic framework that will gradually enhance

the capacity of the road sector agencies by: (i) establishing a coherent and uniform sector foundation across provinces

and municipalities; (ii) improving governance and management capacity; (iii) strengthening their systems and business

processes, mainstreaming a culture of asset management and maintenance; and (iv) evolving them into infrastructure

service providers from the current culture of asset builders. Phase I will start with a manageable three provinces of the

seven provinces and four municipalities/local levels, which have been prioritized based on the number of unconnected

municipalities, impact on the population, and per-capita income. Gradual incorporation of provinces and municipalities

will be considered in the subsequent phases, depending upon their readiness, implementation performance, and

absorptive capacity.

E. Rationale for Bank Involvement and Role of Partners

42.    Through this engagement, the Bank aims to support the GoN in three primary areas: (i) operationalizing federalism

by transferring responsibility for the management of provincial and local roads to the respective PLGs; (ii) achieving

equitable access and connectivity for the rural population and improving service delivery of the existing PLRN; and (iii)

enhancing the governance and institutional capacity of the participating governments. The Program represents a

continued reengagement of the World Bank with the GoN on rural access after the SNRTP. It will be a logical extension

to the SNRTP to contribute to improving rural accessibility, service delivery, and sector modernization within the overall

ambit of federalism.

43.     The World Bank is well positioned to support the GoN’s vision based on its experience with similar projects in

other countries, including operations that (a) support the implementation of federalism and decentralization; (b) utilize

a programmatic approach on rural access projects, such as the two-decade long support to Pradhan Mantri Graham Sadak

Yojana (PMGSY) in India; (c) implement slope stabilization, bio-engineering and climate resilience measures in

infrastructure; (d) implement road safety measures; (e) support asset management with appropriate periodic and routine

maintenance activities; (f) finance resilient construction of bridges and culverts; and (g) promote institutional

strengthening and capacity enhancement of implementing agencies. The World Bank’s experience will facilitate the

inculcation of global best practices and cutting-edge solutions in the planning, design, implementation, and management

of programs. The World Bank’s support for the Program will provide a platform for long-term engagement with a steady

focus on supporting federalism, yet flexibility within the phases to address institutional and financial constraints. The

long-term approach will provide the opportunity for coherent sector modernization and bring uniformity among

provinces and municipalities. Accordingly, the World Bank also carried out a Federalism Institutional Assessment to

understand the gap in envisaged service delivery and current practices post federalism, including capacity levels, to better

inform the design of this Project.

44.    Role of partners. The PLRIP provides an opportunity for development partners (both multilateral and bilateral

agencies) to support the adoption of coherent and uniform approaches in all PLGs, with the requisite flexibility on a case-

(ii)   Paris Alignment

62.    The operation is aligned with the goals of the Paris Agreement on both mitigation and adaptation.

63.     Assessment and reduction of adaptation risks. The deteriorating road quality owing to the absence of all-weather

roads; inappropriate drains in poorly engineered roads leading to floods; haphazard construction leading to slope failures

and landslides/erosion; the consequent loss of animal and plant life; vulnerability of communities due to lack of all-

weather connectivity and access; disruption of services; and the loss of life, property, and livelihoods during extreme

weather conditions are identified as key climate risks. The Project will incorporate climate resilience and adaption

measures throughout the project cycle to reduce material physical climate risk. This includes measures from planning

and designs to operation and maintenance and includes contingency planning and provides support to increase

institutional capacity and coordination. Risks will be measured in terms of probability and impact and higher risk/impact

hazards will be prioritized. The activities include (a) systematic planning where prioritization and selection criteria for

investments will include vulnerability and hazard risk information through adoption of climate vulnerability and risk

assessment tools; (b) resilient measures incorporated in engineering design and construction (para 57); (c) operations

and maintenance - preparation of climate change and green growth strategy for provincial and local roads, guidelines for

sustainable periodic and routine maintenance of roads, and design guidelines for climate-resilient construction; (d)

disaster risk management including early warning systems, evacuation protocols, and restoration procedures; and (e)

capacity building. The Project adequately reduces the physical climate risks to its outcomes, and the Project’s climate

resilience and adaptation design considerations limit the exposure to a low level of residual risk. The integration of

climate resilience in design, construction, and maintenance is expected to mitigate landslide and flooding risks through

proper drainage and low-carbon construction techniques using resource-efficient and nature-based solutions.

64.     Assessment and reduction of mitigation risks. The Project is classified as ‘rural roads’ and is therefore considered

‘universally aligned’ with the Paris Agreement with respect to mitigation. Both local and provincial roads are considered

rural roads, connecting rural communities, and improving essential access for farmers to markets and rural residents to

schools, hospitals, and social services. Albeit of slightly higher volume, provincial roads have the same function as the

local roads. The Project includes mostly upgradation, but in some cases small road widening and construction on existing

paths to provide all weather services, which do not involve deforestation. These roads do not create barriers to non-

motorized modes as they allow for buses, bicycles, and two/three wheelers to operate and serve existing communities

with poor access. They also cannot be substituted by lower emission transport modes such as railways and waterways

due to geography (hilly and mountainous terrain), location disadvantages (land-locked), and low demand, and therefore

these alternatives lack economic feasibility. Vehicles are expected to increase with better roads compared to the ‘without

(iii)    Economic Analysis

65.     A detailed economic analysis was carried out for the six first-year investment roads under a ‘life-cycle cost’

approach for a 20-year analysis period, including all costs and quantifiable benefits. The primary benefits considered for

the economic analysis include: (a) reduction in vehicle operating costs; (b) travel time savings for vehicle users; (c)

reduction in carbon emissions from vehicles using the improved roads, estimated at 22,446 ton per million vehicle-km;

and (d) accident cost savings due to the implementation of road safety measures. Rural road improvements benefits,

including increased agricultural productivity and increased accessibility to employment, health, and educational benefits

(all to be considered under the ‘production surplus’ approach) will reflect in the increased trips considered under the

present ‘consumer surplus’ approach. Road specific traffic survey data from the DPRs were utilized in this regard. The

combined economic internal rate of returns (EIRRs) for the six first year investment roads was 14 percent. The sensitivity

analysis results also showed that all six first year investment roads were found to be economically viable under all

negative sensitivity scenarios, with EIRRs more than the minimum required 6 percent.

D. Environmental and Social

72.    Environmental risk is rated “Substantial” for the first phase of the SOP considering the key environmental risks

and impacts which includes the potential for works (that is, roads, bridges, and ancillary infrastructure) to (a) trigger or

exacerbate landslides due to the difficult terrain, fragile topography, and varied climatic conditions of the project area;

(b) cause air, noise, dust and water pollution, and vegetation loss and impact on habitats; and (c) material extraction

from quarry sites and borrow pits and its associated adverse impacts, and soil contamination from construction activities.

Considering the hilly terrain and fragile geology, construction areas will be prone to slope failures and landslides. Other

environmental concerns include OHS of workers and difficulties in managing solid waste and wastewater from labor

camps. Any activities with significant impact on biodiversity, forest natural resources, and critical habitat and others

requiring additional detailed studies, assessments, and timelines as identified by in-depth/ detailed screening, will not

be supported in this phase of SOP.



73.    The social risks related to the first phase of the SOP are assessed as ‘Substantial’. This first phase of the SOP will

support civil works (upgrading, maintenance, and construction) in rural settings and is expected to result in limited land

taking inducing minor economic impacts such as loss of crops and temporary impacts on business. Any activities

potentially involving significant adverse impacts such as physical displacement, loss of livelihood and impacts on

indigenous peoples (impacts on traditional land ownership and customary use, physical displacement, or impacts on

cultural heritage) will not be supported by the first phase of the SOP. The utilization of voluntary land donations for rural

roads has been an accepted practice under country systems in the past and agreed to be used as one of the land

acquisition options but not as the default option under the Project. The assessment for this Project also noted a growing

awareness among the project affected peoples about their ‘rights to not donate’. Requirements for consultation,

awareness, restrictions on type and magnitude of impact on land and assets, and use of third-party verification to ensure

transparent transaction will be enhanced to support VLD implementation. An RPF has been prepared describing

modalities for negotiation, formal procedures for expropriation with compensation and also includes an annex of good

practice protocols to guide VLD situations that might emerge under the Project. A Resettlement Action Plan (RAP) will be

prepared for each activity/subproject under the project for which the RPF requires such RAP. All costs for implementation

of RAP will be met through counterpart funding. Indigenous peoples are present in project areas and meaningful

consultation will be provided to ensure that significant adverse impacts are avoided. Without adequate consultation

there is also potential for exclusion of vulnerable and marginal groups from benefits offered by the Project such as

employment and improved road access. Besides these impacts and risks, the proposed construction activities around

settlement areas will impact the community health and safety due to noise, dust, and traffic issues related to construction

and disruption of road conditions. There is also potential for excluding vulnerable and marginalized groups from the

planning process, stakeholder engagements, opportunities, and benefits (employment, improved access and others)

offered by the Project. Construction activities may induce some labor influx, sexual exploitation and abuse and sexual

harassment (SEA/SH), accidents, OHS and community health and safety, and road traffic incidents. These issues are

addressed in subproject-level management tools prepared for the Project.



74.    The potential risks and impacts noted above are predictable, reversible, and can be addressed through

systematic planning and implementation that involves rigorous technical investigation, adoption of mitigation

hierarchy, alternative analysis, and design. Given that the designs and precise locations of sub-projects are not known,

the scope and depth of the Environment and Social Assessment at this stage are limited to the general project area, the

routine environmental and social impacts associated with the typology of investments, and concerns noted through

stakeholder consultations and engagement. Based on the assessments and consultations, a draft ESMF, RPF, Stakeholder

Engagement Plan (SEP), and Labor Management Procedures (LMP) have been prepared to mitigate and manage adverse

environment and social impacts whilst enhancing inclusive design and implementation of various sub-projects.

VI.     KEY RISKS



77.    The overall risk for the project is rated as Substantial based on the risks related to political, macroeconomic,

sector strategies, institutional, fiduciary, and environmental and social aspects.



   a. Political and Governance risks (Substantial). The federal system provides opportunities to decentralize

   development benefits and make service delivery more effective and accountable. Nevertheless, the risks of political

   uncertainty, jurisdictional overlap between the three tiers of government, and need for more clarity and coherence

   between policies and devolved powers will persist in the coming period. While the extent to which political risks can

   be mitigated through project-specific measures is limited, the World Bank will maintain continuity in policy dialogue

   with the government, and project design will include specific actions to build public support through broader

   stakeholder participation, strategic communication, and outreach.



   b. Macroeconomic risk (Substantial). The forecast is subject to multiple downside risks. Erratic monsoons and

   natural disasters could dampen agricultural growth. Political uncertainty could impede private investment and delay

   public capital expenditures. A renewed spike in commodity prices or continued food export bans by India would raise

   prices and widen the current account deficit. Higher inflation could keep policy rates elevated, increase domestic debt

   servicing costs, and drag on growth. Delays in pivoting fiscal revenues away from import taxation and towards

   domestic revenue sources would leave fiscal revenues vulnerable to swings in trade flows. The risks will be partly

   mitigated through the on-going IMF's Extended Credit Facility program and the current Fiscal Policy for Growth,

   Recovery and Resilience Development Policy Credit series.



   c. Sector Strategy (Substantial). At the sectoral level, policy framework is in a flux with the National Public Road Act,

   standards, and specifications for PLRN yet to be approved and the absence of a common approach to various planning

   tools. There is a lack of clarity in the ownership and hierarchy classification of the provincial and local roads due to the

   absence of the National Public Road Act. As an interim arrangement, Program level Road Classification Guidelines are

   under preparation to provide clear provincial and local road categorization based on functional classification and

   adjust to the new administrative structure in line with the constitutional mandate. DoLI has also initiated the

   preparation of key framework documents to facilitate uniformity for participating governments to be a part of the

   PLRIP. These include the PTMP/MTMP Guidelines; the ESMF; the MoU to which all participating governments will be

   signatories, standards specifications, and norms; and the POM. Further, to address the risk of abuse and misuse of

   support to the road safety enforcement activities for enforcing traffic safety rules, a risk mitigation plan will be

   prepared and implemented for procurement and use of traffic-enforcement goods and equipment. In addition, a

   security management plan shall also be included as part of site-specific ESMPs to address the risk of engaging security

   personnel to safeguard project workers, sites, assets, and activities, as required.



   d. Institutional Capacity (Substantial). At the provincial and local levels, the institutional base is currently weak, with

   new institutions still lacking the full quota of technical staff and provincial civil service commission legislations yet to

   be finalized (hiring is being initiated in some cases). The coordinating mechanisms across sectors and three tiers of

   government is still underdeveloped. Therefore, the program is being tailored to adjust and reorient implementation

   arrangements in keeping with the current capacities of the provincial and local levels, with Phase I focusing on

   establishing systems and processes. In addition, risks will be mitigated by ensuring adequate staffing of requisite

   expertise throughout the program lifecycle and supporting them through the engagement of qualified consulting firms

    e. Fiduciary (Substantial). The implementing agencies in the provinces are new entities established after the change

                          ANNEX 1: Implementation Arrangements and Support Plan



                                             COUNTRY: Nepal

                        Provincial and Local Roads Improvement Program- Phase 1



A.     Project Description:



1. Component 1: Enhancing efficiency and resilience of Provincial Roads and Bridges. This component will support

improving efficiency of service delivery and enhance climate resilience and safety of the existing provincial roads and

bridges through the following activities:

 1.1          Preparation (DPR/feasibility/ESMP/IEE), upgradation, rehabilitation, improvement, supervision and

 quality assurance of 200 km of selected provincial roads and bridges from existing poor/fair-weather to all-weather,

 resilient, resource-efficient, and safety standards, with carriageway matching traffic needs including connecting rural

 businesses/tourist hubs, agricultural markets, high-risk vulnerable areas, and healthcare and education facilities. The

 interventions will include (a) upgrade to existing road width, widening of formation width if required based on traffic

 parameters,        pavement          strengthening/widening,       geometric       improvements,         rehabilitation/

 improvement/construction of existing/new structures, drainage facilities, bypasses and realignment (if required),

 bridges within the road alignment, improvement of road geometry, junctions, and shoulder improvement; (b) site-

 specific resiliency features, as described in para 57 of the PAD, based on a combination of historical data and

 prediction models using secondary data; (c) resource-efficient construction, as described in para 57 of the PAD; (d)

 cost-effectiveness by undertaking value engineering assessment as part of the preparation of detailed engineering

 designs to optimize cost and achieve value for money including the optimization of pavement layers, materials and

 construction techniques; (e) road safety features- site-specific road safety measures such as road markings, signages,

 crash barriers, traffic/speed calming, raised parapets, delineators, topographic-specific designs of curves, gradient

 improvements, safe shoulders, hazard markings and so on; (f) approach roads to provide access to strategic

 government facilities which are located on either side of the road withing a specific distance (that is 500 mtrs -1000

 mtrs), this may include – prioritized access to senior secondary schools, health centers, government/ municipality

 building, market center, with appropriate standards. The approach roads will only be selectively constructed based

 on the available right of way (no additional land will be acquired for this purpose) with appropriate low-cost and

 resource-efficient design and construction standards including pedestrian and non-motorized access through reuse

 of waste-construction material or other alternate local material as available on the site; and (g) mandatory provision

 of five-years maintenance in the construction contracts in addition to the defect liability period. The construction

 and/or maintenance cost after the project closing date would be financed by counterpart contribution. The

 infrastructure upgrade will consider climate and disaster risk information in planning and engineering designs and

 construction to mitigate against flooding, landslides, and temperature. The CPCU will prepare separate guidelines

 and handbooks including DPR preparation template and review checklist based on the international best practices

 and country-specific standards, specifications, and norms.

 1.2         The sub-component will support periodic maintenance of 300 km of existing provincial roads and bridges

 in the participating provinces for the preservation of existing infrastructure and retrofitting of climate-resilience

 measures through performance-based maintenance contracts. In addition to regular activities under the periodic

 maintenance, the interventions will include providing drainage management, adequate size/capacity and balancing

 of culverts, adequate camber, and erosion control and slope stabilization measures, including bioengineering, low-

 cost surface sealings, grass seedling and so on. This will consider climate and disaster risk information in planning and

 engineering designs and construction to mitigate against flooding, landslides, and temperature. The CPCU will

prepare a separate bidding document for performance-based maintenance contracts based on international best

practices and country-specific standards, specifications, and norms. Additional adaption measures will be undertaken

such as early warning systems, incident detection and handling systems/ protocols, weather stations, road load

censors and other location-specific measures, as necessary.

1.3        This sub-component shall include routine and recurrent maintenance of 2,500 km of existing provincial

roads and bridges to enhance their durability and resilience to climate and geo-physical disasters through community

led RMGs. In addition to routine maintenance activities, this shall include providing revised maintenance schedules

with increased frequency of cleaning and maintenance of drainage systems, removal of storm damage, cleaning of

roads, pruning of bushes and snow and ice removal, debris removal and so on. Additional adaption measures will be

undertaken such as early warning systems, incident deduction and handling systems/ protocols, weather stations,

road load censors and other location-specific measures as necessary. Similar to the SNRTP, RMGs will be mobilized

by IDOs through contracts in consultation with the concerned local governments/ municipalities which include the

minimum 50 percent participation of women, who shall be provided equipment and training support and monthly

maintenance schedule for better planning and flexibility to undertake works. The training shall cover training on

routine maintenance activities, use of equipment, first aid, financial literacy, maintenance skills, bioengineering,

plantations and sexual exploitation and abuse and other benefits (childcare facilities and free transportation to work)

creating an enabling environment, especially for women to work. To operationalize this and ensure sustainability of

RMGs beyond the project, the CPCU will prepare separate guidelines in this regard based on the SNRTP and other

projects experience in Nepal and international best practices and also institutionalize these bodies as part of its

structure with allocated budget.

1.4         The sub-component shall support undertaking pilot investment on development of community-led

complementary/associated climate-smart infrastructure facilities along the roadside to enhance agriculture supply-

chain efficiency for agricultural produce, MSMEs, and industries in the zone of influence of the area. The interventions

could include construction of market sheds, market centers, warehouses, cold storage, collection/ processing/

packaging centres, public toilets, bus-bys, bus stations, and aggregation centers, using energy and resource-efficient

materials and techniques such as solar lighting, and construction through reuse of waste material, debris/hill-cutting

material and so on. The specific interventions will vary from road-to road and will be identified through demand

assessment surveys to be undertaken by the DPR consultants in the zone of influence area of the road (3-5 km of the

corridor of impact). The proposal for such interventions would be included in the DPR along with cost estimates and

accordingly, be included in the bidding document. The CPCU will issue detailed program specific guidelines for this

purpose. Priority will be given to women-led or women-managed user committees to develop complimentary

infrastructure that cater to the specific needs of women.



2. Component 2: Enhancing all-weather connectivity and access to Municipalities: This component will support the

objective of reducing regional imbalances and building resilience of unconnected communities/ municipalities by

providing equitable all-weather and safe access to services and markets through construction of local roads and

bridges. It will include the following subcomponents:

2.1         This sub-component specifically addresses the needs of unconnected very remote/ remote

municipalities/local level centers and villages through the preparation (DPR/feasibility/ESMP/IEE) construction and

upgradation, supervision, and quality assurance of 100 km local roads and bridges to ensure they are all-weather,

resilient, and resource-efficient and meet required safety standards and include carriageway matching traffic needs

along with approach roads and five-year post construction routine maintenance built into the construction contract.

The construction and/or maintenance cost after the project closing date would be financed by counterpart

contribution. The specific interventions are similar to the activities included in Subcomponent 1.1 above.

2.2        The sub-component will support undertaking pilots to improve access in very remote/remote locations

  through non-motorized transport and alternative means of access, thereby mitigating GHG in the rural transport

  sector. In very remote mountainous/hilly regions, where, providing immediate connectivity/ access through

  construction of roads/ bridges could be difficult immediately due to geological / geographic constraints, site-specific

  alternate means of access through non-motorized pedestrian/ walking facilities, ropeways, or timber or other types

  of bridges will be considered.



 3. Component 3: Strengthening governance system and capacity development. This component will support laying

 a strong sector foundation for provincial and local roads by improving the governance systems and strengthening the

 capacity of the participating governments. The focus is to support a harmonized and consistent approach of

 development and management of the sector among provinces and municipalities. The activities will include the

 following:

3.1 Business systems and processes: This sub-component will support the preparation, adoption and implementation

    of an ISMP based on an as-is assessment of the existing organizations, systems, processes, and procedures at all

    three tiers of government. The ISMP will focus on systematic upgrade, standardization, and digitization of the core

    business processes and procedures, which will be implemented and made functional over the short, medium, and

    long-term under the SOP. The ISMP will improve governance and modernize the working of the road agencies and

    assist in achieving greater operational efficiency and make them modern, well-performing institutions. The impact

    of these interventions is expected to vary across provinces and institutions (including federal, provincial, and local

    levels) based on their responsiveness to adoption. As part of this plan, the Government will institutionalize equal

    pay for equal work, introduce flexible working hours and childcare support, and create a safe workplace through

    implementing anti-sexual harassment policies.

3.2 Program management: This will support development and implementation of an online PMMS for day-to-day

    monitoring, management, supervision, and reporting (gradually accounting) at the program level (provision for all

    provinces) in the CPCU. In addition, a province-level road asset management system will be developed and

    implemented by PPMUs in each province to manage the network under the jurisdiction of the respective works

    ministry. Along with standard modules of the RAMS, this system shall also have an accident reporting module and

    a risk and hazard database to undertake predictive prioritization and investments.

3.3 Implementation support: This sub-component will provide implementation support to all three tiers, that is,

    CPCU/DoLI, provincial governments/PPMUs/IDOs, and local governments/LLTUs. Specifically, it shall include (a)

    harmonization of climate-resilient road and bridge standards, specifications, and norms at the program level

    through the CPCU/DoLI; (b) development of PTMP/MTMPs on the basis on the guidelines prepared by the

    CPCU/DoLI; (c) gender-disaggregated road-user satisfaction surveys; (d) outcome monitoring study to measure

    the outcomes of the project activities and their contribution towards poverty reduction, employment, income,

    health, education, resilience and achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals with attention given to

    differential impacts including gender; and (e) project supervision, quality assurance (for activities other than

    Subcomponents 1.1 and 2.1, where cost is included in the respective sub-component), technical audit, and M&E

    as well as incremental operating costs.

3.4 Capacity building: This sub-component shall support training and capacity building of DoLI, provincial

    governments and local levels, through the development and implementation of a capacity building strategy (CBS)

    and an implementation plan to be prepared at the program level covering all anticipated provinces and

    municipalities to be implemented over the short, medium, and long-term and environment and social capacity

    assessment. It should also include establishment of technical centers of excellence at federal level under DoLI for

    supporting trainings, capacity building, skill development, research programs, standards/specifications, and

    norms for building a climate-and disaster-resilient provincial and local roads and bridges network.

3.5 Climate resilience: The sub-component will support carrying out of hazard risk assessments on existing all-

    weather roads and bridges in the participating provinces and adoption and implementation of a climate resilience

    and green growth strategy for provincial and local road network including (a) undertaking vulnerability and hazard

    risk assessment of about 500 km of roads and bridges to inform/ prioritize risk-based project selection and

    management of road assets; (b) adoption of climate vulnerability and risk assessment tool and the climate and

    green growth strategy along with the implementation plan; (c) design guidelines for climate-resilient roads and

    bridges; and (d) capacity building of the participating governments. These activities will be along with other

    activities included in the respective components and described in para 35 and 59 in the main section of the PAD.

3.6 Rural transportation services: This sub-component shall support undertaking assessment of gaps and developing

    a program for strengthening low-emission, cost-effective and safe rural passenger and freight transportation

    services, including electric vehicles. The studies will address the specific travel needs of women and poorer people

    who generally have low vehicle ownership and for whom the critical issue is that new roads should facilitate

    improved transport services. This sub-component will, therefore, also seek to develop a program for

    strengthening the public rural transport services including institutional and policy framework, encourage the

    participation of local transport operators including women’s transport groups, and promote the use of clean fuel

    including electric vehicles. Studies related to improved trade and logistics in rural areas will identify the

    complementary interventions to maximize the positive impact of rural roads on agricultural and industrial supply

    chains for improving freight efficiency. It will also prepare measures to integrate women, children, the elderly,

    and other vulnerable groups in the provision of rural transportation services and in community awareness

    programs on road safety and sexual harassment in public transport.



 4. Component 4: Road safety management. This component will include support for building capacity of provincial

 and local road agencies for road safety management. In particular, it supports the following:

      4.1    PPMUs, in the three participating provinces, to undertake safety audit of selected 100 km and implement

             the audit findings of prioritized 30 km in the participating provinces and municipalities.

      4.2    PPMUs, to prepare and adopt province-level road safety action plans (in the participating provinces) including

             adopting measures that address safety risk due to climate-related hazards.

      4.3    CPCU, to update road design standards/ manual for integrating road safety engineering measures driven by

             predictive approach to climate and disaster resilience for low volume roads.

      4.4    CPCU, to prepare community and work zone safety guidelines to be adopted at the program level.

      4.5    CPCU, to conduct training of trainers and road safety auditors.

      4.6    PPMUs, along with the respective division of the MoPID, in each participating province, to strengthen road

             safety enforcement capacity and post-crash care through equipment and training support, as necessary.

 5. Component 5: Contingent emergency response (CERC). Provision of immediate response to an eligible crisis or

 emergency, as needed.

11. PPMUs and LLTUs will be established at the provincial and local levels, respectively. The PPMUs and LLTUs shall

be exclusively established in each participating province and local levels. PPMUs shall be headed by a project director,

assisted by project coordinators. A team of specialists (either individuals or firm) shall support the PPMU as Project

Management Consultants with expertise in procurement, environmental and social, FM, and project management.

Responsibilities of the PPMU include planning, preparation, and finalization of DPRs; management of land acquisition

and right-of-way (with the support from the local levels); procurement of works for provincial and local roads;

implementation of the ESMF; setting up of accounting system and maintenance of records as per provisions in the

POM; fund flow administration and management; supervision and monitoring reports; facilitating of approval for

variation orders and extension of time; and preparation of monthly, quarterly, and annual progress reports as

applicable. The PPMU will rely on the respective field units, that is, IDOs, for undertaking actual procurement of works

and day-to-day contract management and FM. The IDO team shall consist of a team of engineers and an accounts

officer, supported by a team of surveyors, quality control expert, supervisors, and lab technicians.



12. Local governments, through the respective municipalities participating in the PLRIP, will be supported by LLTUs

created specifically for the PLRIP for facilitating and supporting the PPMUs and IDOs in the implementation of local

road projects and maintenance. The LLTU will comprise technical staff, an accounts officer, and other staff, as

necessary. LLTUs will facilitate and support the PPMU in planning, preparation, consultations, finalization of designs,

management of environmental and social impacts including land taking, monitoring, supervision, and grievance

redress. This will not only ensure activities are undertaken through a consultative process and build capacity of the

LLTUs. This arrangement may evolve during the subsequent phases of the Program provided sufficient capacity exists.

The LLTUs’ expenses in attending meetings at the PPMUs/IDOs will be reimbursed by the PPMU to the LLTUs, based

on actual cost, as per the prevailing practice. LLTUs will not be responsible for direct procurement and implementation

of any activity.

14. Quality assurance. A three-tier quality assurance mechanism is envisaged under the PLRIP. The first tier is the in-

house quality control system of the IDOs and their units, and the second tier will be the quality assurance system of

the PPMUs of the provincial governments. Therefore, the provincial governments would be responsible for the first

two tiers of the quality management structure. The third tier is envisaged as a quality management mechanism to be

operationalized by the CPCU at the federal level for external quality monitoring and verification. Accordingly, a panel

of independent federal quality monitors will be engaged by the CPCU at the federal level and a panel of provincial

quality monitors will be engaged by the PPMUs in each of the participating provinces to support the three-tier quality

assurance mechanism. The details of this three-tier mechanism along with the roles, responsibilities, and process to

be adopted are elaborated in the POM. The CPCU shall also prepare detailed quality assurance handbooks for adoption

at the Program level.



15. Capacity building. Based on the capacity needs assessment, the CPCU will organize and manage training to be

(a) orientation training to relevant staff across three tiers on the World Bank ESF and its implementation modality

(screening, scoping, and so on); (b) training on land acquisition, voluntary land donation, involuntary resettlement,

indigenous people, vulnerable and disadvantaged people, free and prior informed consent (FPIC), Environmental and

Social Impact Assessment, and RAP, procurement and FM arrangements; and (c) occupational health and safety,

community health and safety, labor practices, cultural heritage management, traffic and road safety, exposure visits

to wildlife areas, natural habitat and biodiversity training course, consultant procurement and contract management,

and management of construction Impacts

C. Financial Management Assessment



17. The FM assessment was conducted in the three tiers of the government. The FM arrangements are based on the

FM assessment and consultations conducted in September, 2023.



18. Institutional arrangement and staffing. The Government will assign a dedicated finance officer for the federal

office and each province office. The CPCU will ensure that implementation agencies at the PPMU and respective

province IDOs have a qualified finance officer designated by the Government before assigning budget to the

implementation agencies. In addition, the CPCU and each PPMU will have an FM consultant to support the finance

officer. As a number of cost centers at the province levels have limited or no experience of World Bank-funded

projects, the Project will have coordination complexities. Thus, though each province’s implementation agencies will

also be responsible to maintain effective FM arrangements and practices at their respective offices, the CPCU

established in DoLI will be responsible for overall FM arrangements under the Project. Given the autonomy of each

tier of government, there is no current reporting mechanism for the CPCU to obtain annual plans and reports from

provincial implementation agencies and local levels. The MoU with the subnational level will include an FM section

where planning, budgeting, reporting, and supervision requirements under the Project and coordination among the

three tiers of the government and the role of the CPCU, PPMUs, and LLTUs will be agreed. The CPCU will organize FM

trainings under the project to build capacity of the project FM team at the subnational level.



19. Planning and budgeting. Each implementing agency, in consultation with the PPMU, will prepare the AWpB and

forward it to the CPCU through the respective PPMUs. The respective PPMUs will also submit the activity-wise AWpB

to their respective Ministry of Economic Affairs and Planning for inclusion in the budget and approval by the Province





                                                              46

  The World Bank

Assembly. The CPCU will consolidate the project AWpB (along with breakdown for each implementing agency) and

submit it to the Ministry of Finance to ensure allocation of sufficient budget for the project. The federal government

will allocate budget to the CPCU and to each province through conditional grants, under the separate budget code

allotted to the PLRIP. The CPCU will ensure that the budget for the fiscal year is adequately and correctly prepared

and reflected in the respective budgetary system including required counterpart funds from GoN sources. Any

counterpart funding from local governments will also be captured in the AWpB and recorded in their annual budget.

The CPCU will share a copy of the AwPB by April every year with the World Bank for information and consensus. The

after presentation of the budget in the Parliament.



20. Accounting and reporting. The Project has been allotted a separate budget code. Each federal and province

implementing agency will maintain project accounts in the Computerized Government Accounting System (CGAS) and

the local government’s counterpart financing in the Subnational Treasury Regulatory Application (SuTRA). The

government Chart of Accounts (2074), approved by the Auditor General of Nepal, is used for budgeting and accounting

by all levels of government. The IDA share of the project expenses will be pre-financed by the GoN through the

Government’s consolidated fund. All cost centers will maintain a separate sub-ledger to record and report budget and

expenditures by category as prescribed in the POM based on the Financing Agreement and by component and

subcomponent (activity) in the respecting accounting software based on the activities approved in the AWpB and

aligned with the component and subcomponent mentioned in the Financing Agreement. The CPCU will maintain the

IDA credit register and the Withdrawal Monitoring Register under the Project. The CPCU will prepare IUFRs in the

format agreed with the World Bank, consolidating information from all cost centers under the Project. Such IUFRs will

be submitted to the World Bank on quarterly basis, within 45 days from the end of the quarter. The CPCU is to agree

with all implementing agencies at the federal and province levels to provide them the reports within 30 days from the

end of each quarter or a mutually agreed timeline for consolidation.



21. Internal control. The GoN’s Financial Procedure and Fiscal Accountability Act (FPFAA), 2019; Financial Procedure

and Fiscal Accountability Regulations, 2021; and the respective Provincial Financial Procedure Acts will govern the

project for FM procedures. The GoN has clear legal provisions and procedures in place to ensure budget commitment

control as depicted in the Public Expenditure and Financial Accountability (PEFA) assessment conducted in FY13/14.

The District Treasury Controller Offices (DTCOs) and Provincial Financial Comptroller Offices (PFCOs) will make

payments only after ensuring adequate budget and sources of funds in conformity with the budget release

procedures. Expenditures are deemed to be incurred only if there is an approved budget and funds to cover the eligible

expenditure. Also, the POM should detail the required FM arrangements under the project and be adopted within two

months of project effectiveness in the participating provinces.



22. Internal audit. Under the Project, each implementing agency will conduct internal audit as prescribed in Section

33 (4) of the FPFAA. The CPCU will maintain a matrix of audit observations and action plan for the project. The CPCU

and all implementing agencies will maintain records of internal audit and external audit observations, action plan,

responsible agencies/officials, and timeline for settlement for monitoring the resolution of the project audit

observations.



23. External audit. Given the number of implementing agencies and the need for Office of Auditor General to conduct

and consolidate reports, the external audit report timeline has been extended, from six to nine months after the end

of the fiscal year, to maintain the report’s timeliness. There are no overdue audits from the implementing ministry.

The follow-up mechanism on the audit observations has been observed to be on ad hoc basis in implementing

agencies. Though Karnali Province has started a portal (beruji.karnali.gov.np) to track audit irregularities, the tracking

is mostly done for recoverable amount only, and tracking practices are not consistent for other provinces and

also be provided from the World Bank.

will include planning, budgeting, accounting, funds flow, and reporting arrangements; (b) the POM will include details

of the required FM arrangements and will be finalized within one month of effective date of the project; (c) the CPCU

AWpBs with the World Bank by April every year; and (d) the CPCU will obtain the internal and external audit report

from all implementing agencies, maintain a matrix of project audit observations and action plan, and follow up to

track their resolutions.

goods, works, and non-consulting services, when the borrower uses its own national open competitive procurement

arrangements as set forth in national Procurement Act and Regulations 2007 and revisions thereof, such arrangements

shall be subject to paragraph 5.4 of the Procurement Regulations and the following conditions:

 (a) Model bidding documents, including contract conditions agreed with the World Bank (as amended from time

       to time), shall be used. Bidding documents shall be made available by mail or in person or through Electronic

       Government Procurement portal to all who are willing to pay the required fee.

(b)   Eligibility to participate in a procurement process for and to be awarded the World Bank-financed contracts

      shall be as defined under the paragraph ‘Eligibility’ of Section III - Governance of the Procurement Regulations.

 E. Strategy and Approach for Implementation Support



29. The World Bank will support project implementation based on the risks identified and the mitigation measures

proposed in the risk section. The World Bank will provide implementation support through biannual implementation

support missions and thematic implementation support missions as well as technical visits, regular discussions with

the CPCU and PPMUs, IDOs, and LLTUs to facilitate project implementation, special thematic assessments, discussions

with local communities, training workshops, and resolving of any project implementation issues by closely working

with all stakeholder departments in between the six-monthly missions. To promote a collaborative approach, the

World Bank and ADB will (a) undertake joint tripartite portfolio review meetings and pipeline discussions with the

Government; (b) undertake joint implementation supervision missions in one key province (Karnali) to the extent

possible at least once a year; (c) invite other relevant stakeholder and partners for cross-learning and experience-

sharing during missions; and (d) develop a common communication strategy to highlight joint support as a unified

platform to support the Government in operationalizing federalism.



30. The World Bank shall provide required technical and implementation support through a core team of sector

specialists and experts, who will be complemented by consultants/experts from outside or within the country as

required on all key functional areas: technical, fiduciary management, safeguards and project management, and so

on. The implementation support will include reviewing documents, providing detailed guidance, reviewing or

monitoring procurement and implementation progress, reviewing contract management activities, and identifying

and providing capacity building or training needs, as required from time to time including knowledge exchange

programs and exposure visits. A project orientation will be provided at the start of the Project to all project-related

staff, including project directors/coordinators, procurement officials and consultants, and FM officials and

consultants.



31. A midterm review will be conducted around 30 months from credit effectiveness. The review would assess overall

implementation performance and progress toward the achievement of the PDO.
