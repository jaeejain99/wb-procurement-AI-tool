I.     PROGRAM CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES



1.     This implementation Completion and Results (ICR) report assesses the achievement of the First and Second

Programmatic Fiscal and Public Financial Management (FPFM) Development Policy Credit (DPC) (IDA 61950 and IDA

64400) to Nepal. This series sought to support the Government of Nepal (GoN) to implement reforms aimed at moving

towards fiscal federalism and strengthening PFM at all levels of government. The first (FPFM-DPC1) credit of SDR137.3

million (equivalent to US$200 million) was approved by the Board on February 20, 2018 and the second (FPFM-DPC2)

of SDR 72.2 million (equivalent to US$100 million) was approved by the Board on May 10, 2019.



 A. Context at Appraisal



 Overall Context



 2.     Prior to the appraisal of this Credit, Nepal was hit thrice in succession by large exogenous shocks in 2015, 2016

 and 2017. The earthquake in April 2015 led to the loss of 9,000 lives and the damage of more than the one-third of the

 FY15 GDP1. This was followed by a near-complete disruption of trade because of India’s blockade2 for four months

 that generated acute shortages of fuel, raw materials, and essential supplies as well as a precipitous fall in growth rate

 to less than 1 percent in FY16. A flood in August 2017 affected more than half the districts in the country, with the

 Terai region being the most severely affected, and resulted in damage of 2.8 percent of FY18 GDP 3.



 3.     The shift from a centralized government to a three-tier federal structure also increased public spending.

 Limited state presence in the provision of adequate basic services in education, health, drinking water and connectivity

 – roads, electricity and telecommunication – were recorded grievances during the conflict period (1996-2006). Nepal

 thus decided to transition to federalism through the comprehensive peace accord (November 21, 2006) to deliver on

 greater inclusion and meeting the needs of all its people. Elections to federal, provincial and local levels were

 completed in 2017. With elected officials taking their positions in the 7 provincial governments (PGs) and the 753 local

 governments (LGs), additions to staff and facilities were needed at subnational levels. The devolution of civil

 administration required a reallocation of existing government employees across the three tiers as well as new

 recruitment for additional staff, especially at the local level. The devolution also involved the fiscal transfer in the form

 of grants from federal to Province and Local governments (PLGs) as well from PGs to LGs from FY18. This resulted in a

 one-time jump in recurrent expenditure as a share of GDP by 3.3 percentage points in FY18 4. Revenue sharing from

 federal to PLGs as well from PGs to LGs also started from FY19.



 4.    The three exogenous shocks and the incremental costs of federalism affected the country’s fiscal and external

 balances, adversely. The need to repair and rebuild infrastructure and other assets that were damaged or destroyed

 by the earthquake and/or the flood also called for a jump in investment and a surge in imports. Gross fixed capital

 formation as a share of GDP rose sharply between FY16 and FY19 (Table 1). On the fiscal side revenue remained

 buoyant but total public spending rose much more rapidly from 18.9 percent in FY16 to 27.3 percent of GDP in FY19,

 Structural Context



5.      The new constitution envisaged a shift in the responsibility for delivery of public services to sub-national

levels, requiring devolution of functions, functionaries, and funds. The government sought to implement political,

administrative, and fiscal devolution in parallel, which was no doubt a momentous task. At the time of appraisal, political

devolution had progressed a good deal with elected officials in their respective positions in each of the three tiers of

government. Administrative devolution was also underway with the passage of the Employees Adjustment Act in 2017

whereby 100,000 existing civil servants were reallocated among the three tiers though the actual relocation of

personnel and their families took a couple of years; the recruitment of additional staff needed by LGs had not yet

started. In 2019, it was estimated that less than half of the LG staffing needs were met 5. Sub-national governments

needed reliable and regular access to revenue to execute their spending responsibilities as well as an effective PFM

system to ensure that their spending could be financed adequately, executed efficiently and subjected to proper

scrutiny and accountability. It was time for fiscal devolution to catch up with political and administrative devolution.



6.     The need to establish adequate inter-governmental institutions for fiscal devolution became urgent. The

process of designing inter-governmental fiscal arrangements, developing consensus on such design, enacting umbrella

laws, and operationalizing had to begin. The Constitution provided the broad architecture of fiscal federalism. But many

of the details of the legal framework for intergovernmental fiscal relations, like the unbundling of expenditure

responsibilities, the sharing of federal revenues, the assigning of revenue-raising powers, the adoption of criteria for

various grants, and transfers, the limits of subnational borrowing and so on, had to be agreed, legislated and

implemented.



7.      This devolution of expenditure and revenue required an adequate PFM system at the provincial and local

levels. While a PFM framework and a PFM Information System (PFMIS) existed at the federal level, the same was not

true for newly-created subnational governments. The latter needed new or parallel rules and procedures for

formulating, executing and reporting on budgets, for maintaining appropriate accounts of transactions and for

implementing controls and audits, including relevant legislation. The PFMIS in effect at the federal level had to be

implemented and made operational in all PGs and LGs so that it could deliver reliable and timely fiscal information for

all levels of government.



 Rationale for Bank Involvement



8.     There was thus a strong rationale for Bank support to the government’s move towards fiscal federalism and

improved PFM in each of the three tiers. One of the three key areas of focus in the World Bank’s Country Partnership

Framework has been the strengthening of Nepal’s public institutions. This area includes support for fiscal devolution,

the improvement of PFM in all three levels of government and the restructuring of civil service administration, all

essential for Nepal’s transition to a federal state. Accordingly, the World Bank has been providing analytical work in the

relevant aspects of this focus area, development policy credits (DPCs) in support of institutional reforms, and the longer-

term technical assistance investment project 6 to train and build the capacity of subnational staff so that they can

implement and sustain those reforms.



9.     The reforms in fiscal devolution and PFM, being supported by the first and second FPFM-DPCs are anchored

in the government’s own development strategy, specifically its three-year periodic plan 7 and its 2030 longer-term

Strategy. The outcomes for the reform supported by the programmatic series consisted mainly of the passage of six

umbrella laws and their operationalization as manifest in the achievement of specific targets for each of the 13 results

indicators (RIs). Achievement of those targets would show that numerous fiscal operations were actually completed as

per those laws in respect of raising revenue, sharing revenue, making fiscal transfers, preparing budgets, executing

expenditures, reporting of actual spending and revenue-collection, completing audits and so on.

17. PA#5 in FPFM-DPC2 was changed requiring PGs to adopt gender-responsive-budgeting (GRB) instead of

establishing a response-and-support system for gender-based-violence as per trigger#4. The action to establish a

response-and-support system for gender-based-violence was achieved and to show further progress and to align the

prior action more closely with the fiscal agenda, PA#5 was replaced with the adoption of GRB. The adoption of GRB at

the federal level earlier had highlighted its efficacy in addressing gender-inequality. With fiscal devolution underway,

implementing the same at subnational levels was assessed to be both appropriate and timely. With most of the budget

being devolved, the goal of reducing gender-inequality through the adoption of GRB guideline at provincial level

contributed towards promoting women’s empowerment and gender equality by influencing the planning and budgeting

process to ensure more equitable distribution of public finances for women and men.

  A. Relevance of Prior Actions



      Rating: Satisfactory



20. The relevance of prior actions is rated satisfactory. The two PDOs of the FPFM-DPC series and the program of prior

actions designed to achieve those two PDOs, were relevant. The PDOs were aligned fully with the government’s goal of

fiscal devolution and of improving PFM at all levels and were anchored firmly in the government’s three-year periodic

plan and the 2030 longer-term Strategy. Achieving both goals was necessary if responsibility for delivering basic public

services was to be shifted to subnational governments as envisaged by the 2015 Constitution.



       Pillar 1: Establish Framework to Move towards Fiscal Federalism



21. This pillar supported the government’s efforts to design, develop, and enact the umbrella laws that were

necessary mainly for fiscal devolution. Those laws provided the legal basis for assigning expenditure responsibilities

and revenue-raising powers to each of the three tiers of government as well as for a system of intergovernmental grants

and revenue-sharing as well as of subnational borrowing, all key elements of fiscal federalism. There were nine prior

actions (PAs) in all for this pillar (across the two operations), with four PAs in the first FPFM-DPC and five in the second.

22. PA#1 in FPFM-DPC1 and PAs#1 and #2 in FPFM-DPC2 sought the approval and the passage of Intergovernmental

Fiscal Arrangement (IFA) Act 2017, of the Federal Fiscal Procedures and Financial Responsibility (FFPFR) Act 9 2019, as

well as amendments to the former and the Local Government Operations (LGO) Act to reassign several local taxes,

respectively. The IFA law provided the legal framework under which subnational governments obtained their

expenditure-responsibilities, revenue-raising powers, intergovernmental transfers and the authority to borrow. It also

established an intergovernmental fiscal council, charged with coordinating fiscal relations among the three tiers of

government. The FFPFR law, in line with international best practice, consolidated all functional areas of budgeting 10

processes and procedures under a single law, which would be a significant improvement over the ad-hoc Financial

Directives that were being used before the law was enacted. The implementing regulation of the FFPFR was also

approved and published in the Gazette in December 2020. The amendments to the IFA Act 2017 and the LGO Act 2017

through the Finance Act 2018 clarified the allocation of revenues for six taxes 11 which were deemed concurrent (i.e,

shared by Provinces and local governments) as per the Constitution.



23. PA# 2 in FPFM-DPC1 and PA#3 in FPFM-DPC2 sought the enactment of the LGO Act 2017 and the establishment

and actual operation of local consolidated funds as well as the delegation of spending-authority to the Chief

Administrative Officer, the latter was to be done by at least a quarter of all LGs. The LGO Act 12 mandated the creation

of local consolidated funds with stipulations about which deposits qualified for the fund as well as about the institutional

processes that ought to be used by LGs for tax policy, budget prioritization, expenditure ceilings, spending authority and

so on. A functional local consolidated fund enabled the municipalities to manage fiscal affairs for both revenues and

expenditures, bringing an end to the existing practice of off-budget activities that limited the accountability of funds

use.



24. PA#3 in FPFM-DPC1 and PA#4 in FPFM-DPC2 sought the enactment of the National Natural Resources and Fiscal

Commission (NNRFC) Act 2017 as well as making the NNRFC fully operational. The latter was to be manifested by the

Commission actually providing recommendation on the size of equalization and the basis of conditional grants for

III. OTHER OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS



   A. Poverty, Gender and Social Impacts



43. The PDOs of the FPFM-DPC series are aligned with the twin goals of ending extreme poverty and boosting

shared prosperity but actual impact on poverty have not been measured. Fiscal devolution was aimed at shifting more

public resources to local levels and improved public financial management was meant to ensure that those resources

were used effectively and accountably. The large shift in public resources to subnational governments is expected to

favor better social services at the local level where poverty rates are typically higher. For example, during the COVID-19

pandemic, local governments led the frontline response including providing quarantine and isolation facilities. This is

despite the limited human resources and physical infrastructure. The provincial and local governments also contributed

significantly to the government’s plan of vaccinating 72 percent of eligible population by mid-April 2022.Also, given that

a disproportionate share of the poor lives in areas far from the capital, such a redistribution of resources away from the

center should be favorable for the country’s poor. Expenditure on services by LGs are more likely to be in line with local

requirements because they have better information on local preferences; also locally elected politicians have greater

incentive to ensure better delivery of basic health, education and infrastructure services to their voters. The poverty

and social impact of the program is likely to be favorable at best and neutral at worst, depending on the extent of local

capacity challenges 36.



44. Some of the prior actions in the FPFM-DPC series have contributed directly to addressing gender disparities.

One of those actions adopted an integrated platform for gender-based violence (GBV) response system to provide multi-

sectoral response service to GBV survivors through a 24-hour helpline service and other referral services such as shelter,

legal aid, psychosocial support 37. The other action required that GRB be part and parcel of fiscal devolution, such that

subnational governments prepare and execute budgets in a way that allocates resources for policies/investments aimed

at reducing gender-inequalities. While there is no impact assessment, the specific reforms cited above are designed to

have a favorable impact on gender-equity in the medium to long run.

              C. Institutional Change/Strengthening



47. The FPFM-DPC series supported reforms that contributed to substantial institutional change and strengthening

in two areas: fiscal devolution and PFM. Institutional changes related to fiscal devolution were momentous as it implied

a move from a centralized government to a three-tier federal one with 7 provincial and 753 local governments. This had

to be accompanied by strengthening of subnational institutions for PFM to ensure that expenditures at the local level

are funded adequately, executed efficiently and subject to proper accountability. To effect this change and strengthen

institutions six umbrella laws were passed between 2017 and 2019. In addition, these institutional reforms were

supported by technical assistance projects that provided training and capacity building for subnational staff as well as

equipment for the automated PFMIS for local governments.

V. RISK TO SUSTAINABILITY OF DEVELOPMENT OUTCOMES



52. The development outcomes of the FPFM-DPC series are likely to be sustained, though risks remain. Strong

nationwide-consensus on the goals of fiscal devolution and improved PFM as well as the constitutional mandate for

subnational delivery of basic public services enhances sustainability as they demonstrate a high level of commitment.

The results of reforms supported by this series are early steps in the long road to achieving fiscal devolution and an

effective PFM system which make continued institutional reforms important for sustainability. The Constitution and the

recently passed laws (i.e. IFA, LGO, NNRFC and FFPFR) have established the legislative framework for fiscal federalism

and improved PFM systems aligned to the federal context. The World Bank remains committed to continued support in

these areas; together with other development partners the Bank has already committed longer-term support for this

purpose.



53. The risk to sustainability stems from inadequate staffing of subnational governments and insufficient training

and capacity-building of existing staff. This risk has increased over the last two years as the PGs and LGs remain unable

to recruit employees that they need to discharge their administrative and fiscal roles. The FCNA highlighted in 2019 that

50 percent of the staffing needs of PGs and LGs were not met at that time. Since then there has been no recruitment of

additional PG and LG staff, even as an increasing share of spending is being devolved. The Federal Civil Service Bill, which

provides for recruitment by each tier of government, has not yet been enacted. This has prevented the recently formed

Provincial Civil Service Commission from performing its role and recruiting regular staff; LGs can only recruit staff at

lower levels of administration on short term contracts. This state of affairs risks jeopardizing continued fiscal devolution

and effective public financial management.



54. The risk to sustainability also arises from the low share of own-source revenue in the total revenue. Although

the own source revenue results indicator was achieved, the baseline and the actual share of own source revenue to the

overall revenue is very low. The information advantage PLGs exploit to deliver services to local tastes and preferences

cannot only be sustained by transfers from the central government. They should in time develop the ability to raise a

significant amount of revenue at the local level to finance their mandates.



VI. LESSONS AND NEXT PHASE

   A. Lessons Learned



55.   The following lessons were learned from this programmatic FPFM-DPC series:



56. Lesson 1: Selecting reform measures that are based on sound analytic work whose findings had been discussed

with development partners, ensures continued support of development partners in their implementation. This was







                                                                                                               Page 19 of 34

         The World Bank

         Programmatic Fiscal and Public Financial Management Development Policy Credit ( II ) (P168869)







particularly important for a lower middle-income country like Nepal given its reliance on technical and financial support

from development partners over the long-haul 40.



57. Lesson 2: The availability of substantial and sustained technical assistance can be decisive in ensuring successful

implementation of institutional reforms that take longer to implement and depend on staff capacity. This was more

so in the case of Nepal’s FPFM-DPCs focused on fiscal devolution and PFM strengthening that involved 753 newly-

formed local governments, with a need for continuous training and capacity-building of PG and LG staff. Ensuring that

staff had the capacity and training to discharge its new expenditure and revenue responsibilities was paramount. The

MDTF on PFM has been providing continuing training on processes and procedures for budget preparation, execution

and reporting while the World Bank’s TA project on PFM information system has been funding equipment and training

to automate manual processes, all for staff of subnational governments. This support was important not only for

successful implementation of reforms under FPFM-DPCs but also for their sustainability.



58. Lesson 3: Result-indicators of policy reforms should not only ensure transparency but also ensure it in a way

that it will facilitate citizen engagement. Many of result-indicators of policy reforms supported by this programmatic

series were not explicit in ensuring transparency and where they did, they were not facilitating the citizen engagement.

The citizen engagement component requires that data follow government’s standard reporting format and that they

are not only legally open but are also technically open 41 (machine-readable format).



59. Lesson 4. In designing a program of reforms in a low-capacity country transitioning to federal structure, it is

essential to strengthen M&E system. This is because although many of the data used to construct the result indicators

of the policy reforms supported by the FPFM-DPCs were produced by federal agencies, they were either not publicly

available or when they were publicly available, they were not released in the user-friendly format. In addition, for one

of the indicators, data was found to be not consistent across the reports generated by same federal agency. In other

cases, data had to be obtained from PGs or LGs that are located in different parts of the country. Some of these problems

can no doubt be attributed to capacity-constraints as well to issues of transition. But in any case, the M&E system for

this programmatic series had fallen behind in generating the necessary information in a timely way for many of the

program’s RIs. Thus, it is important for the bank to strengthen the M&E system so that both Bank and the GoN can have

a better understanding of the progress towards the accomplishment of objectives.



60. Lesson 5: In designing a program of reforms in a specific area, it is important to include measures from outside

that area, if implementation of the latter is critical to the program’s success. The reforms supported by FPFM-DPCs

were focused wholly on fiscal devolution and PFM but its full implementation depended on a reform measure in the

area of administrative devolution i.e. the passage of the Federal Civil Service Bill. Excessive delay in the passage of that

bill has continued to prevent, the PGs and LGs from recruiting staff to fill their much-needed positions 42. As a result,

subnational governments continue to face significant capacity constraints that have contributed to unmet targets for

several results indicators of the FPFM-DPC program.

       B. Next Phase



    61. To ensure continuity in fiscal and PFM reforms, follow-on operation is planned. Nepal’s economy continues to

    rely on import-related taxes, with almost half of total tax revenue coming from these taxes. At the same time, Nepal’s

    capital budget execution has not only remained low (average capital budget execution of 80.1 percent during FY15-

    FY19) but also that substantial amount of capital expenditure occurred towards the last quarter of each fiscal year

    (average of 59.9 percent of actual spending during FY15-FY19). The COVID-19 crisis has revealed structural

    vulnerabilities associated with Nepal’s reliance on import taxes and has further exacerbated capital budget execution

    problem. The WB is considering a three-year Programmatic Fiscal Policy for Growth Recovery and Resilience DPC to

    support the Government of Nepal’s efforts to provide relief during COVID-19 and lay the foundation for a resilient

    recovery. Supported reforms are structured around two pillars. The first pillar focuses on strengthening the regulatory

    and institutional framework to build back better and greener, and the second pillar supports reforms to enhance systems

    and regulations to protect the most vulnerable and support sustainable business growth. Proposed reforms under the

    first pillar include: (i) reducing tax distortions for increased production and revenue efficiency; (ii) strengthening debt

    management and transparency (iii) enhancing the efficiency of and climate-responsiveness public capital spending; iv)

    building a green and climate-resilient infrastructure; and (v) building back greener through improved solid waste

    management; and that of second pillar include: (i) compensating for the loss of livelihoods and building resilient social

    protection systems; (ii) enabling improved human capital investments in the federal system; and (iii) overcoming capital

    constraints and laying the foundation for post-pandemic green and climate-resilient growth. The first of this

    programmatic series was submitted to the Bank Board on May 18, 2021 and approved on June 15, 2021.



    62. The new programmatic series is complemented by other operations and analytical work. The proposed

    operation complements the Finance for Growth DPC series which supports efforts to strengthen financial sector

    stability, diversify financial solutions, and increase access to financial services. Implementation of reforms supported by

    this series is supported by the following IPFs: (i) the Integrated Public Financial Management Reform Project, (ii) the

    Youth Employment Transformation Initiative (YETI), (iii) the Urban Governance and Infrastructure Project, (iv) the Nepal

    School Sector Development Program, (v) the Enhanced Vocational Education and Training Project II and (vi) the Higher

    Education Reforms Project. Ongoing advisory and analytical services is being provided to the Digital Nepal initiative to

    harness digital technologies to support recovery from COVID-19, build resilience and promote inclusive growth. Also, a

    planned human capital project will focus on strengthening subnational capacity to deliver education, health and social

    protection services. Finally, the proposed DPC is complemented by on-going analytical work and technical support

    through the Federalism Platform and the Public Expenditure Review.
