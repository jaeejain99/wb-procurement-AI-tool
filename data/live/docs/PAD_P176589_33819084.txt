       I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT



       A. Country Context

   1.       Nepal is a landlocked country of about 29 million people. Most people (80 percent) live in rural

   areas, although the urbanization rate (about six percent) is higher than the regional average. The poverty

   headcount (at the international poverty line of US$1.90 per day) was 17.4 percent in 2019, down from

   30.1 percent in 2014.1 The country’s Human Development Index (HDI) is 0.602, which ranks 142 in the

   world for reporting countries. The inequality-adjusted HDI is 0.446, and life expectancy is 70.8 years.2



   2.        Nepal’s political institutions are still in transition, following the 2015 constitution. Nepal

   transitioned from a monarchy to a federal system of government in 2015, bringing a shared sense of hope

   and optimism after years of political instability. Two years later, in 2017, Nepal held successful elections

   for all three tiers (local, province, and federal) of the new state architecture defined in the 2015

   constitution. At the subnational level, funds, functions, and functionaries hitherto managed by the central,

   district, and village authorities are moving to the seven new provinces and 753 local governments (LGs),3

   while the national level is shifting focus to policy making and oversight. This state restructuring is likely to

   take time before becoming fully operational because most of the required legislation and administrative

   procedures are not in place yet. A new government took over on July 13, 2021, following about two years

   of political uncertainties and the reinstatement of parliament twice, on February 23, 2021, and July 12,

   2021, following its dissolution. The current coalition government of four major political parties is expected

   to remain intact until federal and provincial elections scheduled in Fiscal Year (FY) 2023. A new and full

   cabinet took office on October 8, 2021, and the country will get its next set of LG representatives following

   local polls announced for May 2022. With the expected change in the federal, provincial, and local

   governments this year, development activities are expected to slow while new officials are assuming their

   duties. Institutional capacity at the local level is nascent and evolving. Key staff at decision levels are

   mostly deputed from the Government of Nepal (GoN), and many federal agencies maintain a presence at

   the provincial levels. Some provinces have created Provincial Public Service Commissions (PPSCs) to

   recruit the staff capacity needed for LGs to discharge their mandates. However, the Federal Civil Service

   Act, which empowers PPSCs to recruit civil servants for LGs, has not yet been adopted.4



   3.      Despite these challenges, Nepal’s economy demonstrated impressive growth and resilience

   over the past decade when faced with a wide variety of economic and climate shocks. During FY2012–

   19, Nepal’s economy performed reasonably well despite being hit by three large exogenous shocks: the

   2015 earthquake, 2016 trade disruptions, and 2017 floods. A fourth shock, the COVID-19 pandemic,

   derailed the strong growth trajectory established over the previous three years. Economic growth

   contracted by 2.1 percent in FY20, the first contraction since FY1983, as a nationwide lockdown from

   March to July 2020 imposed to curtail the spread of the virus significantly affected all sectors of the

   economy. The economy is estimated to have grown by 1.8 percent in FY21 even though renewed

   containment measures were imposed in the fourth quarter of the fiscal year. Agriculture, contributing

   over one-fifth of nominal gross domestic product (GDP), has been a bright spot, registering 2.2 and 2.7

   percent growth in FY20 and FY21, respectively, on the back of favorable summer monsoons. After

   4.       The COVID-19 pandemic derailed the economy, but partial recovery is under way. The fiscal

   deficit narrowed to 4.6 percent of GDP in FY21. Economic growth is projected to recover gradually to 4.1

   percent by FY23. The baseline forecast projects a gradual medium-term recovery, with growth

   the end of FY22 (81.7 percent of the population ages 18 and higher have received full doses of vaccine by

   service subsectors. Industry sector growth is projected to be supported by increased hydropower

   production including from the recently completed Upper Tamakoshi plant. However, agricultural growth

   is projected to decelerate in FY22, reflecting a decline in paddy production and the rise of global fertilizer

   prices earlier in the fiscal year. Increasing fuel prices are also expected to weigh on aggregate demand.



   5.       The war in Ukraine is expected to impact Nepal’s economy through commodity prices . Nepal’s

   trade with Russia and Ukraine is limited to less than two percent of total imports―including mostly

   sunflower oil, dried peas, and colza seeds―but the share of fuel, agriculture, metals, and minerals in total

   imports is large. As such, Nepal is less likely to face an immediate negative supply shock because of the

   direct trade linkage. Instead, higher global commodity prices are expected to increase the import bill

   indirectly. Services exports are expected to be less affected given the relatively low share of tourist arrivals

   (less than three percent) from these two countries.



   6.       Nepal is highly vulnerable to climate change. The country’s mean annual temperature is

   projected to increase between 1.2°C and 4.2°C by the 2080s under the highest emission scenario

   compared to the baseline period 1986–2005.5 As total annual precipitation is predicted to fall within a

   shorter time frame and be more erratic, the dry seasons will be longer, and the risk of flooding due to

   higher monsoon precipitation is projected to increase by as much as 14–40 percent by 2030.6 Mountains

   are warming faster than the plains, triggering the melting of ice and permafrost and increasing the risk of

   glacier lake outburst floods. Changing climate has exacerbated more frequent and intense disaster events

   in recent years (drought, floods, landslides, disease outbreaks, and heatwaves), leading to a cascading

   effect on Nepal’s overall economic growth and development.7



       B. Sectoral and Institutional Context

   7.       Water plays a pivotal role in Nepal’s economy but is underutilized and affected by high climate

   variability. Over 80 percent of the monsoon-driven rainfall occurs in four months, causing problems of

   excess in the wet season and scarcity in the dry season. Nepal uses only about 15 billion cubic meters

   (BCM) out of 225 BCM of surface water available annually. Around 96 percent of this 15 BCM is used for

   Therefore, despite being rich in water resources, water security in Nepal is one of the lowest in all of Asia.8

   Nepal has inadequate storage compared to annual water demand.9 Therefore, the seasonal variability in

   precipitation dictates the rhythm of water-dependent sectors: agriculture, energy, and drinking water

   supply. This variability translates to lower agricultural output, high energy imports, and high coping costs

   due to domestic water scarcity in the dry months. The lack of year-round water availability for water

   supply and sanitation (WSS) has high economic costs. In 2015, the forgone output due to inadequate WSS

   was estimated at US$76.8 million.10



   8.       Evidence on the vulnerability of the WSS sector in Nepal to climate is well documented. A 2017

   survey of 41 springs in midwestern Nepal found that 73.2 percent had decreased flow and 12.2 percent

   had dried up.11 The decline in yield has forced communities to use distant alternative sources and even

   use less water than the minimum 50 liters per capita per day (lcpd) for basic needs or the 135 lcpd that

   the GoN aims to provide. In some of the country’s largest cities, water is supplied 3–6 days per week and

   usually for only a few hours on those days.12 Climate change will worsen the water situation as Himalayan

   glaciers melt faster than expected and water springs dry up. As annual and seasonal maximum

   temperatures have risen since 1971, along with higher variability in precipitation, there is also evidence

   that floods and drought frequency in Nepal has increased since 1981,13 with devastating impacts on water

   infrastructure. For instance, the Melamchi water supply project suffered critical damage to its headworks

   due to floods and landslides triggered by extreme precipitation in June 2021, cutting off the water supply

   to the capital, Kathmandu, that had just begun to emerge from a decade of debilitating water shortage.14



   9.       Access to WSS services in Nepal has improved over time, but significant challenges remain for

   the Government to achieve the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 6 on clean water

   and sanitation. According to the Joint Monitoring Program (JMP) database, the country achieved the SDG

   6 target for open defecation free status but is behind the target for access to safely managed water supply

   (Actual 2021 = 21 percent, Target 2030 = 50 percent).15 These numbers are confirmed by the recent

   Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys by the Nepal Planning Commission (MICS 2019), showing that nationally

   only 19 percent of households have access to safely managed drinking water, with urban households

   receiving higher proportions of access (22 percent) compared to rural households (13 percent). The draft

   Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) Sector Development Plan prepared by the Ministry of Water

   Supply (MoWS) in May 2019 estimates that about 16 million lack safely managed sanitation facilities.16

   10.      One of the most salient challenges in water service provision is the low functionality of the

   existing infrastructure. A recent functionality study found that out of 42,039 piped water supply schemes

   examined (both urban and rural) only 28 percent were described as ‘well-functioning; 38 percent did not

   have year-round supply;17 and 34 percent needed major repair, rehabilitation, reconstruction, or were

   non-repairable. The principal reasons for the low functionality rate include (a) inadequate revenue for

   operation and maintenance (O&M); (b) water source issues, particularly due to increasing climate

   variability and poor watershed management; and (c) inability of water supply and sanitation user groups

   (WSUGs) to undertake major repairs. For sanitation, urban households are dependent on pits and tanks

   that are cleaned infrequently, and sewerage coverage is sparse. There is no full-service chain available for

   sanitation (through sewerage or fecal sludge management - FSM), and only 7 percent of the population

   are connected to a private sewer network. As a result, the latest MICS survey data reveal that 85 percent

   of the nation’s household tap water is contaminated with E. Coli.18 Further, a recent sample of 737,000

   national groundwater sources found that close to 10 percent have arsenic concentrations above 10 µg/L

   (the World Health Organization limit),19 putting an estimated 2.6 million people at risk of high arsenic

   exposure.



   11.      Poor water and sanitation services have a high human capital cost. In Nepal, 36 percent of

   children under five years are stunted, with the lowest wealth quintile at 49 percent, compared to 17

   percent among the highest wealth quintile. Evidence shows that poor sanitation contributes to low

   hemoglobin and anemia in children under five and among women of childbearing age.20 One estimate

   shows that approximately 3,500 Nepali children die of water-borne diseases every year.21 In addition,

   women and girls bear the brunt of inadequate WASH services resulting in time poverty,22 limiting their

   ability to engage in other productive activities. Lack of safe and clean toilets also increases their risk of

   harassment and sexual assault. School-going girls also face barriers related to menstrual hygiene,

   including socio-cultural restrictions and practical issues such as shortage of soap and water, sanitary

   products, and waste disposal. These challenges hinder learning and education opportunities, undermining

   lifetime human capital gains.23



   12.      The institutional framework for water and sanitation is still transitioning to align with the 2015

   constitution. The Local Government Operation Act 2017 (LGOA) transferred the responsibility of providing

   safe and inclusive water, sanitation, irrigation, and disaster mitigation services from the GoN to LGs

   37.      The project addresses two key factors that pivotal to ensure sustainability of its investments:

   public support through broader stakeholder participation, strategic communication, and outreach. Other

   measures include implementing the institutional strengthening activities under the project, dialogue and

   close collaboration with Government entities, and proactive engagement by the World Bank team and

   management as issues arise.



   57.     Macroeconomic risks are Substantial. One major uncertainty is the speed of booster dose

   deployment and vaccine effectiveness to stop transmission of a new COVID-19 variant. Another is stronger

   import control measures to mitigate pressures on international reserves, which could affect growth

   through lower trade-related tax revenues, depressed private consumption and production, and lower

   CAPEX. The ongoing Russia-Ukraine conflict, if it deepens further, could lower travel demand and may

   threaten the recovery of tourism and tourism-related sectors. While downside risks are significant,

   Nepal’s strong record of sustained macroeconomic stability during periods of large exogenous shocks

   demonstrates a growing capacity to navigate future macroeconomic risks. If the pandemic proves

   protracted, the consequent macroeconomic effects may undermine the achievement of the PDO,

   especially if they lead to a further tightening of fiscal space and reduced availability of counterpart funds

   or an increase in the price of crucial imported inputs. Mitigation measures include the following: (a) the

   requirement for counterpart funds is kept to a minimum and (b) project costing includes price

   contingencies to mitigate the risk of an increase in the price of imported goods.



   58.     Sector strategies and policies risks are Substantial. Key sector policies and laws are currently

   undergoing revision, that is, the WASH Bill (2021), National Water Supply, Sanitation and Hygiene Sector

   Development Plan (2017–2030, draft), and National Water Supply and Sanitation Policy (Draft) to reflect,

   among other things, the change to a federal system of government which will be important for effective

   implementation of projects. Additionally, the sector suffers from residual tendencies of centralized

   government, limited oversight of fiduciary aspects, and a lack of financial and operational autonomy of

   LGs. The reforms proposed in the project are grounded in the country’s existing robust legislative policy

   regime under the 2015 constitution and LGOA 2017. The World Bank is working closely with MoWS to

   mitigate this risk by ensuring key reforms piloted in this project are also reflected in the revised sector

   policies and will continue engaging in policy dialogue to ensure that the key policy and legislative

   instruments are reviewed, approved, and adopted.



   59.      Institutional capacity for implementation and sustainability risk is Substantial. There is a

   significant risk that government structures, including local levels, may not have enough capacity to

   implement and manage project-supported activities. LGs lack adequate human resources and technical

   and financial capacity to fulfill their mandates. There is also a lack of clarity on the roles of the three tiers

   of government, and intergovernmental coordination mechanisms are lacking. To address these risks, the

   project will provide support to strengthen the institutional capacities of the critical units within the

   provincial and local governments. A participatory Water Sector Federalism Implementation Assessment

   will be conducted in the first year, which will feed into policy dialogue and identify additional reforms to

   be supported by the project. The integrative nature of the project ensures that infrastructure solutions

   will be deployed in synchronization with institutional development to optimize use, services, and costs,

   all aiming at low cost, climate resilience, locally adapted solutions.

       60.     Fiduciary management risk is Substantial. Several initiatives have been implemented to enhance

       the operating environment public finance management (PFM) at the provincial and local government

       (PLG) levels in the project areas, including the development and implementation of financial regulations,

       the introduction of Subnational Treasury Regulatory Application (SuTRA), an external audit carried out by

       the Supreme Audit Institution, good coverage of PFM by Financial Procedures and Fiscal Responsibility

       Act and Regulation, rolling out of Nepal Public Sector Accounting Standards among others, all of which

       include PLGs. However, many PLGs are in the process of building their FM capability, including

       strengthening of their legal environment, system development, staff mobilization, establishing of

       standard internal and external auditing systems, and so on. A mitigation measure is to enhance the FM

       capacity of participating municipalities by providing additional capacity-building programs in collaboration

       with other ongoing DP engagements and Government of Nepal programs, including IPFMRP.



       61.    Procurement risk is Substantial. The bulk of the civil works activities will be undertaken under

       Component 2 by the urban and rural municipalities, while the PMU under the DWSSM will implement

       Components 1 and 3 of the project. Usage of the Systematic Tracking of Exchanges in Procurement (STEP)

       system by the implementing agencies at the municipal level may be challenging due to their weak

       procurement capacity, including a lack of experience in implementing World Bank-financed projects.

       Procurement risks will be mitigated through (a) ensuring adequate staffing of requisite expertise

       throughout the project life cycle, (b) engaging consulting firms for providing technical/management

       support, and (c) designing and implementing an appropriate capacity-building program for

       DWSSM/municipal staff on procurement, contract management, and technical skills development.



       62.      Environmental and social risks is Substantial due to the limited existing capacity and systems in

       implementing entities for identifying and managing E&S risks and impacts, as well as considering the

       potential for adverse impacts of proposed activities on the environment and host communities of the

       project. Overall, the proposed interventions are expected to benefit people residing in selected

       municipalities through improved and climate resilient WSS services and improved capacity of

       municipalities. The E&S risks will also be mitigated through a screening and assessment mechanism

       defined in the ESMF and PIM to exclude all high-risk interventions and to minimize, mitigate, and manage

       E&S risks and impacts in the eligible subprojects. Additionally, the project will provide extensive technical

       training and on-the-ground support for municipalities by deploying design and supervision support teams.

       The PST at the federal level will also have dedicated E&S safeguards experts to provide due diligence,

       quality assurance, and implementation supervision support to the MoWS PMU and social and

       environmental officers at participating municipalities and mobilizers as needed.



       63.     Other risk is Substantial. Due to the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic, the Government imposed

       lockdowns and travel restrictions may be imposed, which may affect the implementation of the project

       during the first year. Construction sites have typically been exempted from these restrictions; thus, civil

       works components are unlikely to be adversely affected by the pandemic. However, the capacity building

       and technical assistance activities may be affected initially. To mitigate this risk, virtual technologies will

       be adopted for training and capacity-building activities where possible. This risk is expected to reduce as

       vaccination rates continue to rise in Nepal, with the expectation that vaccination coverage will reach

       adequate levels to diminish the rates of infection by the second year of the project.

                        ANNEX 1: Implementation Arrangements and Support Plan



                                           COUNTRY: Nepal

                       Water Sector Governance and Infrastructure Support Project



 1.       MoWS will be the primary coordinating agency for the project and will be responsible for project

 implementation, management, supervision, and coordination. A PMU will be established in the MoWS

 DWSSM with a full-time project director and key project management/technical staff. The DWSSM PMU

 will be responsible for implementation of Components 1 and 3, in coordination with beneficiary

 municipalities. The PMU will be supported by a PST, comprising expertise in technical, FM, procurement,

 E&S safeguards, gender and social inclusion, M&E, and project management. The PMU’s role includes

 procurement of technical consultancies such as the PST and MSTs and other necessary service contracts

 for activities under Component 1 and 3. The PST will comprise experts in areas such as water sector

 institutional development, WASH planning and engineering, citizen engagement and community

 mobilization, social development, gender and social inclusion, FM, procurement and contract

 management, M&E, and environmental management.



 2.       In line with their mandate under federalism, Participating municipalities will be responsible for

 the implementation of Component 2, which comprises over 75 percent of Project investments.

 Accordingly, they will receive support to establish the necessary institutional structures and capacity for

 WASH service delivery through Component 1. As a first step, partitipating municipalities will be required

 to establish a dedicated MWASH unit as formal entity legally established within the local government

 institutional structure which will be responsible for WASH service planning, regulation and service delivery

 within the municipality beyond the life of the Project. Due to the limited capacity at the local level, project

 managers will be seconded by the MoWS DWSSM to support a PIU to be established in each participating

 municipality, which will be responsible for the actual implementation of Component 2 activities.



 3.      The PIU is a Special Project Vehicle to embed DWSSM technical assistance and handholding

 support to the Municipalities. It will be responsible for identifying, planning, designing, and implementing

 WSS infrastructure investments, as well as for the management, oversight, and reporting. For each

 participating municipality, this PIU will be led by a project manager seconded by the DWSSM and will

 include the MWASH unit head and staff (established under the project), as well as key personnel in areas

 of WSS engineering, planning, FM, and community mobilization for implementing the infrastructure

 subprojects and institutional strengthening activities. MSTs will be hired by the PMU to support the PIUs

 in the participating municipalities for design and supervision support comprising expertise in

 technical/engineering, FM, procurement, M&E, gender and social inclusion, and E&S safeguards. The

 municipalities through their PIUs will be responsible for implementation in close collaboration with wards

 and WSUCs. In the rural subcomponent, WSUCs in the participating municipalities will be closely involved

 in the identification, implementation, and supervision of rural WASH schemes. All the participating

 municipalities for Component 2 will be required to sign a participation agreement with MoWS to agree to

 the terms and conditions of the proposed project. A responsibility matrix for the implementation of

 Component 2 activities is provided in Table 1.1 below.

 Financial Management



 9.      MoWS has recent experience in implementing World Bank-financed projects but the DWSSM does

 not. The FM sections in both the agencies have qualified and experienced finance staff required for project

 FM arrangement. As the project aims to implement major activities from the LG, assessment of the FM

 capacity at the LG level has been undertaken including field visits to four municipalities: Sharada (Salyan),

 Birendranagar (Surkhet), and Janaki and Joshipur (Kailali).



 10.    FM staffing. The MoWS DWSSM and the participating municipalities have adequate finance staff

 and accountants for FM. Finance staff at the federal PMU and the participating municipalities will be

 provided with training on project-specific FM aspects. A dedicated accounts officer will be hired at the

 PMU at the federal level, that is, at the DWSSM. Each implementing municipality will also have a qualified

 accountant who will be trained on implementing World Bank-financed projects. Considering the regular

 workload of municipality finance staff, the project may hire FM consultants for each municipality as

 necessary. Further, an FM consultant shall be hired to supplement capacity in the PMU.



 11.      Applicable FM regulations. The FM arrangements will follow the prevailing federal regulations—

 such as the Financial Procedure and Fiscal Responsibility Act 2019, Local Government Operations Act

 2017, and Intergovernmental Fiscal Arrangement Act 2017. The FM in the municipalities will be guided by

 the respective Financial Procedures Acts adopted by the local assemblies. The specific requirements for

 project implementation at the municipality level will be guided by the participation agreement between

 MoWS and the respective municipalities, as well as the PIM.



 12.     Planning and budgeting. The project has already got a separate budget subhead in the Red Book

 (Government’s Budget Allocation Book) with a budget allocation of NPR 20.5 million from Government

 source for FY2021–22. Each municipality shall formulate an annual work plan and budget (AWPB) for the

 project as per the planning and budgeting process of the GoN and send the same to the PMU. The PMU

 shall consolidate the AWPBs including for the federal level and provide a AWPB for the whole project. The

 PMU shall have the responsibility to get approval from MoF and ensure that the budget is included in the

 national ‘Red Book’. The project shall have a separate budget subhead and the PMU and municipalities

 will be the cost centers. Line Ministry Budgetary Information System (LMBIS) shall recognize all the

 municipalities as cost centers and accordingly budget authorization and release shall be made.



 13.     Counterpart funding. The Recipient has committed to 20 percent counterpart financing for the

 project. The Recipient’s portion shall be maintained in the relevant fiscal year’s budget and the

 counterpart funding shall be on joint co-financing basis, from the three tiers of government.



 14.     Annual grant cycle and fund flow. The participating municipalities will be assessed for the

 compliance against the GACs on an quarterly basis by the DWSSM PMU. MoWS must submit the annual

 consolidated budget estimate for the project to MoF through LMBIS and the same will be approved by

 MoF as part of annual federal budgeting process. Conditional grants will be made available to the

 municipalities on a quarter basis through the Treasury Single Account, DTCO, in alignment with the

 existing disbursement procedure. At the end of the fiscal year, unspent grant funding will have to be

 returned by municipalities to MoF and will be adjusted in the subsequent annual allocations. To avoid any

 cash problems that may cause issues for municipalities to honor their contractual payment obligations,

 MoF will ensure that municipalities have the required funds to carry out the multiyear investments

 planned under this project through flexible budget reprogramming provisions.

 23.     Procurement risk assessment. The following major risks have been identified: (a) limited technical

 and procurement capacity of DWSSM in-house staff in planning, designing, procuring, and managing

 contracts; (b) delay in decision-making in case of high-value contracts; and (c) adverse impact of the recent

 amendment in public procurement rules, PPR 6th through 11th, which are not in line with good

 procurement practices. These will have adverse implications in bidding process and contract execution,

 such as not allowing bidders to participate in bidding for more than 5 procurement opportunities, unfair

 sanction provision for bidders (i.e. exclusion of bidders against whom fraud and corruption charges has

 been filed in the Court of Law), unfair contract termination & JV provisions, increased performance

 security, addition of several bureaucratic layers for taking procurement decisions, etc. MoWS DWSSM

 has not implemented World Bank-financed projects, though it has experience of executing projects

 funded by the ADB. Considering the high volume of works procurement and scattered procurement, the

 agency’s procurement risk is rated ‘Substantial’. A capacity assessment of the participating municipalities

 by the World Bank noted that they have dedicated engineers/sub-engineers in their procurement

 committees, but these staff have no experience of executing procurement under the World Bank’s

 intermittent basis for the first six months, to assist DWSSM in appointing the PSTs and MSTs and (b)

 training of DWSSM and participating urban/rural municipalities by the World Bank team on the World

 29.      Financing and fiscal capacity. Day-to-day expenses in the water sector rely on consumer fees,

 while investments in the sector mostly rely on Government support. For the project, the investments will

 be based on conditional grants to the municipalities from the Government of Nepal. Counterpart funding

 will be around 20 percent of the total funding and will rely on the Recipient, with varying contributions

 from the participating municipalities. Nevertheless, in the long run, WSS service providers should move

 toward full cost recovery fees (OPEX and partial CAPEX) based on comprehensive affordability analysis

 and stakeholder consultations to increase the financial sustainability. Financially sustainable WSS service

 providers will ease the burden of overreliance on Government’s fund in the sector.



 30.     Birendranagar Municipal water supply. The investment will secure an additional water source

 and expansion of distribution network to service the population in the urban municipality of

 Birendranagar. The new water source is Bheri River located outside Surkhet Valley and Birendranagar. The

 water will be treated and pumped to a nearby reservoir, where it will service the municipality by gravity.

 The capacity is 45,000 m3 per day, and the intake is planned for the year 2050 demand providing 100

 percent coverage of Birendranagar Municipality. To improve the sustainability of services, institutional

 reforms will be introduced to create a municipal water utility and corporatizing the service. The Surkhet

 Valley Water User Committee (SVWUC) will initially operate the assets and bear responsibility for

 supporting processes including billing and fee collection. As a conditional grant will cover the cost of the

 implementation, there will be no local debt associated with the infrastructure improvement. Financial

 projections show that the associated higher costs of operating the new water supply system including

 higher costs of pumping, staff, and repair and maintenance will require increases in water tariff from the

 current level by around 80 percent in real terms, just to recover the cost of O&M costs in 2025/26. With

 increased household coverage over time, this will reduce due to economy of scale. Recovery of full costs

 from fees will require increases from the current tariff level by around 150 percent in 2030/31, when all

 households are connected.
