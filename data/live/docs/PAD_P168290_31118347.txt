    I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT

    A. Country Context



1.       Vietnam has sustained rapid economic growth rates since the introduction of the Doi Moi reforms

in the late 1980s, allowing the country to transform from a low-income economy to a middle-income

economy in one generation. With gross domestic product (GDP) growth averaging 6.7 percent annually,

real GDP per capita more than quadrupled over the thirty-year period from 1987 to 2017. Economic

growth coupled with the Government’s strong focus on inclusive social development has enabled Vietnam

to drastically reduce the incidence of extreme poverty and broaden prosperity. By the World Bank’s

measure of shared prosperity (that is, the income growth of the bottom 40 percent of the population),

Vietnam is one of the most noteworthy cases of long-term shared prosperity globally. The pace of

economic growth is expected to continue, with the country’s Socio-Economic Development Plan (SEDP)

for 2016-2020 setting out an annual growth target of 6.5-7.0 percent.



2.       As is common for developing and industrializing economies, urban growth has accompanied

Vietnam’s rapid economic expansion, with the fastest urban population growth concentrated in and

around Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City (HCMC). The urban population has grown by 3.1 percent annually,

with half the country’s population expected to live in urban areas by 2040.1 While peri-urban areas around

the two major cities have benefitted from their proximity to key economic drivers, regions elsewhere in

the country are at risk of falling behind. The World Bank’s Vietnam Urbanization Review (2011) highlighted

that access to basic services, such as sanitation, drainage, and quality of water, remains low in secondary

cities as compared to large cities. For example, although the access rates to hygienic water ranges from

90–96 percent in HCMC and Hanoi, Class II to III cities have access rates of only 50–70 percent. Similarly,

although the access rate to sanitation with connection is above 80 percent in both HCMC and Hanoi, lower

class cities have access rates as low as 15 percent. Only 7.6 percent of cities and urban areas have

appropriate wastewater collection and treatment systems. Although the rates are relatively high among

Special, Class I, and Class II cities, the majority of cities categorized Class III and below still lack wastewater

collection and disposal systems.

4.      Considering the importance of urbanization in the country’s industrialization and modernization

process, the Government of Vietnam (GoV) developed the Framework Master Plan for Urban

Development in Viet Nam to 2025 and Vision to 2050 (hereby referred to as the National Master Plan) in

2009. The National Master Plan foresees an annual increase in urban population of 5.3 percent, with the

urban population reaching 52 million by 2025. The plans underscore the importance of well-planned and

well-managed urban growth for the country to continue its transformation into a high-income economy.



    B. Sectoral and Institutional Context



5.       Current urbanization trends. Urban areas currently account for 34 percent of Vietnam’s

population and contribute more than half of national GDP.6 Global evidence suggests that the benefits

from urban growth come from encouraging economic densification, which allows cities to harness the

agglomeration economies that enhance productivity, spur innovation and economic diversification, and

facilitate more efficient service delivery. However, a notable characteristic of urban development in

Vietnam has been low and stagnant levels of urban density. Between 2000 and 2015, urban density

remained at 18.9 urban residents per hectare even as urban land expanded by over 650,000 hectares.7

Increasingly fragmented urbanization is driven in part by Vietnam’s current City Classification System,

which provides fiscal incentives for rapid land conversion and physical expansion of cities, with little

emphasis placed on urban density.8 In an analysis of seven cities that attained Class I status between 2009

and 2011, all but one city failed to meet the minimum standards for urban density, implying that other

factors, such as the non-agricultural labor population and development of infrastructure, were relied upon

to qualify for upgrading.



6.      Services that promote opportunities for both men and women to benefit from and contribute to

local economies are important for cities in stimulating economic growth. In Vietnam, however, women’s

household and care responsibilities constrain their ability to work on equal terms as men. There is a

gender gap in the share of urban women engaged in paid work compared to men. In 2014, the proportion

of female workers without an employment contract was 47.8 percent, whereas this ratio among male

7.        Role of secondary cities. The National Master Plan focuses on achieving balanced and strategic

growth through a national urban system, consisting of urban centers of various grades and types

distributed throughout the country. Specifically, it envisages the development of secondary and tertiary

cities as hubs to drive development within larger urban areas and provinces. This is consistent with

international experience, where there is growing recognition of the role of secondary cities as catalysts in

facilitating localization economies and the efficient transfer of goods, people, services, and information

within a system of cities at different levels (that is, metropolitan, regional, national, and global).12

Balanced regional development and appropriate definitions of functions among different hierarchies of

urban areas are of great importance. For example, large cities should provide a diverse range of services

and connect to external areas, thus promoting international competitiveness, whereas secondary cities

should focus on specialized manufacturing activities. Many countries have been successful with this

development pattern.13



8.        As Vietnam seeks to sustain an ambitious growth trajectory, nurturing secondary cities that have

demonstrated the economic potential to play a greater role in enhancing productivity and growth will be

essential. However, it remains a challenge for many secondary cities to raise capital and attract the

investment required to build infrastructure and support communities that are critical to create dynamic

economies, improved livelihoods, and jobs.14 Demand for basic infrastructure remains high in smaller

cities in Vietnam—many still lack wastewater treatment facilities and public transport networks often do

not exist. Poor provision of infrastructure has implications both for the quality of life for existing residents,

as well as on the attractiveness of the city for further investment and growth.



9.      Growing exposure to climate change. Compounding the need for infrastructure is the pressure

brought by climate change. Vietnam is particularly exposed to a variety of hydrogeological hazards,

including the increased frequency and intensity of droughts and storms (including typhoons). The impacts

of climate change are made worse by inappropriate land use planning and ecosystem degradation as well

as climate change. The increasing occurrence of droughts is likely to result in increasing rural-urban

migration, which will place further strain on Vietnam’s cities. Growing frequency and intensity of storms

10.      World Bank Urban Strategy in Vietnam. The World Bank’s flagship report “Vietnam 2035: Toward

Prosperity, Creativity, Equity and Democracy” emphasized the need to strengthen institutions for

integrated urban planning—both functionally (that is, within and across sectors) and spatially (that is,

across contiguous urban areas and encompassing provincial and metropolitan/city-level plans)—to

encourage scale economies at corridor, metropolitan area/conurbation, and regional levels. The report

cautions cities in Vietnam against becoming locked into a “large scale” development mindset, in which

the accelerated conversion of rural to urban land encourages sprawl and oversized infrastructure. Large

infrastructure and a lack of medium- and small-scale street networks will eventually limit the available

connectivity options available (for example, public transport and non-motorized transport systems),

further increasing private motorization. The key lessons from this study are directly relevant to the

challenges faced by secondary cities.



11.      The World Bank’s urban sector engagement strategy in Vietnam therefore recognizes the growing

role of secondary cities and the critical need for stronger, more integrated urban planning, including an

increasing focus on helping cities mitigate climate change risks. The urban portfolio currently focuses on

cities at different scales such as: (a) integrated stand-alone operations in large cities (including HCMC,

Hanoi, Da Nang, Hai Phong, and Can Tho), (b) multi-city approaches targeting infrastructure development

and strengthening urban management and planning in secondary cities, and (c) piloting new approaches,

such as Program-for-Results (PforR), for supporting small cities and towns in lagging regions.



12.     The proposed Dynamic Cities Integrated Development Project (DCIDP) is aligned with the World

Bank’s multi-city approach for secondary cities, broadly defined to include Class I, II, and III cities with

populations ranging from 100,000 to 500,000. These four proposed subproject cities (Ky Anh, Tinh Gia,

Hai Duong, and Yen Bai) were originally planned to be included in the first phase of the project approved

in FY18. However, only one subproject city (Thai Nguyen) managed to obtain approval in time for the

scheduled negotiations and obtain board approval within FY18. By the 1st quarter of 2019, the GoV

approved the pre-feasibility studies of the four remaining cities, which paved the way for the proposed

project to be negotiated and approved within FY19.

 II. PROJECT DESCRIPTION



14.      DCIDP will support four secondary cities that have demonstrated both current significance and

future growth potential as province- and region-level urban economic centers. As consistent with

approved city master plans, the proposed operation will provide financing for strategic and climate-

resilient municipal infrastructure that will help the proposed subproject cities to: (a) improve access to,

and reliability of, urban services for the bottom 40 percent of population; (b) promote the development

of neighborhoods with access to high quality public spaces and public transport; (c) support continued

socio-economic growth (for example, by enhancing productivity and localization economies, removing

infrastructure constraints, improving connectivity, facilitating local job creation); and (d) promote

women’s opportunity to access paid work. DCIDP will also support the subproject cities in addressing

fundamental urban development challenges through TA for integrated urban planning and management

that will promote more sustainable urban development and the development of higher quality

neighborhoods.



15.       Background on Subproject cities. The proposed subproject cities of Ky Anh, Tinh Gia, Hai Duong,

and Yen Bai were identified on the basis of their economic growth potential, urgent need for improved

urban infrastructure services (particularly in relation to climate/disaster risks, management of urban

sprawl, and environmental protection and management), and operational readiness. Driven by the

establishment of large-scale industrial economic zones, Ky Anh (Ha Tinh Province) and Tinh Gia (Thanh

Hoa Province) will be upgraded from district-level towns to Class IV cities by 2019. In contrast, Hai Duong

(Class II, Hai Duong Province) and Yen Bai (Class III, Yen Bai Province) have longer histories as the urban

networks. The lack of financing sources along with poor investment prioritization have resulted in

increasing traffic congestion, flooding, and uneven spatial development, as well as the deterioration (or

lack of provision) of public spaces. Road network development has also tended to encourage

motorization, with little attention paid to the development of appropriate public transportation systems.

19.      Constraints in integrated, climate-resilient urban planning. Although each of the proposed

subproject cities has generated economic growth that has driven their development as dynamic urban

centers, the spatial and economic planning capacities of the cities remain uniformly weak, with tenuous

inter-linkages between spatial, economic, and investment planning at both the city and provincial level.

To capitalize on their competitive advantages and enable further growth, the cities require not just

improvements to basic urban infrastructure, but also more comprehensive and integrated urban planning

that links physical development to long-term socioeconomic strategies, and to climate change adaptation

and disaster risk management plans, which have become increasingly essential for the cities in recent

years. Currently, the master plans of the proposed subproject cities through 2030 suggest conversion of

agricultural to urban land and urban expansion with minimal restrictions, without clear market demand

analysis or an indication of phasing plans. The five-year socio-economic development plans of the cities



                                                                                                Page 13 of 69

(through 2020) are very broadly defined and do not fully account for how each city strategically plans to

leverage their respective economic endowments and address disaster and climate change risks to

accelerate local economic development.



20.      At the neighborhood level, city plans do not adequately consider the provision of greenery or

public space. The proposed expansions of road networks are not consistently supported by adequate

traffic-demand analysis. Plans also do not currently account for the need for public transport, which is a

key element in reducing motorization and avoiding large-scale, inefficient transport infrastructure.

Importantly, road networks do not provide a suitable environment for pedestrians or cyclists, and do not

necessarily facilitate residential and commercial accessibility for existing communities. Though they are

at different stages of development, the road networks in all the proposed subproject cities face the same

challenges—the demand for improved connectivity and the need to integrate infrastructure and land use

planning. Further studies are necessary to assess street patterns and the potential for developing

appropriate public transport systems based on each city’s anticipated growth patterns and projected

populations.

 B. Project Components



23.      The total project cost is US$276.17 million, of which US$194.36 million will be financed by a

transitional IDA Credit. The remaining project cost of US$81.81 million will be financed through

counterpart fund (CF) contributions from the Provincial People’s Committees (PPCs) of each subproject

city, ranging from 23 percent to 38 percent of the concessional loan for each subproject city, that will

finance land acquisition and compensation, TA activities, project management, and other costs. Table 5

provides a breakdown of the DCIDP cost and financing plan for each subproject city. The project consists

of two main components.



24.      Component 1: Structural Investments - Rehabilitation and Construction of Resilient Urban

Infrastructure (total cost: US$234.02 million, IDA: US$184.89 million, CF: US$49.13 million). A series of

municipal investments will be financed in each subproject city to improve the access to and quality of

critical urban infrastructure services, including those in urban environmental management, urban

transport, and urban amenities and public spaces. Given the vulnerability of the subproject cities to

disaster risks and climate change, the proposed investments are focused on strengthening overall urban

resilience and, in particular, reducing flooding in historically prone areas. Spanning several sectors, the

proposed investments are consolidated under a single project component to ensure that DCIDP provides

sufficient flexibility to support a menu of municipal infrastructure solutions to address the specific

demands of the subproject cities. Investments in all cities will be designed, constructed, and managed

with resilience to climate change duly factored in.



25.     The selection of infrastructure subprojects will be aligned with the respective updated master

plans of each city. Given that the investments will be identified based on plans developed before project

implementation, the proposals will be rigorously prioritized to ensure that these are no-regret





                                                                                               Page 15 of 69

       The World Bank

       Vietnam - Dynamic Cities Integrated Development Project (P168290)







investments that will: (a) improve access to, and reliability of, urban services for the bottom 40 percent

of the population, (b) promote the development of neighborhoods with access to high-quality public

spaces and public transport, (c) support long-term socio-economic growth objectives, (d) robustly meet

demands for climate change adaptation and disaster-resilience, and (e) meet accepted standards for

technical and economic soundness. Further, the design and implementation of infrastructure subprojects

will factor in access to services for women and men (for example, differentiated travel patterns and safety)

and universal design (that is, ensuring accessibility to older people and people with disabilities)

considerations.



26.      In the interest of maximizing finance for development, due consideration will be made to identify

opportunities for increased private sector participation in the proposed environmental sanitation and

urban transport investments. The project will explore options for private sector participation through

partial financing of infrastructure, utility reform, and network operations by private operators.

Furthermore, through investments in critical infrastructure such as urban drainage and flood protection,

there are expected to be spillover benefits into investments in IPs, EPZs, and tourist attractions that will

lead to further private investment in the subproject cities.



27.    Ownership of the proposed infrastructure investments will be assumed by the subproject cities,

which will be required to establish adequate institutional arrangements and operations and maintenance

(O&M) plans to ensure future sustainability. The proposed sub-components are as follows:



        •    Sub-component 1.1 – Urban drainage: Across all subproject cities, the overall improvement

             of the local drainage systems (including construction of new drains, dredging and

             embankment of streams and lakes) has been proposed to address the need for improved

             flood management using principles of urban integrated water management, particularly in

             light of both current and projected susceptibility to climate change. This is critical for

             enhancing resilience capacity against floods and sustaining the significant private and public

             investments in IPs, EPZs, and tourist attractions in the subproject cities. The designs of

             drainage and flood control infrastructure will take in account scenarios produced by Ministry

             of Natural Resources and Environment (MONRE) and reflect them through improved

             hydraulic modeling works and flexible use of structural and non-structural approaches.



        •    Sub-component 1.2 – Urban environmental sanitation: This includes the rehabilitation and

             construction of sewer collection networks, and construction of wastewater treatment

             plants. To address the pollution caused by domestic wastewater, new wastewater collection

             and treatment systems have been proposed for three of the four subproject cities that

             include provisions for addressing generated fecal sludge. In addition to improving the

             sanitation conditions of the subproject cities, the wastewater collection and treatment will

             also help to reduce wastewater related greenhouse gas emissions, though it will not be

             substantial. Technical designs for urban environmental sanitation investments will explore

             low impact designs and water-sensitive urban design interventions.



        •    Sub-component 1.3 – Urban transport: This includes investments in strategic urban roads

             and bridges for better connectivity. Each of the subproject cities has proposed road and

             bridge subprojects that were identified in existing master plans. These proposals have been

             vetted at the preparation stage to ensure that they are based on sound analyses of travel



                                                                                                Page 16 of 69

       The World Bank

       Vietnam - Dynamic Cities Integrated Development Project (P168290)







             and traffic demand and street design patterns. The provision of the proposed roads and

             bridges in each city is expected to provide better accessibility for residents to jobs,

             education, and other services, as well as to improve traffic safety, which are critical for

             sustaining rapid local economic development in the subproject cities. In addition, the

             investments will promote non-motorized transport options as well as safeguard flexibility

             for the introduction of a public transport system. The newly constructed roads and bridges

             will help increase resilience of the subproject cities against inundation by providing better

             flood segregation and mobility in emergencies. As such, technical designs will provide

             flexibility for the introduction of public transport systems and/or adoption as potential

             public transport routes. Furthermore, traffic safety facilities have been included in all

             preliminary technical designs. Traffic issues will be thoroughly reviewed and mitigated,

             especially at intersections with major roads and transit roads of national highways/bypasses.

             To address the potential impacts of climate change, adaptation measures will be included in

             the road designs (both at basic and detailed stages) to correspond to the most updated

             MONRE climate change scenarios. Road drainage structures will be designed based on

             hydrologic analyses that adopt climate change scenarios that account for increasing

             precipitation and seawater levels, whereas the elevation of roads and bridges will take into

             account projected flood distribution increases. Other issues such as road slope protection

             will be required for subprojects in mountainous areas as well as for the high embankments

             in flat conditions. Complex drainage systems combined with slope protection will be

             required to encourage bio-engineering for climate resilience.



        •    Sub-component 1.4 – Urban amenities and public spaces: The improvement of lakes and

             channels proposed under the other sub-components will strengthen urban resilience and

             also provides potential opportunities to introduce new, accessible public spaces around the

             improved infrastructure. The improvements will include public green spaces and

             promenades with lanes for both cyclists and pedestrians. The corresponding planting of

             trees as part of these improvements is expected to further the climate change mitigation

             benefits of the project. The project will also support the development of resettlement sites

             to accommodate families that may have to relocate or resettle due to the project

             investments.

   E. Rationale for Bank Involvement and Role of Partners



  32.    Rationale for World Bank involvement. Although the economic benefits of the DCIDP-financed

  subprojects are expected to exceed their economic costs, the services to be provided by the subprojects

  cannot be delivered by the private sector profitably because of the following:



             (a)     Missing markets. The market for a service is deemed “missing” if the service is non-rival and

                     non-excludable. For the DCIDP-financed subprojects, markets are missing for inner roads,

                     bridges, drainage and flood control systems, and urban management. The private sector is

                     unlikely to invest in such services because it will not be able to exclude non-paying

                     consumers from using the service.



             (b)     Positive externalities. Wastewater treatment can, in principle, be provided to individual





                                                                                                                              Page 20 of 69

       The World Bank

       Vietnam - Dynamic Cities Integrated Development Project (P168290)







              customers. However, it is unlikely that customers are willing to pay a price for these services

              that are sufficient to cover the full cost of construction and subsequent O&M (including a

              risk-adjusted profit margin). Therefore, the proposed subprojects will not be financially

              profitable to their owners, even though they generate substantial benefits (“positive

              externalities”) to other parties.



33.      Without some form of public intervention, the market is thus likely to underprovide urban

infrastructure services than is optimal from a societal point of view. To address the above mentioned

market failures, Government intervention is proposed in the form of public investments in critical

municipal infrastructure and sustained funding of O&M budgets at adequate levels. The World Bank is

well positioned to support the GoV to address these specific issues through the provision of TA and

financing in the design and implementation of the proposed project. This is a role that has been validated

by the GoV through a long-term engagement on a broad portfolio of urban development projects.



34.      Role of Partners. The World Bank coordinates extensively with development partners engaged in

urban development projects in Vietnam (for example, Swiss State Secretariat for Economic Affairs, Asian

Development Bank [ADB], Japan International Cooperation Agency, Export-Import Bank of Korea, Agence

Française de Développement, and European Union). Partnerships will be explored with development

partners to leverage additional development financing to supplement the non-structural investments

under Component 2. In particular, discussions are ongoing with the Swiss State Secretariat for Economic

Affairs and Global Facility for Disaster Reduction and Recovery to mobilize trust funds to support the

subproject cities in: (a) developing integrated master plans, and (b) strengthening technical and regulatory

capacity for integrated urban planning and development.



 F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design



35.     The operational design of DCIDP integrates lessons learned from other urban operations in the

country, such as the Medium Cities Development Project (MCDP), Da Nang Sustainable Development

Project, Vietnam Urban Upgrading Projects (VUUPs), and Can Tho Urban Development and Resilience

Project, as well as similar operations globally (including China, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, Tanzania, and

Uganda).



        (a)   Need for strong, sustained political commitment. In recognition of the critical need for

              urban infrastructure financing, and capacity building for developing integrated and

              evidence-based master plans that are linked to the budget process, the project has received

              strong support from the central, provincial, and city governments. However, the sustained

              commitment of the provincial authorities and their technical departments will be essential

              for the effective preparation and implementation of the project.



        (b)   Verification of fiscal capacities of provinces of subproject cities to borrow on-lent

              concessional loan and provide CFs. Each of the participating PPCs have been assessed as

              having sufficient fiscal capacity to (i) cover debt service payments on the proposed sub-loans

              for DCIDP and (ii) provide the minimum required CF contributions. This is critical in light of

 A. Institutional and Implementation Arrangements



36.      Following the lessons and best practices from the World Bank’s recent urban development

projects in Vietnam (for example, MCDP, Da Nang Sustainable Development Project, and VUUPs), DCICP

will be implemented in a decentralized manner, with the subproject cities as the project owners under

the supervision of their respective PPCs, which are legally designated as the implementing agencies of the

project. Each subproject will be implemented independently with a specific credit line for the subproject

city. The Ministry of Planning and Investment and Ministry of Finance (MOF) will oversee overall project

implementation, with each subproject city periodically reporting on the project implementation progress

and quality to the ministries.



37.       At the appraisal stage, the PPCs have each established a Project Preparation Unit (PPU) within an

existing Project Management Unit (PMU) that has been implementing either ODA-funded or GoV-funded

urban infrastructure investments in the respective subproject cities. A PMU for DCIDP will be formally

established from the PPU in each subproject city upon the approval of the Investment Policy Report (pre-

feasibility study) of the subproject city by the GoV. Given the crucial role of provincial leadership in

facilitating project implementation in each subproject city, the PPCs may each establish a Project Steering

Committee that is comprised of relevant sectoral departments to guide, support, and supervise the

respective PMUs.



38.     In terms of implementing the non-structural investments under Component 2, MOC will serve as

a technical partner in supporting the TA and capacity building activities. Subject to availability, trust fund

resources will be mobilized to provide capacity building support to MOC to enable key officials to

participate and learn from international urban planning approaches and local planning processes, and to

subsequently review and revise relevant policies and regulations as appropriate.



 B. Results Monitoring and Evaluation Arrangements



39.      The M&E system, including a Results Framework, is designed to assess whether the project is

being implemented in line with the proposed objectives and its achievement of expected results.

Measurement of the project’s progress in carrying out the activities within the identified components that

feed the M&E system will be documented in regular project progress reports. The PMUs of the subproject

cities will be responsible for the preparation of these reports. The PMUs will be fully accountable for

collecting all relevant information and data required for monitoring the implementation of DCIDP based

on the Results Framework. The reports will be prepared on a semi-annual basis and submitted to the

World Bank for review. In addition to reporting on the project results indicators and intermediate

outcome indicators, the reports will include information on disbursements; compliances with FM,

procurement, social and environmental policies and guidelines; and an updated annual plan of works and

activities. Beneficiary surveys will be carried out for all subproject cities from the year that investments

are completed and put into service, and annually thereafter. Survey results for low-income segments of

the population and female beneficiaries will be disaggregated from the overall results. The detailed

methodology and frequency for the conduct of the various M&E tools, including beneficiary surveys, will

be specified in the Project Implementation Manual (PIM). The PPCs will be required to implement the PIM

as a legal obligation under the project’s Financial Agreement, providing strong assurance that project

monitoring will be strictly implemented based on the DCIDP Results Framework.



 C. Sustainability



40.      At the project design stage, sustainability is enhanced by ensuring that technical designs are based

on thorough studies of past patterns and trends, as well as future projections relevant to the

infrastructure being developed. For example, in terms of drainage interventions, designs must consider



                                                                                                  Page 23 of 69

by Vietnamese law, will also ensure that designs best meet local needs.



41.     To promote sustainable urban development beyond the project, non-structural TA components

have been designed to build capacity in several areas. These include: (a) city-specific economic

development strategies to enhance competitiveness and provide frameworks for continued, targeted

economic growth, and (b) integrated urban planning strategies to address existing weaknesses in urban

planning processes. By bringing sectoral plans together (for example, land use planning, transport, and

other basic service infrastructure) and linking them to socio-economic development plans, budgeting and

phasing, the integrated urban planning TA aims to ensure that future urban development will be

42.     In addition, to ensure sustainability of the assets built under the project, the subproject cities will

options for involvement private sector partners, and cost-recovery mechanisms, as appropriate. Specific

appropriate arrangements for O&M—where O&M contract with qualified operators could be considered,

and (b) sufficient allocation of financial resources for proper O&M—where adequate wastewater fees

would be considered. The introduction and application of wastewater fees would also help implement the

GoV’s policies in the sector.



 IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY

 A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis



43.      Drainage and wastewater systems. The proposed investments for improving drainage and

wastewater services in the subproject cities were well studied. The improved canals/drains can improve

flow conditions and contribute to reduce floods caused by storm water in critical areas of the cities.

Computerized hydraulic models were used to analyze the status of floods and flow conditions of the

invested infrastructure. The selected technical options are consistent with the subproject cities’ long-term

development master plans and feasible for implementation. The improved wastewater systems employ

the construction of wastewater collection and treatment infrastructure that can collect wastewater

generated from households and then transport and treat it to meet the discharge standards regulated by

the GoV before discharging to the environment. The wastewater collection and transportation networks

are combined sewers in old urban setups and separated ones for new urban areas that are consistent with

the GoV’s guidance and the cities’ master plans. The improved drainage and wastewater services will lead

to improved environmental and sanitation conditions that can reduce the risks of health-related diseases,

attract tourism, and leverage economic development in the cities.



44.     There will be three treatment plants to be constructed for Ky Anh, Tinh Gia, and Hai Duong





                                                                                                   Page 24 of 69

            The World Bank

            Vietnam - Dynamic Cities Integrated Development Project (P168290)







subprojects, which will utilize well-proven technologies (biological ponds in Ky Anh and Tinh Gia, and

sequencing batch reactors in Hai Duong) that demand low levels of O&M skills and costs. CH4 emissions

were calculated following guidance provided by the Climate Action for Urban Sustainability tool (CURB

Tool version 1.0, May 2018 edition). The current CH4 emission in the project areas covered by the

treatment plants in the three cities is estimated at about 227,916 GPC per year.20 The projected CH4

emission by biological ponds in Ky Anh and Tinh Gia is 21,040 GPC per year. The projected CH4 emission

by sequencing batch reactors in Hai Duong is 65,745 GPC per year. Hence, the resulting reduction in CH4

emissions in the three cities due to the project’s investments in wastewater treatment plants is estimated

at 141,131 GPC per year.



45.     Transport. The proposed investments in strategic urban roads and bridges were assessed to

improve connectivity and reduce vehicle travel times within the subproject cities. The proposed roads and

bridge were well studied and based on traffic forecast analyses utilizing origin-destination surveys for their

function and priority, as well as scope of work. They are consistent with road network development plans

and visions of the cities to 2030 and have been approved by respective CPCs. The proposals are also

aligned with the provincial transport development plans of the provinces. The proposed items were

reviewed to avoid overlap with investment projects funded by state budget and other funds and to ensure

consistent application of social and environment policies. Technical issues and construction technologies

covered in proposed road and bridge investments are not complicated and are within the capacity of local

technical management agencies, consulting designers, and contractors. The designs are supported by

current national standards and specifications that consider climate changes issues and follow national

strategies by MONRE for climate resilience. The designs are also enhanced by the integration of public

transport and non-motorized vehicles, and traffic safety improvements.



46.     The project institutional arrangement was assessed as generally sound and follows current

national system for project management. At the provincial government level, although the Department

of Investment and Planning and Department of Finance cover issues relating to investment procedures,

the Department of Transportation is responsible for the technical management of transport items for

urban road network, and the Department of Construction is responsible for the technical management of

urban drainage system development and improvement and rainwater and wastewater drainage

construction. At the subproject level, consultants will assume responsibility for technical monitoring and

management. Local quality management system is sound at both government and project levels.



47.     Maximizing Finance for Development. DCIDP can be considered as an enabler for maximizing

finance for development in two ways. Firstly, through the structural infrastructure investments in roads

and wastewater treatment across all the subproject cities, there is potential for private sector involvement

to provide ancillary infrastructure alongside the main investments and to enter into public-private

partnerships (PPPs) with the city governments to provide O&M services. Transportation infrastructure

and wastewater treatment are among several sectors defined as eligible for PPPs under Decree 15, which

was enacted in 2015 and established a new legal framework as well as additional modalities for PPPs.



48.     Second, as illustrated in the project’s results chain, the longer-term impacts of TA activities can

provide the institutional framework for encouraging future private sector investment. The various

strategies developed under the project’s TA activities will also identify opportunities for cities to bring in

Anh and lowest at 13.1 percent for the Ninh Hai - Lach Bang Bridge in Tinh Gia. For drainage and sanitation

subprojects, the IRR was highest at 38.9 percent for the Water Drainage System of Nguyen Luong Bang

and Thanh Binh roads in Hai Duong and lowest at 11.6 percent for the Hao Gia Embankment in Yen Bai.

             evaluation was conducted using Vietnamese Dong as the unit of currency. The exchange rate

             Costs and benefits were calculated from economic prices rather than market or financial,

             prices. Each category of capital costs and O&M costs were broken down into categories such

             as traded material, non-traded material, skilled labor, unskilled labor, transfer cost (taxes,

             subsidies, levies), and others. These financial costs were converted from Vietnamese Dong

             prices. The economic costs were converted from the financial cost using conversion factor

             of 0.9 for investment costs and 0.95 for O&M costs, which are based on international border

             prices.

             capital investments and O&M costs, which are converted into economic prices. Capital

             investments include construction costs and other costs during preparation and

             implementation of construction, such as design, supervision consulting, resettlement,

             environment management, and so on. Annual O&M costs were expressed as a percentage

             periodic maintenance, and 30 percent for major repairs. For water drainage and sanitation

             subprojects, the annual O&M cost was estimated as two percent of the economic

             investment cost.

             conventional economic benefits: savings in vehicle operating cost (VOC) and savings in

               passenger travel time (Vehicle Operating Time [VOT]).21 Traffic demand was estimated using

               the System for Traffic Demand Analysis (STRADA), which is a package for transport

               forecasting that is widely used in Vietnam. The system was used to calibrate current and

               projected passenger traffic of the city road networks and to assign traffic to each of the

               project roads in the “with project” and “without project” networks. Traffic demand was

               computed from existing and projected population statistics, passenger occupancies, trip

               areas to calculate VOT per vehicle.

 V. KEY RISKS



82.     The overall risk to the project is rated as Substantial, given the Substantial ratings for several

categories, including Macroeconomic, Technical Design, Institutional and Implementation Capacity,

Fiduciary, and Environmental and Social Safeguards (see the SORT in the datasheet for detailed ratings).



83.       Macroeconomic: Substantial. Although macroeconomic performance has been strong, fiscal

constraints associated with the high public debt to GDP ratio (which at 61.4 percent is close to the 65

percent statutory debt limit, even if down from the 2016 high of 63.7 percent) pose some risk to the

attainment of project development outcomes. Over the last year, the GoV has cut back its budget

allocation to the World Bank projects to avoid taking on further debt—to the point that allocations have

in some cases been insufficient to implement project activities. In addition, the current Medium-Term

Investment Program (MTIP) for 2016–2020 of the Government has little room to accommodate additional

loan-financed activities, further constraining the allocation of budget (loan) for project activities (at least

until 2021). However, the potential impact of both of these risks should be relatively short-lived and

confined to the first few years of the project. Each of the four provinces, especially Ha Tinh and Yen Bai,

require significant extra allocations under the MTIP for the implementation of on-going projects,

potentially making it difficult to request additional allocation of on-granted ODA funds based on the

disbursement plans of the cities. This poses a substantial risk of disbursement delays for the subproject

cities in 2020, depending on the status of the ODA ceiling of their respective provinces under the current

MTIP for 2016–2020. In mitigation, the project implementation and disbursement plans of the subproject

cities have been reviewed to conservatively assume realistic levels of implementation and disbursement

in 2020, when the current MTIP is still in effect. The focus for the subproject cities will be to prepare high

quality technical designs for the infrastructure investments in 2020 so that contracts can be awarded in

2021, when the ODA ceilings of the provinces will have sufficient space to accommodate the allocation of

on-granted IDA credit to the cities.



84.      Technical design: Substantial. Overall, the technical approach and portfolio of civil works is not

expected to be complex, although the investments will be multi-sectoral in nature and be implemented

in multiple cities. The subproject cities have experience implementing similar investments financed by

other sources. Based upon previous experiences, the proposed investments were assessed to be within

the capacity of the local authority and contractors. The key risks that the subproject cities need to manage

include: (a) ensuring the alignment of the technical designs of the structural investments with the

integrated urban master plans of the cities, (b) ensuring that technical designs are responsive to climate

change risks and minimize the scope of land acquisition and impact, (c) strengthening the capacities of

the PMUs to supervise construction and ensure technical quality of the investments, and (d) developing

and implementing comprehensive O&M plans to effectively manage the structural investments. The GoV

has provided guidance to not use the credit for technical design, which may prevent the cities from

mobilizing qualified international consultant for the design services. In addition, the PMU and line

departments may not have adequate technical capacity to supervise the consultant to ensure the quality

of the technical design. The extensive program of non-structural investments under Component 2 of the

project was specifically designed to provide the subproject cities with the needed TA to mitigate these

particular risks in technical design.



85.     Institution and implementation risks: Substantial. The rating was cautiously based on the lack of

authorities in monitoring the fund flows and in meeting the financial reporting requirements given the

decentralized project design, which provides extensive flexibility and autonomy to the project owners.

Therefore, the FM risk rating was assessed as High. The following mitigation measures were identified and

will be implemented to lower the risk rating to Substantial:



        (a)   Appoint accounting staff with qualifications and experiences acceptable to the World Bank

              at all PMUs to be in charge of the project’s finance and accounting works.



        (b)   Develop and adopt an FMM for the project by all PMUs.



        (c)   Adopt upgraded accounting software (based on software currently being used in similar on-

              going World Bank projects) in line with the project’s accounting and reporting requirements.



        (d)   Conduct training for the FM staff of the PMUs on the World Bank’s FM and disbursement

              requirements and procedures.



        (e)   Provide substantial monitoring by the provincial authorities on the project financial

              reporting and expenditures. The PMUs’ annual financial statements and the final reports for

              each investment are to be approved by the Departments of Finance and PPCs within 3

              months after report submission.



89.      The procurement capacity assessment showed that the procurement risk for the proposed project

is High for the following reasons: (a) all PMUs being relatively new to the World Bank’s procurement

procedures, (b) lack of practical guidance on the steps of procurement process, (c) potential preference

to try and use national procurement rules and procedures when there is difference with the World Bank’s

procurement regulations, (d) potential fraud/corruption and collusive practices, and (e) inadequate

contract management capacity causing implementation delays and/or leading to contractual disputes.

Three major mitigation actions are identified: (a) providing regularly procurement training for PMUs’ staff,

(b) preparing and adopting a PIM, including a detailed chapter on procurement and contract

management, and (c) providing appropriate training on contract management and hands-on support to

PMUs’ staff during contract execution.

    was found to have Moderate risk to climate change and disasters overall. The cities proposed for inclusion

    under DCIDP face different climate change-related risks, given their varying geographical locations. Ky

    Anh, Thanh Hoa, and, to an extent, Hai Duong are already susceptible to typhoons because they are

    situated along or close to Vietnam’s coastline, though it is not known what the impact of climate change

    on the frequency and intensity of typhoons will be in future. However, sea level rise is expected to

    exacerbate coastal flooding and could potentially result in larger storm surges associated with typhoons.

    Yen Bai, which is situated in the more mountainous Northeast, faces heightened risks related to increased

    rainfall and landslides. Projected increases in summer and winter rainfall, runoff, rainfall variability, and

    the proportion of rain falling in heavy events will have profound implications for flooding, both in coastal

    and deltaic areas as well as in hilly terrain. The proximity of Yen Bai to the Red River also leaves the city

    exposed to floods, particularly flash floods and associated landslides during the monsoon rainy season.



    93.     Increased urbanization, population growth, and the increase in infrastructure assets along the

    coast mean that the damage potential from climate change and disasters will likely rise with time.

    Mitigation measures incorporated into design will include: (a) increasing the drainage capacity of canal

    systems, and (b) preserving green spaces for water retention within city limits. Engineering designs of all

    subprojects will incorporate climate and disaster risks factors. Importantly, the project design includes

    non-structural components that are specifically aimed at ensuring more strategic, climate change-

    informed urban planning, as well as improved management of assets to withstand shocks from extreme

    weather events or natural hazards.

                                            ANNEX 1: ECONOMIC ANALYSIS



A.        Methodology



1.       The evaluation followed the conventional economic appraisal methodology for infrastructure

improvement schemes. It compared annual discounted costs and benefits of the project to society as a

whole over the project life cycle in “with” and “without” cases using the NPV and the IRR indicators to

justify the economic viability.31 Annual project costs and benefits were evaluated for a 25-year period,

allowing for a two- to five-year construction period starting in 2019. A discount rate of 10 percent was

employed to convert annual cost and benefit streams into present value in the year 2017 according to the

recent guidelines of the World Bank in 2016.



2.      The unit prices use for estimation of project costs and benefits were based on prices in Vietnam

in 2017. The evaluation was conducted using Vietnamese Dong as the unit of currency. The exchange rate

of Vietnamese Dong 22,500: US$1 for converting the Vietnamese Dong to U.S. dollars was close to the

market rate and so no shadow foreign exchange rate was considered necessary. The economic analysis

was conducted on the basis of constant prices without taking the impact of inflation on prices into

account.



3.      For the economic evaluation, costs and benefits were calculated from economic prices rather than

market, or financial, prices. Economic prices reflect the resource cost or value of an item to the country.

Economic costs are the actual parts of the results of financial cost conversion in which market factors are

eliminated. Each category of capital costs and operation and maintenance costs were broken down into

categories such as traded material, non-traded material, skilled labor, unskilled labor, transfer cost (taxes,

subsidies, levies), and others. These financial costs were converted from Vietnamese Dong to U.S. dollar

and were multiplied with respective conversion factors, to arrive at economic prices. The economic costs were

converted from the financial cost using a conversion factor of 0.9 for investment costs and 0.95 for

operation and maintenance costs, which are based on international border prices.

1.      A baseline survey was conducted in February 2019 to assess the potential impacts on income

opportunity and access to paid work for men and women of the inclusion of adequate provision of

childcare services in the integrated urban development plans in the four project cities (Hai Duong, Tinh

Gia, Ky Anh, and Yen Bai).



A.       Methodology



2.      Both quantitative and qualitative methods were employed. The quantitative survey involved six

different groups of households to assess their demand and challenges in meeting the demands for

childcare of young children. The survey targeted females for two reasons. First, the share of female

employees in IPs is generally much higher than that of male employees. Second, time use data reveals

that, on average, women spend five hours every day on unpaid care work at the household level and

within the society, about more than two hours more than men do.33 The surveyed groups are the

following:34



         (a)    Group 1A: Households that have a child age 3–72 months attending a public care facility,

                and mothers work at an IP

         (b)    Group 1B: Households have a child age 3–72 months attending a public care facility, and

                mothers do not work at an IP

         (c)    Group 2A: Households have a child age 3–72 months attending a private care facility

                (unregistered household-based childminders), and mothers work at an IP

         (d)    Group 2B: Households have a child age 3–72 months attending a private care facility

                (unregistered household-based childminders), and mothers do not work at an IP

         (e)    Group 3A: Households have a child age 3–72 months at home, and mothers work at an IP

         (f)    Group 3B: Households have a child age 3–72 months at home, and mothers do not work at

Negative impacts of the lack of accessible and affordable childcare services



8.       In the surveyed sites, no public preschools currently accept children below 25 or 36 months or

children of migrant workers. In Hai Duong and Thanh Hoa, when a household cannot access affordable

and adequate public childcare services, a small number of mothers who work for an IP either quit their

jobs or find jobs with flexible time arrangements. However, most of them seek support from grandparents

(61 percent) or hire a nanny (24 percent). Among the women who hired a nanny, 40 percent expressed

concerns about the quality. Among the group with children taken care of by others at home (Group 3A

and B), 23 percent said that they might have to quit their job to care for children instead of outsourcing.

Nearly a quarter of this group also said they were frequently interrupted at their work in the daytime to

come home for reasons relating to childcare.



9.      In Ha Tinh and Yen Bai, large numbers of young women who are not working in IPs are self-

employed in small trading or agriculture with low and unstable incomes. If presented with the scenario to

access childcare, this group states they would prefer to stop working to take care of their children rather

than invest in private childcare services. However, they also said that they would use public childcare

services if available and if the quaility of service was acceptable.
