    I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT

    A. Country Context



1.      The Kenyan economy experienced strong, resilient, and broad-based growth over the past decade but faces

increasing macroeconomic challenges. The economy expanded at an average annual gross domestic product (GDP)

growth rate of over 5 percent between 2010 and 2019. Following a contraction in 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic,

the economy rebounded with a real GDP growth rate of 7.5 percent in 2021 and 4.8 percent in 2022,1 outpacing pre-

pandemic growth driven by broad-based increases in services and industry. The economy is expected to remain resilient

in 2023, supported by continued strong performance of the services sector and expected recovery in the agriculture

sector. However, macroeconomic prospects are dampened by challenges related to global commodity price shocks, high

interest rates, debt, and inflation.



2.      The industrial and services sectors played an important role in driving economic growth, but agriculture

continues to play a large role in Kenya’s economy, especially with respect to employment. Services contributed 54.4

percent of GDP in 2021, followed by agriculture (22.4 percent) and industry (17.0 percent). Post-pandemic growth has

been mainly driven by services and industrial output, while agricultural output contracted by 1.5 percent in the first half

of 2022 due to drought, underlining the sector’s vulnerability to climate-related risks.2 Although agriculture’s share of

employment has decreased since the mid-2000s, it remains the largest employer. Crucially, value added per worker in

agriculture remains lower than in industry and services.3



3.      Economic growth in Kenya has helped reduce poverty and inequality, but significant disparities continue to

exist. The share of the population living below the national poverty line decreased from 46.8 percent in 2005 to 36.1

percent in 2015, and further to 33.6 percent in 2019. A further 11.8 percent of the population was not poor but had a

consumption level within 20 percent of the poverty line in 2019, making them economically vulnerable to falling into

poverty in the event of an adverse economic shock. Poverty in Kenya is spatial in nature, with counties in the north and

northeast having higher, and stagnant, poverty rates. Similarly, while inequality has decreased since 2005, the pace of

decline has slowed in recent years and the Gini coefficient has slightly increased from 39.1 in 2015 to 40.7 in 2019.



    B. Sectoral and Institutional Context



The Jobs and Economic Transformation (JET) Challenge in Kenya



4.      Economic transformation in Kenya is underway, but the share of employment in low-productivity sectors and

firms remains high, stifling livelihoods and limiting inclusive growth. As mentioned previously, agricultural output as a

share of GDP has declined, in parallel with output growth in the higher-productivity industrial and service sectors.

However, this has not translated to high-quality jobs at scale: Even though overall unemployment decreased from 10

percent in 2006 to 5 percent in 2019, labor force participation decreased in recent years, dropping from 73 percent in

2015 to 69 percent in 2019. Jobs remain concentrated in sectors with low labor productivity as job creation has not

matched output growth in high-productivity sectors, and productivity growth has been limited in traditional sectors.4

Sectors such as agriculture, retail and wholesale trade, accommodation and food, education, health, and social security

account for 80 percent of employment but all have below average aggregate sector productivity. As a result, wages remain

5.       Low productivity is correlated with decreased competitiveness in labor-intensive sectors, as evidenced by

6.       Challenges in generating high-productivity employment on a large scale are also reflected at the firm level. The

private sector accounted for almost 70 percent of employment in 2021, but most private firms remain small and informal.

Of the estimated 7.4 million micro, small, or medium enterprises (MSMEs), 94 percent are formal or informal micro-sized

firms (1-4 workers), in line with the average of 97 percent across Sub-Saharan Africa.7 MSMEs are generally characterized

by low productivity, innovation, and competitiveness, with negative effects on business survival, scale-up, and productivity

(productivity is lowest in informal micro-sized firms). While there are many firms being registered in Kenya, most are not

scaling up: approximately 46 percent of Kenyan MSMEs do not survive their first year, and 80 percent fail within the first

five years, again on par with the average five-year failure rate for enterprises in Africa.8 Most quality jobs are created by

larger firms, but they are fewer in number, and MSMEs in Kenya rarely scale up to become larger firms.



7.      Women continue to face the highest barriers to employment across sectors. Kenya has made important strides

toward gender equality by developing a strong policy, regulatory, and institutional framework to support women’s

empowerment, with notable results. Gender parity in secondary school completion was achieved by 2016.9 Women’s

labor force participation rates have climbed steadily over the past few decades and stood at 73 percent in 2022, compared

to the rate for men (76 percent) and the average for women in Sub-Saharan Africa (61 percent) and globally (47.1

percent).10 Despite these advances, important disparities remain. Even with near parity at the secondary education level,

the education gender gap widens in the late teen years. By age 18-22, 18 percent of women have left school, compared

to 14 percent of men the same age.11 Partly as a result, women accounted for 72 percent of the 4 million Kenyans of

working age who were not in employment, education, or training in 2019. Where they are employed, women tend to

occupy lower-quality jobs with reduced remuneration. The proportion of working women in vulnerable employment was

69 percent in 2021, compared to 52 percent for men.12 In 2019, women accounted for only 42.8 percent of wage or

Existing Market Failures and Interventions to Address Them



8.        Low levels of firm growth, weak productivity, and eroding competitiveness in Kenya reflect various market

failures, notably regulatory barriers to entry and growth and diversification of productive firms, lack of market access

(reflecting coordination failures), and low firm capabilities (reflecting information asymmetries). In 2022, the World

Bank undertook a deep-dive analysis of value chains located in the counties of Kisii, Kirinyaga, Mandera, and Uasin Gishu.14

The analysis highlighted several constraints related to institutions, regulations, market access, enterprise support, finance,

skills, and innovation. It also highlighted potential viable investment opportunities in the edible oils (e.g., avocado pressing

line) and apparel (e.g., linking existing MSMEs to international markets). These findings echo results from earlier national-

level surveys and endline data collected under the International Finance Corporation (IFC) Kenya Investment Climate

Project III: MSME respondents to the 2018 Enterprise Survey were most likely to cite practices of the informal sector (23

percent of respondents) and access to finance (18 percent) as their biggest obstacles, and 11 percent of firms cited tax or

regulations. In turn, these perceived constraints reflect various market and government constraints: Constraints related

to market access, informality, and local competition reflect coordination failures between fragmented upstream suppliers

and buyers,15 while firm capability and capacity constraints relate to information failures regarding gap16 or adverse

selection issues that prevent viable MSMEs from obtaining financing.17



9.       Regulatory barriers associated with county-level registration and licensing processes and gaps related to the

facilitation of FDI are key constraints to Kenya’s investment climate. Most firms cite opaque, complex, duplicative, and

manual business registration and licensing processes and fees—which are often imposed at the county level—as leading

reasons for remaining informal.18 This discourages jobs in the formal sector, curtails growth in receipts from local taxes,

and exacerbates pressures on often already narrow tax bases. Currently, over 45 percent of all fees and charges go to the

county government, and most are not properly anchored in policy and a legal framework.19 Although a single Business

Permit (SBP) was introduced in 2000 to replace multiple local authority licenses, many licenses have persisted (or newly

emerged) alongside the SBP, and processes remain manual and fragmented. To date, no county has developed a Tariff

and Pricing Policy to guide imposition of fees and charges, a legal requirement under the County Governments Act, 2012.

Similarly, FDI in Kenya is held back by complex entry and licensing processes spanning multiple points of contact within

the government. While the Kenya Investment Authority (KenInvest) is the national focal point for incoming foreign

investors, investors perceive its mandate and capacity as limited given the limited implementation of its One Start-One

Stop Center for investors and ambiguity in the legal, regulatory, and strategic framework for FDI in Kenya, which was

drafted before devolution and currently creates confusion between KenInvest and county-level units.20

10.     Further, there are challenges related to access to markets and linkages across sectors and businesses. The main

markets reported by most businesses across all regions in the country are individuals rather than downstream

businesses.21 This reflects limited value chain linkages attributable to coordination failures between large downstream

buyers and potential MSME suppliers.22 MSMEs presently have limited capacity to meet standards and order volumes

required by buyers, but they are also not incentivized to invest in this capacity without confidence in being able to sell

their output to buyers, or they face market failures (i.e., adverse selection) in obtaining financing for upgrades and capacity

expansion. Identifying and building linkages across more skill-intensive sub-sectors such as agriculture thus offers an

opportunity for the growth of firms and jobs. Nevertheless, emerging local agglomerations of MSMEs in the same value

chain (commonly referred to as “clusters? in Kenya) demonstrate potential to serve as focal points for aggregation and

market linkages. In the Kenyan legal context, such clusters may take the form of MSME associations23 or agricultural

cooperatives. As of 2023, there are over 1,200 MSME associations and 7,700 agricultural cooperatives in Kenya.



11.     On the supply side, Kenyan firms must also improve their capabilities to access new markets and generate high-

quality jobs, notably with respect to product standards and technology adoption as well as business skills, such as

marketing and financial management. The Firm-level Adoption of Technology Survey finds that firms in Kenya are far

from the technological frontier, relying primarily on basic digital technologies to perform general business functions.

Adoption of technologies is very heterogenous and associated with firm size, highlighting a duality between sophisticated

enterprises and a larger number of unproductive firms.24 The persistence of this dispersion reflects many MSMEs’ lack of

knowledge that such gaps exist in the first place (i.e., information failures). In other cases, firms must invest in equipment

and infrastructure (e.g., processing machinery) to improve productivity and quality of output, but they often lack access

to finance or sufficient scale at the individual firm level for such investments.



12.      Exacerbating these challenges, climate change poses a significant and growing risk, especially for MSMEs. Kenya

is one of the most vulnerable and least ready countries to respond to climate change, ranking 150 out of 185 in the Notre

Dame Global Adaptation Initiative (ND-GAIN) Country Index.25 According to the Climate Risk Profile for Kenya,

temperatures in Kenya are projected to rise by 1.7°C by the 2050s and by approximately 3.5°C at the end of the century.

Sea level rise is also projected to cause a significant risk to the five coastal counties. Climate change will increase the risk

and intensity of natural hazards, including floods and droughts. Intense rainfall and flooding will also increase the

likelihood of mudslides and landslides, particularly in mountainous areas. Natural hazards will have devastating

socioeconomic impacts, especially on MSMEs. For example, climate hazards can lead to business disruptions for MSMEs

in sectors that rely on natural resources (e.g., agriculture). MSMEs located in vulnerable regions (e.g., cities that are

exposed to coastal flooding) may also face material losses to capital and infrastructure. Compared to large companies,

MSMEs often have far less resources and expertise to prepare and respond to these climate shocks. The transition toward

a low carbon economy also poses risks for MSMEs. Even though climate mitigation actions offer important benefits for

MSMEs26 and policies, new technologies and changes in investor and consumer sentiment are enhancing the demand for

these practices, MSMEs lack the expertise and access to funding to support transition to cleaner models of operation.

13.     Adequate incentives are thus needed to close the finance gap to meet Kenya’s climate goals and contribute to

sustainable and inclusive growth. According to Kenya’s 2020 updated Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), the

estimated cost of Kenya’s mitigation and adaptation actions stands at KES 6,775 billion (US$65 million) in 2020-2030.

Currently, climate finance in Kenya primarily comes from external loans and grants from international public institutions,

with significant contributions from the national government. In 2018, public and private climate finance flows amounted

to KES 243.3 billion (US$2.4 billion), roughly representing only one-third of the financing that Kenya needs annually to

meet the targets set in its NDC.28 Climate finance for MSMEs is especially limited: climate hazards reduce the reliability of

collateral and reduce firm productivity, and impacts are heightened for MSMEs—which already lack financial access in

many cases—given the vulnerabilities described above. Although traditional banking products are available to most formal

enterprises, they often come at a high cost and are short-term in nature, making them unsuitable for many climate

projects which have long payback periods and high upfront costs.29 Thus, there may be opportunities for Kenya to diversify

financial product offerings by tapping into patient capital (e.g., private equity investments) for green firms and

investments, especially for medium sized firms.



14.     The focus on financing small and medium enterprises (SMEs) in Kenya has been largely debt-driven from both

the private and public sector, and equity finance for SMEs is severely lacking. Debt finance dominates various initiatives

for SMEs and such funding options for SMEs in Kenya range from microfinance banks, digital lenders, Savings and Credit

Cooperative (SACCO), commercial banks, and public sector initiatives, yet the inherent risks associated with SMEs have

resulted in high debt pricing and limited affordability and accessibility. Equity finance aligns higher risks with high potential

returns for SMEs with a longer term but remains significantly low in Kenya. Public equities (stock markets) are not a

mainstream financing source for SMEs given the higher entry bars. Private equity funding is limited in Kenya, with most

funds having a regional mandate and thus a limited allocation to Kenya. Challenges for private equity funds in African

countries including Kenya include high target returns to compensate the high (perceived) risk of investing in businesses

with unproven track records; high target investment tickets to reduce costs; limited exit options; and limited deal flow

and the costs associated. Nevertheless, Kenya remains a promising investment location for Private Equity in Africa, coming

in third after South Africa and Nigeria with investments totaling US$1.2 billion between 2017 and 2019, with deals

concentrated in agribusiness, financial services, and fast-moving consumer goods.30 Against this backdrop, demonstrating

the viability of a Kenya-focused private equity fund targeting green/greening firms and ticket size that is underfinanced

could help address finance gaps and unlock the potential of private equity (more details in the project’s Technical Design

Details Note).

Institutional Context



16.      The JET agenda is a key focus area of the government’s Vision 2030, its Fourth Medium-term Plan (MTP4, 2023-

and Small Enterprise Authority (MSEA) is housed under the State Department of MSME Development (SDMSME) in the

Ministry of Cooperatives, and Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises Development (MCMSME) and is responsible for

formulating, implementing, and evaluating policies and programs for Micro and Small Enterprises, including research,

product development, patenting, access to markets, innovation, technology development and transfer, capacity building

and others. The proposed project would complement and strengthen these efforts via a whole-of-government approach

to drive the private sector and MSME agenda in Kenya.

         MSMEs that have created formal jobs and are exposed to severe climate shocks. First, it would unlock capital for

         MSMEs that have productive potential but are constrained in their borrowing by exposure to climatic disasters

         primarily drought and floods. Second, it would increase the expected survivability of businesses that are already

         leveraged and would have otherwise been viable had the disaster not occurred. Initially the target recipients are

         expected to be smaller MSMEs with the potential for productive job creation but vulnerable to climate shocks.

         KDC will be the implementing agency for this sub-component and will collaborate with MSEA to ensure the

         MSMEs supported under component 2 will benefit from the Facility. TA to KDC during the project implementation

         will explore the possibility of using additional financial instruments such as insurance to scale the Facility further.

         A broad framework for eligibility criteria for MSMEs and Participating Financial Institutions (PFIs) is included in

         Annex 3 of the PAD to guide a comprehensive framework that is to be detailed in the POM. This financing will be

         offered only once to eligible beneficiary firms—in the form of concessional loans for specific purposes for a well

         specified period—and will be triggered by external climatic events, including droughts and floods, using objective

         indices precisely described in these lending terms and to be defined in POM. These indices should be based on

         data that are: (a) timely and regular, so that funding can be released quickly after an event; (b) relevant, so that

         the Facility offers reliable and impactful protection; (c) objective and possible to audit, to avoid subjective

         analysis; (d) cost-effective; and (e) available over a long time horizon so that the likelihoods of triggers can be

         analyzed to support budget requirements. For example, triggers could be based on a regional weather index for

         drought or flood issued by a national or international or other specific and credible triggers that will be detailed

         in POM. Subcomponent 3.2 complements Subcomponent 3.1, which targets the need for capital investments for

 D. Results Chain



28.      The theory of change of the proposed project stipulates that: (i) the implementation of broad-based targeted

reforms and support programs will reduce the costs of doing business for the private sector and increase access to markets

for firms unlocking private sector investment; (ii) the funding of catalytic and transformative co-investments in common

services and infrastructure for MSMEs, complemented by firm-level BDS, will increase the competitiveness of targeted

growth-oriented clusters; and (iii) the increase in available funding for green MSMEs will increase sustainable investments,

green and circular economy practices, reduce the environmental footprint of, and increase resilience of the private sector.

The development of needed common infrastructure and services could create high-productivity jobs, while the growth in

supported dynamic higher-value added clusters could absorb labor reallocated from less productive sectors and improve

jobs. Support programs and interventions that improve firm productivity would also improve the quality of jobs within

MSMEs, while support for growth-oriented SMEs would increase high quality job creation. Targeted investments in green

firms and practices will additionally support greening of existing jobs and creation of new green jobs. The interlinked

project interventions would thus contribute to the creation of new jobs and an improvement in overall job quality,

resilience, and environmental sustainability.

necessary preconditions, the need for a governance structure based on private sector practices, and the need to focus on

clear market gaps. Identified preconditions include a relatively developed PE/VC industry and ideally a certain maturity of

domestic capital markets, or at least an accommodative legal and regulatory framework to allow for offshore structure.

 A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis



31.      The project will improve multiple aspects of the competitiveness of the MSME sector, delivering a net positive

impact for the economy of Kenya. For Component 1, expected benefits include decreased time and costs spent dealing

with government business regulation and decreased costs and time required for firms to access domestic or international

markets. The reduced cost of doing business and trading is expected to lead to the entry of new firms, which can generate

profits and new jobs, as well as increased revenue, productivity, and export-orientation in existing firms. For Component

2, expected benefits include increased linkages of MSMEs with firms (including export-oriented and foreign-owned firms)

and clustering of key sectors through various agglomeration models, increased investment in common goods and services,

and increased firm capabilities and export orientation, which will contribute to increases in revenue, value addition, and

job creation, ultimately contributing to a long-term decrease in poverty rates. For Component 3, expected benefits include

a crowding in of private financing contributing to build resilience and increase green firms, jobs, and investments that will

contribute to Kenya’s climate mitigation and adaptation priorities.



32.      A detailed economic analysis based on direct and indirect costs and benefits was conducted for subcomponents

with clearly attributable and quantifiable benefits at the individual beneficiary level. The costs and benefits of each

component were estimated separately for Component 2 and Subcomponent 3.1. Separate estimates of the impacts should

provide a lower bound on the overall benefits as total project benefits are deemed to exceed those of individual

components through their interdependencies and spillovers (e.g., calculation would not account for synergies generated

if GIF investees under Subcomponent 3.1 have commercial relationships with participants of Component 2).



33.      The analysis of Component 2 and Subcomponent 3.1 accounts for the following benefits:



      (a) Increased profits (resulting from revenue increases) of MSMEs due to improved capacity from BDS and co-

          investment under Component 2;



      (b) Wages (less opportunity costs) of new incremental workers directly employed by MSMEs supported under

          Component 2 as a result of increased revenue/activity (i.e., direct jobs);



      (c) Wages (less opportunity costs) of new incremental workers employed by suppliers and buyers of MSMEs

          supported under Component 2 as a result of increased sourcing and input provision by supported MSMEs (i.e.,

          indirect jobs);



      (d) Increased profits (resulting from revenue increases) of created by SMEs from receiving investment from the GIF;



      (e) Wages (less opportunity costs) of new incremental workers directly employed by SMEs receiving investment

          from the GIF as a result of increased revenue/activity (i.e., direct jobs); and



      (f) Wages (less opportunity costs) of new incremental workers employed by suppliers and buyers of SMEs receiving

          investment from the GIF as a result of increased sourcing and input provision by GIF investees (i.e., indirect jobs).



34.      In line with the approach of economic analyses from prior MSME support projects (e.g., Zambia Agribusiness

and Trade Project-II P179507), the analysis accounts for the following cash flows to the project: a) incremental cash

flows to beneficiary MSMEs resulting from project-driven revenue increases; and b) incremental wages from direct and

indirect jobs created due to the project. The cost-benefit analysis was carried out over 10 years and at a discount rate of

12 percent in line with other similar projects in Kenya and in the Eastern and Southern Africa region.

35.     Under the conservative base case assumptions, the combined economic internal rate of return (EIRR) for

Component 2 and Subcomponent 3.1 is 25.8 percent, and the project generates an economic net present value (NPV)

of US$98 million over 10 years at a 12 percent discount rate. Component 2 is projected to generate an EIRR of 28.8

percent and NPV of US$82.6 million. These figures come from base assumptions of 1,200 subprojects supported with BDS,

600 subprojects further supported with co-investment, and beneficiary revenue growth rate uplift from the project of 3

and 5 percentage points, respectively, for BDS alone and BDS plus co-investment.56 These figures are conservative relative

to the prior experience of MSEA and similar past projects such as the Zambia Agribusiness and Trade Project (ZATP.

P156492), which typically observed uplift rates above 10 percentage points from BDS and financing. Subcomponent 3.1 is

expected to generate an EIRR of 19.0 percent57 and NPV of US$15.4 million. These figures come from base case

assumptions of 12 investees receiving investments of US$4.7 million each (implying matching by GIF LPs of US$38 million

on top of the project’s US$38 million) and revenue uplift from equity investment of 5 percentage points.58 These two

components remain viable under a wide range of parameters in sensitivity analyses, demonstrating the robustness of their

rationale.



36.     Economic and financial analysis for Component 1 presents challenges due to the indirect relationship between

the business environment and investment policy and promotion reforms supported and the stream of benefits that

these reforms are expected to trigger. In the absence of commonly accepted methodologies for economic analysis of

these types of investments, such projects are based on cost-effectiveness and the well-substantiated literature tying

business environment reforms to private sector growth and entrepreneurship. A qualitative analysis of the literature has

been included for Component 1 to provide further support for this component.



37.      The economic and financial analysis for Subcomponent 3.2 is based on scenario analyses and will be further

refined via a subsequent technical FS. Large number of MSMEs in Kenya are exposed to shocks, without access to finance.

The main premise of the component is that with increased resilience and survivability, MSMEs will be able to make longer-

term plans, including investments in more and higher quality jobs. To assess the investment multiplier of the project the

team are undertaking a FS to inform assumptions on the level of investments between high return innovation and low

return traditional investments within the MSME agriculture sector. The Team will then model the increased investments

in high returning innovation, following reduced liquidity risks related to climate shocks. Public funding is needed to bridge

the supply gap of the risk-averse financial sector, which underinvests in MSMEs due to their high exposure to climatic risk.

                                   ANNEX 1: Implementation Arrangements and Support Plan

1.      Implementing Ministries: The project will be implemented through (i) MITI which will implement Component 1

(Strengthening Business and Investment Enabling Reforms) and Component 3 (Scaling up Green Financing and

Strengthening Climatic Resilience for SMEs) and (ii) MCMSME which will implement Component 2 (Enhancing MSME

Cluster Competitiveness).



2.      Project Implementation Units and Teams: Two dedicated PIU structures—one in each ministry—will be leveraged

for day-to-day management of the project. SDMSME under MCMSME will coordinate Component 2 and provide oversight

for MSEA to implement it. A PIU under SDMSME will be established and will act as the anchor PIU in charge of overall

reporting, missions, operational and fiduciary planning, and updating the overall Project Operations Manual (POM), on

top of supporting Component 2 implementation. The PIU in SDMSME will provide a dedicated team to support project

implementation, including a Fiduciary Specialist, Climate Change and Environmental and Social Specialist, M&E Specialist,

Communication Specialist, and other staff on an as-needed basis to be detailed in the POM. MSEA will further provide a

dedicated implementation team, comprising of Value Chain Specialist(s), Legal Specialist(s), Fiduciary Specialist(s), and

other staff on an as-needed basis to be detailed in the POM. SDIP will coordinate Components 1 and 3, providing oversight

for KenInvest and KDC, respectively, to implement. A PIU under SDIP will be established to support implementation of

Components 1 and 3. The PIUs will develop annual performance targets, in consultation with the Project Coordinator and

approved by the respective Principal Secretaries and the World Bank. The PIU under MCMSME will act as the anchor PIU

in charge of coordinating reporting, missions, operational and fiduciary planning, as well as updating the overall project

operational manual.



3.       Project Steering Committee: To support project implementation, KJET’s governance structure will include a PSC.

The POM will define the membership of the PSC and regularity of its meetings. The PSC will be chaired by the Principal

Secretary (PS) of SDMSME under MCMSME and co-chaired by PS of SDIP under MITI. As dictated by GoK’s PIM guidelines,

the PSC will assume oversight over project implementation functions and PIUs including, among other functions, (i)

oversight of overall implementation of the project; (ii) provision of supervision support, including monitoring project

results; (iii) ensuring of inter-agency coordination within GoK that is required for project implementation; and (iv) review

and approval of annual work plans and budgets.

17.     Use of national procurement procedures. All contracts following the national market approach shall follow the

procedures set out in the Public Procurement and Asset Disposal Act, 2015 (PPADA). The PPADA governs the purchase of

works, goods, and services using public resources by the national and county government entities, local authorities, state

corporations, education institutions, and other GoK institutions. The PPADA sets out the rules and procedures of public

procurement and provides a mechanism for enforcement of the law. Under the PPADA, the PPRA was established along

with the Public Procurement Directorate in the NT. The PPRA has oversight and regulatory functions, including undertaking

procurement reviews and audits. There is a Public Procurement Complaints Review and Appeals Board (Appeal Board)

under the secretariat of the PPRA that deals with complaints received from bidders or consulting firms.

procurement activities will support the development objectives of the program to deliver the best value for money under

a risk-based approach. The PPSD covered the identification of specific program needs and summary of procurement

contracts, assessment of program operational context and implementing agency procurement capacity, market research

                                          ANNEX 2: Jobs Definition and Measurement

1.       From a theory of change perspective, the KJET project is expected to generate new and higher-paying jobs

through increased revenue and improved firm-level productivity, primarily through the direct jobs channel (i.e., among

beneficiary firms) for Components 2 and 3. BDS and co-investment provided to MSME clusters under Component 2 is

expected to increase MSMEs’ revenue and efficiency through improvements in business practices, expansion in productive

capacity, and/or linkages with new downstream buyers. This will in turn increase MSMEs’ demand for labor (new job

creation) and/or improve existing workers’ productivity (improved jobs) to meet new demand. Similarly, financing

provided under Component 3 is expected to finance green productive capacity of investees, driving up demand for labor

and/or worker productivity. Most priority value chains for Component 2—the largest component in terms of funding and

number of beneficiaries reached—are likely to be in agribusiness, where there is significant headroom to increase

productivity, but not necessarily number of workers given land constraints. As such, the largest jobs impacts are expected

to be in terms of job improvements of existing workers.



2.       Two main jobs-related indicators have been proposed to measure these impacts:



     (a) Jobs created at beneficiary firms: Net increase in Full time Equivalence (FTE) employment at beneficiary firms

         (MSMEs in beneficiary clusters for Component 2 and investees for Component 3); and



     (b) Jobs improved at beneficiary firms: Number of preexisting FTEs at MSMEs at beneficiary firms that experienced

         worker productivity growth (defined as revenue/FTE) above 5 percent. While the latter measure may not perfectly

         account for differential distribution of pay increases across employees of a given firm, the productivity growth

         threshold of 5 percent is set sufficiently high such that job improvements could feasibly be expected to extend to

         all workers. MSME survey data in Kenya suggest that larger firms are actually less productive on average, with a 1

         percent increase in workforce associated with a 0.09 percent decrease in labor productivity. Relative to the

         (negative) baseline, the 5 percent threshold is also roughly in line with labor productivity impact estimates from

         MSME projects with similar BDS and asset investment interventions.65 Although the project will gather data and

         report on both indicators, targets are set for new and improved jobs combined given uncertainty over whether

         individual beneficiary firms will serve increased sales by increasing staffing or productivity.



3.       KJET could also generate indirect jobs through the consumption (higher demand for goods and services by

benefitting workers) and backward and forward factor usage channels (higher demand for and supply and inputs by

beneficiary MSMEs); however, these impacts are not incorporated into the results framework (although forward and

backward linkages are incorporated in the economic analysis). This is due to challenges with direct measurement of

indirect impacts and in the spirit of conservatively estimating impact. Similarly, while comparing beneficiaries’ outcomes

relative to the counterfactual is critical for robust impact assessment, the results framework does not directly incorporate

a counterfactual due to challenges with direct measurement. Estimation of indirect impacts and counterfactuals could be

explored in separate analytical work, albeit not as part of the formal results framework. Subject to securing additional

resources, parallel analyses could leverage collaboration with the GOK, development partners, and researchers.

                                         ANNEX 3: Private Capital Mobilization





1.       Subcomponent 2.2 will generate PCM through its co-investment arrangements with the project. Co-investment

arrangements entail beneficiary MSMEs and MSME clusters matching a fixed portion of project funding upfront to buy a

specific piece of equipment or machinery as approved under the project (see discussion on Component 2 beneficiary

selection and implementation details). The beneficiary MSME and MSME clusters are all expected to be private entities

carrying out business purposes and financially and managerially independent from the government. The KJET project will

play an active role in mobilizing the private co-financing as the plan to purchase the equipment or machinery will be

developed under and approved by—with contracts as evidence—the project. Thus, all the private co-financing used to

match project funding will fall under the harmonized MDB definition of PCM. As co-investees will be required to match

at least 30 percent of project investment costs upfront, the team estimates PCM of US$0.3 for every US$1 of co-

investment from the project in its targets for the results framework, implying total PCM from Subcomponent 2.2 of roughly

US$20 million given expected co-investment outlay of US$66 million.



2.       Subcomponent 3.1 will also generate PCM through participation in the GIF by private LPs investing alongside

KDC and through investment deal level co-financing. The non-KDC LPs are expected to be a mix of private investors (e.g.,

private pension funds), Development Finance Institutions (DFIs), and/or sovereign wealth funds. KJET will play an active

and direct role in mobilizing such co-financing as it will: (a) play a direct role in the design and setup of the GIF; and (b)

support KDC’s participation as an anchor LP in the GIF. Private LPs will thus fall under the harmonized MDB definition of

PCM. Investment by DFIs and sovereign wealth funds participating as LPs will fall under the definition of PCM if they are:

(a) financially and managerially autonomous vis-à-vis government; and (b) not participating in joint reporting by MDBs

and DFIs on PCM (i.e., no double-counting). LPs that do not meet both criteria will not be counted toward PCM for project

purposes. The team estimates a target PCM ratio of US$0.43 for every US$1.00 of project investment for results

framework purposes, based on initial investor feedbacks and capital structures of other PE funds suggested by the FS.

Additionally, there is potential to generate PCM at the deal level if private investors who are not LPs in the GIF invest

alongside it. The team will capture such PCM insofar as it is line with the harmonized MDB definition, but it has not factored

such impacts into the results frameworks targets as it is not feasible to predict deal-level co-investment at this stage.

Based on market sounding with potential investors during the GIF FS, the team is targeting private LP contributions (and

therefore PCM) of US$17 million for Subcomponent 3.1.

                                ANNEX 4: Financial Intermediary Financing Assessment



1.        The Kenya Jobs and Economic Transformation (KJET) Project includes the provision of financial services,

including (i) a green investment facility offering equity and mezzanine financing (which begins as debt and may change

to equity over time), (ii) a blended finance facility for green and climate resilience investments of individual MSMEs,

and (iii) co-investment in productive equipment for organized clusters of MSMEs and potentially individual MSMEs. An

FIF review is required to ensure that good practices, lessons from World Bank experience, and policy requirements of the

World Bank are reflected in the design of the financial activities.



2.       Macroeconomic environment and fiscal situation. The average inflation rate for August 2023 (yoy) is 6.7 percent,

with a short spike of about 10 percent during the election period. Over the past five years, inflation has remained in single

digits, making the baseline interest rate for financial intermediation reasonable. While Kenya is on a path to high debt

distress, with support from the IMF the national debt began to stabilize at 68 percent of GDP, even with government

support programs for COVID-19.



3.      Institutional arrangements and products – Co-investment in clusters (Sub-component 2.2). The project will

provide partial financing for equipment per the specific technical needs of clusters and, in some cases, firms with strong

forward or backward linkages. The governance structure of the investment facility will enable individual investment

decisions to be made by an experienced fund manager independent from the State and other investors. The value of the

equity portfolio should be designed to protect the value of the capital of the facility. To ensure the quality of sub-projects,

the project provides TA to competitive cluster development (2.1) and business development services (BDS, in 2.2). Each

subproject will have as its beneficiary touchpoint a cluster of MSMEs (i.e., legally organized agglomeration of MSMEs in

the same value chain and geographic area or, in select special cases, a single firm (e.g., firms with significant backward or

forward linkages or export potential, women-owned/led business). The BDS provider will be competitively selected and

support the cluster or firm with a diagnostic and support on financial management, strategy, and marketing. Co-

investment will initially begin in the edible oil, apparel, and construction/building materials value chains, with further

expansion at the project team’s discretion based on the outcomes of Subcomponent 2.1.



4.      The MSEA of the MCMSME is responsible for coordinating MSME and entrepreneurship activities. The MCMSME

manages the Financial Inclusion Fund (the ‘hustler fund’), a digital financial inclusion initiative to improve financial access.

Under the MSME Act of 2012, MSEA is mandated to coordinate, facilitate development and regulation of micro and small

enterprises with the goal of increasing access to technical capacity building, finance, technology, markets, and government

services. MSME has developed or refurbished 158 micro and small enterprise infrastructure sites, and 40 have been

equipped with common user machinery, using regional MSEA staff. Since MSEA is already an implementing agency of the

World Bank’s National Youth Opportunities Towards Advancement Project, and KYEOP, it has operational systems in place

that meet World Bank standards and will receive TA from the project to adopt existing systems to this innovative portfolio.



5.       Institutional arrangements and products – GIF. The project will lend to KDC who will then invest as a LP into the

GIF. The KDC offers debt and equity financing, as well as advisory services. It was created in late 2020, through a merger

of operations of the Industrial and the Commercial Development Corporation, Tourism Finance Corporation and IDB

Capital Limited. It only accounts for 0.6 percent of assets and 0.2 percent of loans in the banking system. KDC is a board

run company, wholly owned by the GoK, with majority of directors being independent. It has audit and risk committee,

HR and Governance committee and Finance and investment committee, chaired by independent directors. It intends to

hold a minority share of the GIF, to ensure that national climate commitments are met. The KDC will be a catalytic investor.

As of June 2022, the KDC’s financial performance was strong, with operating profit at 38 percent, and return on assets at

6.       The GIF would be a newly established fund to provide risk-adjusted long-term equity and debt for eligible

MSMEs with green productive sub-projects. This is a key part of the Government’s support to greening the private sector.

The GIF will finance green enterprises, the greening of existing SMEs, and adoption of recycling practices. PBCs will be

used to gradually support the legal establishment and operational systems to develop a green MSME pipeline.

Management of the fund will be outsourced to a professional fund manager competitively selected, and with knowledge

of the targeted sectors. The project operational manual will include technical and financial due diligence processes to

identify eligible firms and eligible sub-projects. There will be a timebound exit strategy from equity investments. The fund

will be fully owned by KDC at inception, as KDC intends to crowd in capital from private sector and other investors as

founding shareholders of the fund including possible foreign investors. KDC intends to become a minority shareholder in

this fund in the medium term. Project funds will provide blended finance, given the strong public goods nature of firms,

whether new or adding green processes and products.



7.       Institutional arrangements and products – Strengthening MSMEs Climatic Resilience (US$3 million). An

innovative pilot will be launched to enhance the climate resilience of MSMEs by using blended financing to boost MSMEs'

liquidity and minimize their default risk following external climate-related shocks. This blended approach is appropriate,

given the public good content and asset and job protection that climate resilience can provide. KDC will act as an apex

financial intermediary and will extend short term maturity loans (would be concessionally priced debt financing) and

available only once per firm. Firms would graduate from this facility to apply for commercial financing. This fund will be

managed with long term commercial viability in mind. While initial pricing may be relatively low, they will be above the

rate of inflation to protect the value of the investments.



8.      Monitoring and evaluation. The key performance indicators for the three FIF activities at the project and

disaggregated levels will include (i) the number of firms participating in GIF financing, (ii) the number of PFIs offering

climatic shock responsive financing for SMEs, and (iii) a measurement of portfolio quality (most likely non-performing

loans).



9.       Coordination with the IFC. Given the focus on private capital mobilization and the use of equity (sub-components

2.2 and 3.1) and contingent disaster risk financing (DRF) (sub-component 3.2) to support MSMEs, coordination with the

IFC is important. The team has been in ongoing discussions and consultation with the IFC team since the beginning of the

concept, for potential IFC investment and advisory support as well as IFC views to inform project design. Sub-components

supporting co-investment (2.2) activity and DRF (3.2) are targeting clusters of firms and vulnerable yet viable firms

(consecutively) that may not be strong candidates for IFC’s equity investments. As firms and clusters mature with support

from the three FIF activities, they may become potential clients of the IFC. For sub-component 3.1, although IFC is unable

to invest in this fund at this stage (due to its decision to focus on Pan-African/regional funds instead of a single country

funds in Africa), IFC remains open for investee level co-investments and/or advisory support. In addition, GIF keeps the

option of future fund investment despite of its current focus on direct investments, including potential investments in IFC

funds with a significant allocation to or a focus on Kenya, if such opportunities present and are evaluated as appropriate

by the selected fund manager.
