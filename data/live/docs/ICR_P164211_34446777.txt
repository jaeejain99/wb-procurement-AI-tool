 I. PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES

     A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL

 Context



1.        In the decade leading up to the Ghana Tourism Development Project (GTDP), Ghana’s economy experienced

robust growth, elevating the country to lower middle-income status. Ghana successfully held seven consecutive free and

fair elections, with three peaceful transfers of power, underscoring its political stability. Rapid economic growth and

structural transformation reshaped Ghana’s economic landscape, with the services sector, driven by transportation, banking,

and retail, boosting its contribution to Gross Domestic Product (GDP) from 32 percent in 2000 to around 57 percent by 2016.

The industry sector, including mining and quarrying, held the second-largest GDP share at 24.3 percent in 2016. This strong

economic performance supported progress in poverty reduction and job creation.



2.        However, Ghana's economic growth began to slow as the country faced significant macroeconomic shocks that

intensified fiscal imbalances and adversely impacted the real sector. The GDP growth rate, which had reached 8.1 percent

in 2017, slowed to 6.3 percent in 2018 due to these challenges. Ghana’s economy, heavily dependent on key commodity

exports, was vulnerable to price fluctuations. Gold prices dropped from $1,600 per ounce in 2012 to $1,073 in 2015, while

oil prices fell from $91 per barrel in 2012 to $35 per barrel in 2015.



3.        In response to these economic challenges, the new government elected in December 2016 committed to

diversifying Ghana’s economy beyond its reliance on a few primary commodities. Tourism was identified as a key sector

for economic diversification and job creation, particularly benefiting women and youth in rural areas. As an essential driver

of foreign exchange and tax revenue, tourism made substantial contributions to Ghana’s economy. From 2007 to 2017,

international tourist arrivals grew from 580,000 to 980,141, largely due to business tourism. Tourism receipts also increased

from US$879 million to US$1.8 billion during this period. Nature-based tourism, including ecotourism in parks like Kakum

and Mole, positively impacted local employment and community incomes, especially in coastal areas. By 2016, tourism

accounted for three percent of Ghana’s GDP and provided direct employment to nearly 288,000 people, underscoring its role

in economic growth and environmental sustainability in tourist areas. With its rich cultural and natural attractions, Ghana

became a key destination for high-spending, environmentally conscious travelers. Nature-based tourism, ecotourism, and

cultural experiences emerged as leading segments, with ecotourism comprising 35 percent of travel activities by 2017. The

growth of eco-lodges, like Zaina in Mole National Park, highlighted this market’s potential. Domestic tourism also grew,

fueled by middle-class Ghanaians visiting coastal and park destinations.



4.       With its emphasis on economic diversification, investment generation, and foreign exchange attraction, the

Project was well-aligned with both the Government of Ghana’s priorities and the World Bank’s strategy in Ghana. It aligned

with the Country Partnership Strategy (CPS) for FY13-16 (extended to FY18), which advocated for a Private Sector

Development Strategy addressing competitiveness in sectors with high diversification potential, including tourism. The

project also supported the Government’s Coordinated Program of Economic and Social Policies (2017), which identified

tourism as a priority, and aligns with national frameworks such as the National Tourism Development Plan (2013–2027), and

the Long-term National Development Plan (2018–2057). These frameworks consistently emphasized tourism as a strategic

sector for economic growth, employment, revenue and foreign exchange generation, environmental conservation, and

fostering national cohesion.



5.        To achieve these objectives, the Project aimed to address key barriers impeding the growth of Ghana’s tourism

sector. These included high business costs, complex regulations, costly air travel, visa issues, and limited domestic air

transport options. The sector was further constrained by a shortage of skilled workers and a lack of standardized tourism

training. Additionally, Ghana’s tourism assets, including forts and national parks, were underdeveloped and poorly

maintained, while a weak marketing strategy hindered its competitiveness with neighboring countries. Tackling these

challenges, strengthening the enabling environment, and boosting private sector investment were seen as vital steps to

advancing Ghana's leisure tourism sector and unlocking its economic development potential.



 Theory of Change (Results Chain)



6.         The project’s Theory of Change (TOC) aimed to enhance tourism performance by strengthening the enabling

environment, developing tourism sites and destinations, and supporting tourism enterprises. The Project was designed to

improve the tourism sector's economic impact and appeal by strengthening the supporting environment, enhancing tourism

destinations, and promoting local enterprise. By increasing the skills and qualifications of tourism workers, upgrading

facilities, and improving tourism sites, the project aimed to elevate Ghana's tourism standards to compete globally. This

included activities such as establishing tourism training facilities, advertising Ghana's tourism potential, and upgrading both

publicly and privately owned tourist sites.



7.        Additionally, the project emphasized the development and support of Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) in

the tourism sector through business training, matching grants, and access to investment. This was intended to foster

private sector growth, increase job creation, and boost revenue within the tourism sector. The long-term goals included

higher private investment, increased tourism revenue through longer visitor stays, and improved tourist satisfaction. The

project’s outcomes were aligned to create a more attractive and sustainable tourism industry that would contribute

significantly to Ghana's economy.

 Project Development Objectives (PDOs)

8.       The project development objective was to improve the performance of tourism in targeted destinations in Ghana.



 Key Expected Outcomes and Outcome Indicators

9.       Key expected outcomes: The project sought to improve the performance of the tourism sector in targeted

 Components

11.     The project had been designed around four components to address the government of Ghana's goal of promoting

the tourism sector, particularly the leisure segment. The components were: (i) strengthening the tourism enabling

environment; (ii) developing tourism sites and destinations; (iii) supporting tourism enterprises; and (iv) project

management and institutional strengthening.



 Component 1: Strengthening the Tourism Enabling Environment (SDR 10.4 million - US$15 million equivalent)



12.      This component was designed to strengthen Ghana's tourism enabling environment, addressing key constraints

in training and skills development, aviation and visa policies, and branding and marketing . Under tourism skills

development, a sustainable business model for training facilities was to be implemented, along with support for curriculum

development and teacher training. Aviation and visa policy reforms included attracting air travel operators and implementing

streamlined visa systems to enhance leisure and business tourism. Additionally, a comprehensive national branding and

marketing strategy was created, incorporating digital promotion and targeting key tourism segments, with support for its

implementation and monitoring.



 Component 2: Developing tourism sites and destinations (SDR 10.4 million - US$15 million equivalent)



13.       This component aimed to diversify Ghana's leisure tourism by identifying and upgrading promising destination

areas with significant public goods requirements and potential to attract both domestic and international visitors.

Intended activities included supporting destination planning and implementing access, safety, and quality improvements.

Key activities included identifying priority destination areas and developing detailed destination management plans through

stakeholder consultations. A site upgrades program was also planned to improve quality, access, and safety at a minimum of

five publicly owned sites through small infrastructure works such as signage, fencing, utilities, and walkways. For non-publicly

owned sites, matching grants were intended to support quality upgrades. In addition, the component aimed to provide

training on site management and maintenance to all selected sites and conduct assessments of ownership structures,

management, and safeguards. A comprehensive grants manual was prepared to guide the selection criteria and proposal

management for the matching grants.



 Component 3: Tourism enterprise support program (SDR 3.5 million - US$5 million equivalent)



14.      This component aimed to support tourism SMEs in Ghana by enhancing their business planning, formalization,

and market access, while offering matching grants to improve their products and services . It specifically targeted women-

owned SMEs and financed tourism enterprise matching grants and business development services and training. Qualified

businesses were invited to submit proposals for evaluation, with existing viable businesses fast-tracked for grants. New

businesses with viable proposals were required to complete a project-supported training program before applying for grants

through a business plan competition. The grants program was to be managed by a competitively selected team under the

supervision of financial experts from a technical committee.



 Component 4: Project management and institutional strengthening (SDR 3.5 million - US$5 million equivalent)



15.       This component aimed to support the Ministry of Tourism, Arts, and Culture (MOTAC) in managing and

coordinating the project by financing key activities. These included developing a tourism sector action plan, facilitating

public-private dialogue for the sector, and conducting capacity assessments with training for allied agencies such as the

Ghana Immigration Service, Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Ministry of Aviation, and the Forestry Commission. It also focused on

strengthening MOTAC staff and its departments in areas like tourism policy, planning, fiduciary management, environmental

gathering baseline visitor demographic data for project monitoring.



     B. SIGNIFICANT CHANGES DURING IMPLEMENTATION (IF APPLICABLE)



 Revised PDOs and Outcome Targets

16.     The PDO was not changed at project restructuring.



 Revised PDO Indicators

17.       The PDO indicator end targets were revised at project restructuring. The restrictions on international travel amidst

the COVID-19 pandemic lowered the prospects for international arrivals and the number of international visitors at targeted

sites, as well as the prospects for private investment in the tourism sector. Accordingly, relevant results indicators were

revised downwards. Conversely, a sharper rise in domestic tourism during the pandemic motivated an upward revision in the

growth of domestic tourists at targeted sites. Revisions are summarized in Annex 6.



 Revised Components

18.     As part of the project's restructuring, funds were reallocated across components to shift resources towards better

performing activities. These are summarized below.



 •   Component 1: Tourism Enabling Environment was scaled down, reallocating $10.5 million, resulting in a revised budget

     of $4.5 million. This involved narrowing the tourism skills development activity, removing the aviation master plan and

     e-visa system, and reducing the scope of branding and marketing activities.

 •   Component 2: Tourism Sites & Destinations saw its original budget of $15 million increased by an additional $5 million,

     for a total of $20 million. These additional resources supported site upgrades, design plans, and feasibility studies.

 •   Component 3: Enterprise Support: received an additional $5 million, raising its budget from $5 million to $10 million to

     strengthen the tourism enterprise support program.

 •   Component 4: Project Management & Institutional Strengthening was enhanced with an additional $500,000, bringing

     the total to $5.5 million. This funding supported the implementing agency’s coordination efforts with relevant

     stakeholders.



 Other Changes

19.     The restructuring also revised the Grant Management Framework, transferring management responsibility from

the Ghana Enterprises Agency (GEA) to MOTAC.



 Rationale for Changes and Their Implication on the Original Theory of Change



20.        The main project restructuring in June 2022 aimed to accelerate implementation progress following a slow start

in the first two years. From project effectiveness in February 2019 to March 2021, weak progress led to the implementation

progress and progress towards achieving the PDO ratings being downgraded to Moderately Unsatisfactory. Several factors

contributed to these ratings, including initial lack of readiness, turnover within the Project Coordination Unit (PCU), and

limited ownership by MOTAC and other government stakeholders. The COVID-19 pandemic further hindered progress

through travel restrictions and delays in contract execution. In response, Ghanaian authorities and the World Bank agreed

on a strong action plan in April 2021 to revitalize the project. This renewed commitment led to accelerated disbursements,

with $16.73 million (42 percent of the $40 million total) disbursed by the end of March 2022. Building on this momentum,

the Ghanaian counterparts and the World Bank team agreed to a project restructuring to reallocate resources to high-

performing components and ensure completion of all project activities and achievement of results by the closing date. Two

subsequent project restructurings occurred in September and December 2023 to facilitate the processing of additional

financing, which ultimately did not materialize.



21.       These restructuring changes did not alter the project's underlying theory of change. The adjustments were made

to align resources and targets with the realities of implementation, while the core objective of enhancing tourism performance

in Ghana remained intact. The project’s strategy continued to support increased tourism demand, improv ed infrastructure,

and strengthened private sector involvement as initially envisioned. Similarly, the PDO indicators were unchanged.

Accordingly, a single rating is retained.



II. OUTCOME

     A. RELEVANCE OF PDO



 Assessment of Relevance of PDOs and Rating

 Rating: High

22.      The PDO remains highly relevant to the World Bank’s ongoing engagement in Ghana. The project was well aligned

with the CPS for FY13-16 (Report No. 76369-GH), which was extended to FY18. The CPS emphasized a Private Sector

Development Strategy that extended beyond Doing Business reforms to address sector-specific competitiveness issues in

key industries with diversification potential, notably tourism. Furthermore, the PDO continues to align with the Country

Partnership Framework (CPF) for FY22-26, which prioritizes tourism as a key non-resource-based sector to advance the

objective of improving conditions for enterprise development. This includes measures such as streamlined tourism

regulations and enhanced access to finance for tourism businesses.



23.       The PDO has remained highly aligned with the Government of Ghana’s developmental goals both at the time of

project appraisal and in recent years. The project supported the Government’s Coordinated Program of Economic and Social

Policies (2017), which identified key development priorities for the subsequent four years, including tourism. Since then, it

has continued to align with several national development frameworks, including the National Tourism Development Plan

(2013–2027), the Long-term National Development Plan (2018–2057), and the most recent Vision 2057, launched by the

National Development Planning Commission in May 2024, which consistently identify tourism as a strategic sector for

achieving development objectives. Moreover, tourism plays a vital role in the country’s economy, accounting for 20 percent

of formal employment outside the agriculture sector and serving as a significant source of foreign exchange.

25.      The project achieved progress towards its objective by addressing both demand and supply-side challenges,

helping to attract more visitors and bolster the country’s tourism infrastructure. The interventions were intended to

increase Ghana's visibility as an attractive destination for both international and domestic tourists and strengthen the sector's

capacity to deliver quality services.



26.        On the demand side, the project focused on enhancing Ghana's reputation as a premier tourism destination

through strategic marketing initiatives. A key element of this effort was the development and implementation of a

comprehensive National Tourism Marketing and Brand Strategy. High-impact campaigns, such as a collaboration with CNN

that broadcasted 440 thirty-second commercials across North America, Europe, and the Middle East, amplified Ghana’s

visibility on a global scale. Digital promotions, including a targeted campaign on Expedia, generated significant returns,

attributing US$5.6 million of the US$12 million in bookings on the platform directly to the campaign in 2022. While the direct

impact of these campaigns on international arrivals is difficult to determine, available data shows a 4 percent increase in

tourist arrivals from targeted markets—the US, Germany, the Netherlands, and Canada—between 2019 and 2023. In

contrast, arrivals from non-targeted regional countries, Nigeria and South Africa, both of which rank among Ghana's top 10

sources of visitors, declined by 30 percent during the same period. In addition, the project provided the tourism sector with

training on social media and digital branding, creating a community of digital experts, event planners, and creatives who

participated in the government’s "Year of Return" and "Beyond the Return" initiative, aimed at promoting tourism and

fostering economic linkages and investments from diasporas.



27.       These initiatives have played an important role in attracting international tourists, given the proportionate

increase in tourists from targeted markets, compared to non target markets. In 2023, international arrivals reached 1.01

million, exceeding the target of 0.75 million.1 This positive trend continued into the first half of 2024, with 611,478 arrivals

recorded by mid-year—a 39 percent increase compared to the same period in 2018. Based on historical patterns, where

     A. KEY FACTORS DURING PREPARATION



45.      Project design adopted a holistic approach to tourism development. The project design was simple and included

enablers of tourism both on the demand and supply sides as well as a focus on tourism assets. It also sought to build the

capacity of MOTAC as the main implementing agency. It benefited from the knowledge of tourism experts familiar with the

country and regional contexts and from sound analysis (e.g., the Bank team undertook a study to inform the most suitable

business model for a tourism training facility).



46.       The substantial TA component and lack of site selection slowed implementation progress. Although the team was

advised during concept review to scale down the ambitious US$15m TA component, it was retained in the Project Appraisal

Document (PAD) and later significantly restructured due to limited performance, particularly in the skills sub-component. For

Component 2, while the National Tourism Development Plan (2013-2027) identified nine priority sites for development, the

PAD left site selection open and no systematic approach to site selection was adopted during implementation. This

contributed to delays, leaving Component 2 rated as MS until closing. Despite these challenges, the project successfully

upgraded the flagship KNMMP and numerous smaller sites (listed in paragraph 28). However, time and resources ran out to

fully upgrade other iconic sites, though design and safeguards studies were completed.



47.      During project preparation, there may have been greater opportunities to enhance coordination and synergies

with complementary projects. At the decision review stage, the team was asked to ensure alignment with ongoing and

planned initiatives, particularly those relating to the SME support component. Notably, the Ghana Economic Transformation

Project (GETP), approved around the same time, included a significant SME support component implemented by the Ghana

Enterprises Agency (GEA) and other International Finance Corporation (IFC) SME-focused interventions. The GETP’s grants

program, managed through the GEA, was launched rapidly in response to COVID-19. The GTDP could have leveraged GEA’s

established capabilities earlier, which might have enabled the SME support program under the GTDP to commence more

quickly. Additionally, the GETP’s work on investment promotion and facilitation offered another opportunity for closer

collaboration.



48.      The high institutional capacity risk was appropriately rated as high at appraisal. Despite mechanisms such as the

NSC for inter-agency coordination and the PCU’s capacity being bolstered with qualified public servants and competitively

recruited consultants, the risk initially hindered implementation. This was mitigated only after the mid-term review (MTR),

when government ownership and leadership improved, and the PCU began functioning effectively. However, challenges in

procurement and financial management (FM) persisted intermittently (see fiduciary compliance section).

     External factors

53.      High inflation and currency depreciation lowered available project resources. The COVID-19 pandemic, followed

by a severe macroeconomic crisis, caused unexpected increases in the cost of goods and labor, limiting the scope for site

upgrades. Inflation in 2022 and 2023 outpaced the dollar’s appreciation relative to the Ghanaian cedi. Additionally, the

depreciation of the SDR against the dollar resulted in a loss of approximately $3 million (from SDR 27.9 million to around

$37.81 million) between 2018 and 2023.



54.       The COVID-19 pandemic had a profound impact on Ghana’s tourism sector, exacerbated by mobility restrictions

that affected both travelers and key project stakeholders, including contractors and project supervision teams. Ghana

confirmed its first COVID-19 case on March 14, 2020, prompting the government to implement a series of stringent measures

to control the virus’s spread. These included suspending public gatherings of more than 25 people, closing universities and

schools, and imposing a mandatory 14-day quarantine for residents returning from high-risk countries. On March 23, Ghana

closed all borders to travelers, and by March 30, a partial lockdown was enforced in major urban areas. As a result,

international tourist arrivals plummeted by nearly 73 percent, from 1.3 million in 2019 to just 355,000 in 2020, with only

411,000 arrivals in the first nine months of 2021. The hospitality industry suffered severe setbacks, with widespread hotel

closures leading to a 30.7 percent decline in tourism employment, according to a report by Oxford Business Group. 6 The

sector’s economic contribution was similarly hit hard, with tourism’s share of GDP dropping from 5.9 percent in 2019 to 2.9

percent in 2020, and sector revenue falling from $3.7 billion to $1.9 billion.

shortcomings, and reflected inputs from global tourism expertise; the risks were adequately assessed and various aspects of

the project designed accordingly. Project supervision was hands-on and effective in turning the project around and ensuring

it achieved its results; however, underbudgeting of site upgrade activities (and the Bank’s decision to not grant additional

financing) prevented the completion of all intended site upgrades.



      D. RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME

67.        Risks to development outcomes for the project include a lack of clarity in site management arrangements,

particularly for the KNMMP. While the Bank recommended that the site be managed privately to enhance operational

efficiency and sustainability, this arrangement has not yet been implemented. This raises concerns about the government’s

ability to effectively maintain the site and sustain the investments made under the project.



68.       Another risk stems from the lack of follow-up on the findings of the PPP feasibility study for a tourism training

school, which was commissioned under the project. The study outlined potential pathways for establishing a training

institution to support workforce development in the tourism sector. Without further action to implement these

recommendations, the capacity-building goals of the project may remain unrealized.



69.      Finally, there is a risk related to the absence of government budget allocation for continued tourism marketing.

Sustained marketing efforts are essential for promoting tourism growth and capitalizing on the project’s achievements.

Without dedicated funding, there may be limited capacity to attract new visitors and support the long-term growth of the

tourism sector.





V. LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS



70.        Thorough preparation during pre-appraisal is critical for projects involving selective infrastructure investments

to minimize implementation delays. Project teams can take several key actions during this phase to ensure smoother

implementation. These include: 1) early identification of key project sites; 2) conducting rapid assessments to generate

realistic and indicative cost estimates; 3) preparing detailed site management plans; and 4) utilizing a project preparation

           The World Bank

           Tourism Development Project (P164211)                                                                 ICR DOCUMENT





advance or establishing a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) to streamline processes. A transparent and systematic

approach for site selection can also serve to safeguard against political influence over site selection. By integrating these

steps into the preparation phase, the project would have been better positioned to mitigate risks and facilitate more efficient

implementation.



71.       Focusing efforts on a smaller number of high-impact sites can enhance both the quality and sustainability of

project outcomes, particularly when resources and agency capacity are limited. By prioritizing flagship sites, projects can

create standout destinations that attract tourists, stimulate private sector investment, and generate greater local economic

benefits. Additionally, adopting a market- or demand-driven approach during site selection would have been valuable in

identifying a "minimum viable proposition" for each site at the outset. By channeling investments into destinations with

strong demand potential, the project could have ensured a more efficient and impactful use of resources. This focused

strategy also allows implementing agencies to provide more effective oversight and better support the long-term

maintenance of sites, ensuring lasting improvements to the tourism sector.



72.       The rate of disbursements can be highly non-linear, especially when projects involve building capacity of

implementing institutions. In projects partnering with implementing agencies with limited experience in Bank projects,

disbursement targets may be better reflected by an S-curve, low in early years and accelerating as institutional capacity takes

off and a well-functioning management is put in place.



73.       Appropriate risk mitigation measures should be implemented early to effectively reduce risks. The project’s

implementation delays were partially due to weak institutional capacity, which was identified during the design phase, with

the residual risk related to institutional capacity rated as "high." In hindsight, this risk could have been mitigated through a

clear, proactive plan to strengthen implementation capacity. This could have involved actions such as increasing staffing

levels, ensuring competitive appointments for project leadership, and considering outsourcing for specific tasks where

necessary. Addressing these areas early on could have reduced the institutional capacity risk to at least a “substantial” level,

improving the likelihood of successful implementation.



74.       Flexible budget planning and contingency reserves from the outset allow projects to better adapt to unforeseen

costs, such as those driven by inflation or pandemic-related disruptions. Early cost assessments and periodic budget reviews

help ensure adjustments can be made as needed, allowing core activities to continue smoothly while avoiding overruns.

Furthermore, the project demonstrated the value of completing technical studies for asset conservation activities during

appraisal to ensure accurate budgeting, proper costing, and to avoid potential cost overruns during implementation.



75.       It is essential to remain flexible and adapt project interventions to achieve their overarching objectives, especially

when challenges arise. In this project, despite the e-visa program not materializing, the broader goals could have still been

advanced through alternative means. Similarly, although the interventions related to the tourism training school and site

management arrangements were not fully realized, these initiatives could be further supported through other mechanisms,

such as IFC investments, to ensure more sustainable outcomes.



76.       Effective data collection and robust M&E frameworks are critical for accurately assessing the impact of tourism

projects. Tracking international and domestic arrivals, for instance, requires precise data sources. Monitoring ticket sales at

local tourism sites can serve as a reliable measure of visitor numbers, providing a solid basis for reporting and impact

assessment. Additionally, establishing a Tourism Satellite Account TSA would be valuable for capturing comprehensive data

on tourism’s economic contribution, including employment, revenue generation, and local economic impact. While support

for a TSA did not materialize in this project, implementing it in future initiatives would significantly enhance data quality and

provide a more detailed understanding of the tourism sector's contribution to the national economy.

                                         ANNEX 4. EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS



1. In the economic and financial analysis, a cost-benefit approach was applied, evaluating the projected stream of

benefits against the projected costs to calculate the net present value (NPV) and the economic rate of return (ERR).

Methodologically, the analysis considered welfare changes for all beneficiaries, including visitors and future

generations, in alignment with the World Bank's Operational Procedures 10.04 for the Economic Analysis of Investment

Project Financing. The analysis used a 5 percent discount rate and projected benefits over a 20-year period. Fiscal

benefits were expected to accrue through foreign exchange earnings from increased international tourist arrivals,

corporate taxes, value-added tax (VAT) from tourism-related businesses, income tax from employees in the tourism

sector, and various other fiscal channels such as departure taxes, tourism levies, and local municipal fees.



2. From an economic perspective, the project was expected to generate significant benefits for Ghana's economy,

particularly in the tourism sector, by improving facilities and boosting visitor satisfaction. The anticipated growth in

tourist arrivals would lead to the creation of direct and indirect jobs across the tourism value chain, including hotels,

restaurants, tour operators, and construction. This would benefit Ghanaian households, firms, and local establishments

in the project area, which included Greater Accra, Kumasi, coastal, and heritage sites. SMEs received business

development services and financial support to enhance their operations and generate investment, while local

governments and municipalities were expected to benefit from increased tax revenues and improved infrastructure.



3. The analysis was based on key assumptions. A 5 percent discount rate was applied in line with World Bank

guidelines, with the assumption of stable macroeconomic conditions, including steady prices and exchange rates. Fiscal

impact projections included corporate and income tax rates of 33.5 percent and 30 percent, respectively. Demand

growth estimates, aligned with the National Tourism Development Plan, anticipated a 5 to 10 percent increase in tourist

arrivals annually from 2018 to 2022, with a further 12.5 percent annual increase beyond that period. At project

completion, these projections were adjusted to reflect actual arrivals. The analysis also assumed an average stay of

eight days per international tourist, with daily expenditures of $250 and an average hotel occupancy rate of 59 percent.

Increased international arrivals were expected to boost foreign exchange earnings, benefiting local economies,

particularly communities near tourism sites and small urban businesses in Greater Accra and Kumasi. According to

National Development Plan estimates, for every $100 spent on accommodations, additional spending spanned other

sectors: $23 on food, beverages, and livestock; $12 on utilities; and $7 on manufactured goods. The projected growth

in tourism arrivals was also expected to generate fiscal revenue through corporate taxes on tourism-related businesses,

VAT, income taxes from industry employees, departure taxes, and a 1 percent tourism levy. Additional revenue was

anticipated from municipal taxes, property taxes, copyright fees, and entrance fees to tourist attractions.



4. The analysis indicates that the project had an ERR of 10.4 percent with an NPV of US$ 62.79 million at closing.

These results are slightly below the estimates at the time of project design, where ERR was estimated at 13.4% with an

NPV of US$ 74.55 million. The decrease was driven by the sharp drop in international arrivals between 2019 and 2022

due to the COVID-19 pandemic.
