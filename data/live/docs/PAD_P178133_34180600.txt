I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT



A. Country Context



      1.      Bangladesh experienced rapid social and economic progress in recent decades, reaching lower middle-

      income status in 2015. Stable macroeconomic conditions drove 6.7 percent average annual real Gross Domestic

      Production (GDP) growth between 2010 and 2019. The national poverty rate fell from 48.9 to 24.5 percent

      between 2000 and 2016, supported by labor market gains. However, poverty reduction has recently slowed,

      particularly in urban areas and in the west of the country.1 Annual consumption growth of the bottom 40 percent

      (1.2 percent) trailed the overall population (1.6 percent) from 2010 to 2016.

      2.      Rising global commodity prices and global monetary policy tightening disrupted the economic

      recovery from COVID-19. Government stimulus programs supported a rapid economic recovery in FY21 as

      movement restrictions ended. However, the current account deficit surged in the second half of FY22 amidst

      rising commodity and intermediate goods prices, which contributed to accelerating inflation. Import price

      moderation and rationing narrowed trade deficit in FY23, supported by resilient export growth. However, this

      was offset by a sharp contraction in trade credit and lower medium- and long-term lending in the financial

      account. The balance of payments deficit reached US$8.2 billion by end FY23. Authorities requested support to

      restore external balance, including an International Monetary Fund (IMF) program approved in January 2023.

      3.      The fiscal deficit widened to an estimated 4.4 percent of GDP in FY23. Tax revenues remained among

      the lowest in the world at an estimated 7.6 percent of GDP in FY23. Expenditure growth accelerated with higher

      subsidy spending as a result of elevated commodity prices, while revenues declined with lower imports. Capital

      expenditure was led by infrastructure megaprojects, with rationalization of other projects in FY23 to slow the

      growth of the budget deficit.

      4.       Real GDP growth is expected to reach 6.2 percent in FY24 before accelerating to its long-term trend.

      Growth is expected to converge to 6.5 percent over the medium term as inflationary pressure eases, depending

      on the depth of reforms implemented. The fiscal deficit is expected to gradually narrow with accelerating

      economic activity, higher incomes, and tax administration reform implementation. The Current Account Deficit

      will narrow as imports normalize with moderating commodity prices. Remittance inflows are expected to rise,

      supported by strong external demand for migrant workers. The financial account deficit is projected to moderate

      in FY24.

      5.      Structural reforms are needed to support a faster pace of growth over the medium term. Priorities to

      reach upper middle-income status by 2031 include building a competitive business environment, diversifying

      exports, increasing human capital, building efficient infrastructure, deepening the financial sector, and attracting

      private investment. These reforms will support international competitiveness as Bangladesh prepares for

      graduation from the Least Developed Country status in 2026, which will gradually lead to reduced concessional

      financing and preferential market access for its exports.

      6.      Bangladesh is at low risk of overall and external debt distress in the January 2023 joint IMF-World

      Bank Debt Sustainability Assessment. Bangladesh is not currently subject to Debt Limits Conditionality under

      the Sustainable Development Finance Policy. In its most recent Staff Report, the IMF stressed the need for

      7.      Bangladesh faces a high level of vulnerability to the effects of climate change, which poses a further

      risk to growth and development. The Global Climate Risk Index ranks Bangladesh as the world’s seventh most-

      affected country in 2000-2019.2 Rising temperatures leading to more intense and unpredictable rainfalls during

      the monsoon season and the already high probability of cyclones is expected to further increase, resulting in

      increased tidal inundation. Flooding in Bangladesh is a near-constant phenomenon, recurring with varying

      magnitude and intensity, affecting a greater population than any other natural hazard. Floods and riverbank

      erosion in Bangladesh affect about one million people annually and can be substantially higher.3 Once every

      three to five years, up to two-thirds of Bangladesh is inundated by floods. In addition, the country is projected

      to have 13.3 million internal climate refugees by 2050.4 The Bangladesh Country Climate and Development

      Report (CCDR) highlights internal climate migration as a pressing issue for urban areas. Urban centers, especially

      Dhaka, have been a destination for those trying to escape rural poverty and climate change is accelerating the

      race to the cities. For the urban poor, the exposure to climate and/or economic risk, is exacerbated by limited

      access to services: only 11 percent of urban households benefit from at least one social protection program

      compared to 36 percent of rural households.5 Addressing these risks will be critical to support sustainable

      economic development, ensuring that the vulnerable populations are not left behind.



      8.     Evidence from the last decade shows that the parts of Bangladesh exposed to multiple overlapping

      shocks have witnessed relatively slower poverty reduction. In the years ahead, climate change is likely to

      continue to slow the progress on poverty reduction. With higher frequency of climate related shocks and

      disasters, vulnerable populations and the chronically poor will face long lasting and multigenerational effects,

      resulting in costly coping strategies such as divesting productive assets and curtailing investments in human

      capital. Strong, adaptive social protection measures can enable poorer households to reduce negative coping

      strategies and build resilience.



B. Sectoral and Institutional Context



      9.     Together with poverty reduction, Bangladesh has made significant progress in child and maternal

      health outcomes. Between 2011 and 2017-18, the estimated under-five mortality rate declined from 53 to 45

      per 1,000 live births while estimated maternal mortality ratio declined from 248 to 174 per 100,000 births.

      Similarly, prevalence of stunting among under-five children improved from 41 to 24 percent; child wasting

      declined from 16 percent to 11 percent, and the rate of child underweight declined from 36 percent to 22

      percent.6 These improvements signify important gains with short- and long-term impact on poverty and shared

      prosperity.

Early childhood is a critical stage for human capital accumulation: a weak start in the first 1,000 days followed

by inadequate investments in early childhood education limits children’s cognitive and non-cognitive

development. This lowers their school readiness and leads to poor school enrollment, retention, completion,

and learning outcomes, leading to lower lifelong earnings, creating a vicious cycle. Low-income households in

Bangladesh are particularly vulnerable, as nutrition and health outcomes are twice as worse in the poorest

quintile as compared to the richest quintile. In the wake of COVID-19, low-income households were also more

likely to be food insecure and experienced higher levels of stunting and wasting, which has direct impact on their

long-term outcomes.7



11. Other than income, unhealthy household environment, inadequate feeding practices, and lack of home

stimulation significantly affect developmental outcomes. While household income affects purchase and

consumption of various food groups and thereby implicitly impacts the nutrient intake of households, diets do

not improve automatically with higher incomes and lower prices. Similarly, improved household diets do not

necessarily translate into better diets for women and children. For example, women’s diets are lacking despite

access to nutritious diets at the household level, and children’s diets are poor in quality regardless of household

income. This suggests that other factors matter, such as women’s empowerment and awareness of feeding

practices.8



12. Underutilization of key health and education services is one of the key causes of lagging human capital

outcomes particularly among low-income households. Lack of awareness, high out of pocket expenses, and

restrictive social norms contribute to the underutilization of services. Getting permission to go to the doctor is

also one of the most cited barriers to accessing healthcare by women aged 15-49 in Bangladesh. Given mobility

and financial constraints faced by women, almost 70 percent of women of reproductive age report difficulties

in accessing healthcare.9 As a result, less than one in five women take up on the four recommended antenatal

care visits throughout pregnancy. This is also reflected in the percentage of child deliveries at health facilities,

with only one quarter of poor households attending a health facility, compared to 76 percent of households in

the wealthier quintiles. Home stimulation also plays a fundamental role in children’s developmental outcomes,

but this is more prevalent in richer households: only 50 percent of children aged 2-4 years in poor households

have parents that engage in early stimulation activities that promote learning and school readiness, compared

to 80 percent in rich households. Consequently, only 17 percent of children aged 3-4 years in the poorest quintile

are developmentally on track in literacy and numeracy, compared to 43 percent in the richest quintile.10 High

prevalence of gender-based violence (GBV) is also linked to chronic malnutrition and underweight rates in

women and their children through reduced physical capacity, increased psychological stress, and possibly diet

deprivation. A high percentage of married women report facing GBV or find it acceptable.11 Despite the wide

prevalence and severity of impacts on mothers and children, lack of information on already scarce GBV services

13. Climate shocks pose an additional threat to development and wellbeing, particularly for children. The

poor face a disproportionate impact because they generally live in higher-risk areas, rely on agriculture and

other climate-sensitive natural resources for income and livelihoods, and have limited coping mechanisms.

Progress in alleviating undernourishment has also been relatively slow because of climate change. The World

Food Programme (WFP) estimates that without adaptation, the risk of hunger and child malnutrition on a global

scale could increase by 20 percent respectively by the 2050s.13 Higher drinking water salinity from rising sea

levels will increase the prevalence of diarrhea and cardiovascular disease as well as affect the usability of

groundwater, while more frequent extreme heat events will increase mortality rates among the elderly,

children, chronically ill, socially isolated, and at-risk occupational groups.14 Extreme weather events brought on

by climate change have also been attributed as one of the triggers of a host of psychological issues, including

depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder. Rising temperatures and humidity make respiratory

illnesses, waterborne diseases, and dengue more likely. A wide range of climate change fallouts are experienced

differently by men, women, and children due to lack of access to healthcare facilities, late diagnosis, reduced

awareness of diseases, and restrictions in decision-making processes.15



14. Achieving stronger human capital outcomes requires overcoming multiple constraints simultaneously

starting as early as possible in the lifecycle. Food insecurity, low parental education, lack of stimulation at home,

and parental distress are key risks that jeopardize children’s optimal development. Weak support for children

and youth could limit Bangladesh’s economic and demographic potential as investments in early childhood are

more efficient when they start as early as possible, beginning at conception to ensure pregnant women are

consuming nutritious food and receiving appropriate health support and continues through the child’s earliest

years when there is a rapid phase of growth involving physical, neurodevelopmental, and socioemotional

changes. Positive development during early childhood will have lasting effects for the rest of the individual’s life.

To reach their full potential, children need all components of nurturing care, which include good health,

adequate nutrition, responsive caregiving, opportunities for early learning, and safety and security.16 This is also

reflected in the country’s Comprehensive Early Childhood Care and Development Policy 2013, which lays out an

integrated and holistic system of child development from survival, protection, care and development through

family, community, learning and school-based programs. The Policy designates the Ministry of Women and Child

Affairs (MoWCA) as the lead ministry to coordinate and oversee implementation of the Policy, in collaboration

with various ministries, non-governmental organizations, and international organizations.

F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design



      29.     The proposed project builds on global evidence of the importance of an integrated approach to

      improve human capital investments. Social safety nets can play a powerful role in combating poverty,

      inequality, and vulnerability as well as contributing to improved human development outcomes. Global reviews

      of cash transfers have found unambiguous improvements in dietary diversity and uptake of health and nutrition

      services, both crucial elements of better nutritional outcomes, as well as significant positive impacts on stunting

      among children.22, 23 The combination of cash transfers with behavior change communication (BCC) has shown

      to be more effective in improving nutrition outcomes, parenting practices, and other behaviours, globally and

      in Bangladesh. In Indonesia, stunting among children aged 0-5 years fell by up to 10 percentage points by using

      this combined approach. This is also substantiated by findings of the Transfer Modality Research Initiative in

      Bangladesh, which notes that the combination of cash transfers with BCC led to significant improvements in

      nutrition knowledge and stunting.24 Moreover, cash transfer programs can also address GBV. A mixed-method

      review of 21 studies in low- and middle-income countries identified that most studies have evidence that cash

      transfers decreased GBV.25 Evidence from Bangladesh indicates that when cash is combined with BCC, the

      reduction of GBV continues even after project closure.26

      project (P123629) indicated that robust targeting, communication, payments, and monitoring of cash transfers

      to the target population led to increase in monthly household food consumption and significant impact on the

Climate



55.     The project has been screened to identify and address any potential climate and disaster risks and

follows recommendations of the Bangladesh CCDR. The CCDR highlights the importance of shock responsive

social protection to strengthen resilience, including (i) integrated gender aspects to ensure access to support

services and reduce potential for GBV and other vulnerabilities; (ii) leveraging existing digital payment

infrastructure to transfer cash electronically in the event of a shock; (iii) streamlining data to rapidly identify

recipients of income support, before or after shocks and natural disasters; and (iv) increasing coordination to

improve efficiency across multiple social programs.



56. Certain regions of Bangladesh are assessed to be vulnerable to floods, cyclones, and storm surges, and

the whole country is highly susceptible to extreme climate events due to its position as a low-lying delta. As

already detailed in paragraphs 7, 8, and 13, the poor in particular bear the brunt of climate events and internal

climate migrants are predicted to become a pressing issue for urban areas due to their heightened exposure to

economic and climate related risks.



57. Project activities will enhance households' adaptation and resilience to climate-induced shocks. The

Project will enhance adaptive capacity of households, in particular women and children, through expansion and

strengthening of the MCBP. Operational guidelines are set to be approved to enhance the Programme’s

scalability and inclusivity, emphasizing climate vulnerability in the targeting strategy. Specifically, cash transfers

to poor mothers, awareness building and behaviour change counseling, and enhanced program guidelines will

address food insecurity through consumption smoothing and encouraging utilization of critical healthcare

services. Effective M&E systems would allow program officials to gather relevant feedback and adjust processes

to strengthen inclusion and respond to the needs of climate vulnerable populations. The project will also explore

shock-responsive measures, ensuring swift response during extreme climate events and fostering community

resilience through improved health systems and disease prevention strategies.

           59. Grievance Redress. Communities and individuals who believe that they are adversely affected by a

           project supported by the World Bank may submit complaints to existing project-level grievance mechanisms

           or the Bank’s Grievance Redress Service (GRS). The GRS ensures that complaints received are promptly

           reviewed in order to address project-related concerns. Project affected communities and individuals may

           submit their complaint to the Bank’s independent Accountability Mechanism (AM). The AM houses the

           Inspection Panel, which determines whether harm occurred, or could occur, as a result of Bank non-

           compliance with its policies and procedures, and the Dispute Resolution Service, which provides communities

           and borrowers with the opportunity to address complaints through dispute resolution. Complaints may be

           submitted to the AM at any time after concerns have been brought directly to the attention of Bank

           Management and after Management has been given an opportunity to respond. For information on how to

           submit complaints to the Bank’s GRS, please visit http://www.worldbank.org/GRS. For information on how

          60. The overall rating for the project is rated moderate. This reflects substantial risks on the following two

          61. Institutional Capacity for Implementation and Sustainability. This risk reflects the implementing agency’s

          limited experience with implementing World Bank and other donor-funded projects, primarily as a coordination

          partner or in donor-supported technical assistance interventions. To mitigate this risk, the project will establish

          the PMCCU with adequate staffing and expertise in project management, procurement, and financial

          management, while capacity building, training, and hands on support will be provided to MoWCA and other

          relevant partners throughout implementation, including targeted training and guidance on technical,

          procurement, financial management, and environmental and social aspects of project management.



          62.    Fiduciary. The risk is specific to financial management: while the majority of project funds will be towards

          reimbursing cash transfers to beneficiaries made through the national digital payment platform, the

          implementing agency has no prior experience with managing Bank funds. The key financial management risks

          are noted to be (i) accuracy and timeliness in accounting and financial reporting of eligible expenditures under

          PBCs; (ii) insufficient audit capacity of the SSAD to cover program audit under Component 1; (iii) weak internal

          control systems; (iv) limited interoperability between MIS; and (v) inefficient reconciliation procedures for

          incremental cash transfer expenditures. These risks will be mitigated throughout project implementation

          through substantial capacity building, technical support, and intensive supervision.

Implementation Arrangements

    1.        Component 1 will provide reimbursement to GoB against cash transfer expenditures through PBCs that

    will support expansion of beneficiary coverage as well as quality improvements to the Programme. These will

    include approval of the MCBP operational guidelines (PBC 1); strengthened capacity for effective Programme

    implementation and monitoring (PBC 2); increasing beneficiary coverage (PBC 3) and expanding tenure of support

    (PBC 5); and enhancing program monitoring through regular program reviews and beneficiary surveys to identify

    areas of improvement on targeting effectiveness and implementation processes, including integrated climate risk

    considerations in the M&E system (PBC 4). PBC achievement will be supported through technical support provided

    under Component 2.



    2.        A PMMCU to be housed in the Ministry of Women and Children Affairs will be primarily responsible for

    implementing the project in close consultation and coordination with the Finance Division, Department of Women

    Affairs, and the MCBP Programme Implementation Unit. Regular PSC and PIC meetings, with membership from

    relevant partners and stakeholders, will provide strategic and policy guidance, and guide implementation.



Implementation Support Plan

    3.        The Implementation Support Plan has been developed based on the specific nature of project activities

    and lessons learned from past operations in the country and sector. The plan will be reviewed annually to ensure

    that it continues to meet the implementation support needs of the project. Key elements of the World Bank’s

    approach to providing implementation support are:



    a.     A core task team led by the Task Team Leader and co-Task Team Leader will ensure regular dialogue with

    implementing agencies. The core team will provide intensive supervision and handholding on project

    management, particularly in the first two years, as well as conduct regular technical meetings. The task team

    will also conduct at least two implementation support missions every year to review progress, including

    achievement of PBCs, identify implementation challenges, and suggest options for addressing emerging issues.



    b.    Regular E&S, procurement, and financial management reviews and financial audits to ensure compliance

    with E&S and fiduciary requirements. In addition, intensive capacity building will be provided throughout

    implementation to ensure the implementing agency carries out the project in full accordance with Bank

    guidelines and processes.



    c.    The Mid-Term Review (MTR), expected to take place in July 2026, will review project arrangements and

    progress, identify any bottlenecks to implementation or emerging priorities, and recommend measures to

    address them.
