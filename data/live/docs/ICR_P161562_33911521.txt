   I.       PROGRAM CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES



            A.   Context at Appraisal



        1.         The US$200 million Disaster Risk Management (DRM) Development Policy Credit with a

        Catastrophe Deferred Drawdown Option (“Cat DDO”) supported the Government of Kenya’s reform

        program to improve the country’s capacity to reduce disaster risks and improve management of the

        socioeconomic and fiscal impacts of disasters. The Government of Kenya (GoK) initiated a shift toward a

        more proactive and comprehensive approach to managing disaster and climate risks, including addressing

        impacts of climate change, to build programs and strategies that strengthen national response, and put

        in place contingency financing that would serve to improve post-disaster liquidity needed to meet

        emergency and recovery needs. This shift was in part due to the 2008-2011 drought which highlighted the

        importance of disaster risk management, as evidenced in the Post Disaster Needs Assessment (PDNA) 1.



        2.          Extreme climatic events presented a significant risk to Kenya and contributed to increasing

        levels of vulnerability of the agriculture sector, triggering episodes of acute food insecurity. Of concern

        were floods and droughts, with major droughts occurring on average once every 10 years, and moderate

        droughts or floods every three to four years 2. Floods resulted in the greatest loss of human lives 3, whereas

        droughts affected the most people and had the greatest economic impact – on average, a 0.6 percentage

        point decline in Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth is observed in Kenya in years of poor rains 4. The

        2008-2011 drought is estimated to have cost Kenya US$12.1 billion, including US$805.6 million for the

        destruction of physical and durable assets, and US$11.3 billion for losses in the flows of the economy

        across all sectors 5.



        3.          Kenya, like other East African countries, was also vulnerable to emerging infectious diseases

        and endemic zoonoses, with epidemics of infectious diseases ranking among the top five disaster

        hazards in the region. In Kenya, multiple health hazards including disease outbreaks (e.g., anthrax, rabies,

        and dengue fever) contributed to an increased momentum to strengthen mechanisms to manage

        pandemics. At the time of appraisal, COVID-19 was not yet a global pandemic.



        4.          At the time of appraisal, Kenya was at an early stage of urbanization with about a quarter of

        the population living in cities and that number was expected to double by 2050. With an urbanization

        rate at about 4.3 percent a year 6, the urbanization process was unmanaged and was accompanied by

        increasing levels of urban poverty (roughly 60 percent of Kenya’s urban households lived in slums 7),

        growth of informal settlements, poorly functioning land markets and institutions, and an incomplete legal

        and building regulatory framework. In addition, there was an outdated building code, as well as low

        technical capacity to enforce and to build resilient infrastructure. All these factors contributed to

        increasing the vulnerability of the population, the infrastructure, and the economy.

       5.          Despite these vulnerabilities, Kenya had struggled for the better part of twenty years to

       advance the approval of a national DRM policy and law, although the Kenya Constitution (2010) made

       provision for it. There were numerous attempts to advance DRM legislation, however, they were often

       overtaken by other national priorities, such as elections. When the dialogue on the Cat DDO was initiated

       with GoK, the DRM roles and responsibilities were not clearly defined across the national and county

       governments, sectors, agencies, and initiatives. There was lack of coordination and no common

       understanding of risks and responsibilities. In addition, the first and second Medium Term Plans (MTPs)

       did not include DRM considerations or interventions.



       Macroeconomic Context at Appraisal



       6.         At the time of Appraisal, economic activity in Kenya weakened in 2017, but a nascent

       recovery was underway. Economic growth decelerated to a 5-year low of an estimated 4.8 percent in

       2017 in part due to heavy rains, slowdown in credit growth to the private sector and election-induced

       uncertainty. However, the economy showed early signs of recovery in 2018 as there was a rebound in

       tourism, strong public investment, and resilient remittance. Inflation was well within the target band

       (5±2.5 percent), although credit conditions remained tight. The improvement reflected the impact of the

       Government subsidies to address food shortages.



       7.          Drought conditions, economic and political challenges limited economic growth. The poor

       weather conditions in late 2016-early 2017 resulted in a decrease in the agricultural sector’s contribution

       to GDP growth in 2017 to just 0.2 percentage points, the lowest since 2009. However, with improved

       rains, the sector was starting to see some recovery. Activity in the manufacturing sub-sector was impacted

       by a prolonged electioneering period and poor agricultural harvests which dampened business sentiments

       and weakened agribusiness activity. The services sector, which accounts for 58.5 percent of GDP, was the

       main engine of economic growth in 2017.



       8.          The macroeconomic policy framework was considered adequate for the Cat DDO. The

       adequacy of the macroeconomic framework was premised on the authorities’ commitment to sound

       macroeconomic management and efforts to rein in fiscal deficit and maintain sustainable debt levels.

       Kenya was rated as a strong policy performer among low and middle-income countries in the World Bank

       Group’s (WBG’s) Country Policy and Institutional Assessment. Significant investment in systems for public

       financial management and progress in improving the framework for public investment management

       complemented measures to safeguard the vulnerable macro fiscal position. Further, Kenya’s growth

       prospects remained robust and were underpinned by strong domestic demand, a rebound in tourism, a

       stable macroeconomic environment, and the easing of supply-side constraints due to completed

       infrastructure projects.



      Rationale for World Bank Assistance



       9.         At the time of Appraisal, the WBG was supporting GoK in advancing a transformative reform

       program to address identified gaps and improve national capacity to manage climate and hazard risks.

       The reforms aimed at enhancing the resilience of urban areas, mainstreaming DRM, and climate change

       adaptation (CCA) into key sectors and advancing financial protection from disasters. The Operation also

       aimed to translate the overall objectives of the reforms into concrete disaster and climate risk reduction

       measures for key sectors supporting an important pivot towards a more holistic approach that

       encompassed risk identification, risk reduction and risk financing as well as emergency preparedness and

       response (EP&R). This operation was part of the GoK’s strategy to address contingent liabilities and

       provide timely liquidity in case of hazard-related catastrophes, including public health-related events.



       10.         The Cat DDO was well-aligned with the WBG’s Country Partnership Strategy (CPS) for Kenya,

       which covered the period July 1, 2014, to June 30, 2018 8. The CPS was anchored in the country’s Medium-

       Term Plan (MTP) II for 2013-2017 and recognized the importance of managing risks (including from natural

       hazards) that could threaten overall poverty reduction prospects. This operation helped to mitigate that

       risk and in addition, supported policy reforms which contributed to each of the three main domains of

       engagement in the CPS: (i) fortifying institutional, planning and policy frameworks to manage climate and

       disaster risk, supported the objective of Domain 2 ‘to protect the vulnerable and deliver shared

       prosperity’; (ii) strengthening resilience to climate and disaster risk in the urban and water sectors,

       supported the objective of Domain 1 ‘to strengthen planning and management of urban growth to have

       competitiveness and sustainable growth’; and (iii) policy reforms supported the objective of Domain 3 ‘to

       build consistency and equity through devolution’.



       11.         This Operation became the first IDA Cat DDO, one of the first Cat DDOs in Sub-Saharan Africa

       and the first Development Policy Financing (DPF) in Kenya after many years. During preparation, the

       Operation drew on lessons from earlier Cat DDOs and WBG experience in DRM operations, from which

       recommendations were that result frameworks should be further strengthened in the selection of

       relevant and critical prior actions 9. The design and engagement approach capitalized on synergies with

       other operations for accompanying technical assistance (TA). The reform program focused on upstream

       urban resilience, EP&R, and disaster risk financing (DRF). The Operation made use of the IDA18 provision

       which allowed the GoK to leverage 50 percent from the country’s concessional core IDA allocation with

       the balance financed by IDA’s overall resources. It also included health emergencies as one of its triggers,

       making it possible for the GoK to subsequently drawdown from the Cat DDO for COVID-19. The Operation

       was the flagship for other subsequent Cat DDOs in IDA countries and in sub-Saharan Africa.



       12.        In hindsight, given that this was Kenya’s first budget support operation in many years, it also

       required continuous learning across GoK on the modalities of the instrument which later may have

       contributed to some of the implementation challenges. Nevertheless, the learning under this Cat DDO

       for both the WBG and the GoK has contributed to opening the door for future, impactful development

       financing operations series in Kenya.

      Original Policy Areas/Pillars Supported by the Program (as approved)



       14.         The Operation was structured around three pillars with seven prior actions (PAs), fully aligned

       with the Government’s program and priorities. All PAs were agreed jointly with relevant GoK agencies and

       were part of ongoing or planned initiatives. The results indicators were selected directly from actions

       identified within the respective policy actions and were aligned to the associated policy reforms. All of

       which aimed to bolster government ownership of the Operation and to integrate the program into

       national actions thereby not creating parallel processes for the implementation. These policy reforms and

       indicators collectively aimed to fill critical gaps in the institutional and financial framework to strengthen

       the government’s ability to comprehensively manage and reduce hazard and climate risks as well as to

       respond to the impacts of disasters and improve the country’s financial resilience.



       •    Pillar 1 to fortify institutional, planning and policy frameworks to manage climate and disaster risk.

            The policy reforms and associated activities under this pillar supported the GoK’s capacity to

            formulate, prioritize, finance, implement and monitor actions to support the national DRM

            framework.

       •    Pillar 2 to strengthen resilience to climate and disaster risk in the urban and water sectors. The policy

            reforms and associated activities under this pillar contributed to reinforce the overall country’s

            capacity of strategic sectors to mainstream DRM and CCA into development planning and public

            investment systems. They also contributed to building urban resilience10 and improving flood risk

            management.

       •    Pillar 3 to build national financial capacity to advance climate adaptation and mitigation, as well as

            respond to the impacts of natural hazards. The policy reforms and associated activities under this

            pillar supported the development of national financial capacity for early action for both fast and slow

            onset disasters and contributed to increasing transparency, effectiveness, and efficacy of the

            government’s interventions for climate adaptation and mitigation, including disaster response.



            B.       Significant Changes During Implementation



       15.        In March 2020, with half the implementation period remaining, the GoK declared a state of

       emergency due to the COVID-19 pandemic. As a result of the pandemic, the GoK faced substantive fiscal

       constraints as resources were required to address the emergency, and national priorities shifted to

       emergency response and recovery. Social distancing measures resulted in further challenges as the

       Government was not able to convene physical meetings and capacity-building and training activities

       needed to be held virtually, both of which limited interaction and knowledge transfer. All of which

   II.    ASSESSMENT OF KEY PROGRAM DESIGN AND OUTCOMES



         17.         The overall assessment of the Cat DDO’s design and outcomes is Moderately Unsatisfactory

         (MU) with the Relevance of the prior actions assessed as Satisfactory and the Achievement of the

         Objectives (Efficacy) assessed as MU as only three out of ten indicators were fully achieved. The Cat

         DDO was approved in June 2018 and closed, fully disbursed, in September 2021. Though all the indicator

         targets were not reached, key achievements of the Operation include broadening the GoK’s

         understanding of climate and hazard risks and enhancing GoK’s capacity to manage these multiple

         hazards; promoting urban resilience as a key factor of economic development; and strengthening the

         fiscal capacity of the GoK to finance effective and timely response following a disaster. Of the ten expected

         results being monitored, one is exceeded, two are fully achieved, one is substantially achieved, four are

         partially achieved, and two not achieved (Table 1). In addition, the implementation of the reforms as well

         as the steps taken towards advancing the actions for indicators have directly contributed to the PDO and

         building Kenya’s resilience to climate and disaster risks.

       22.         Overall, the Operation’s efficacy is rated moderately unsatisfactory as three results

       indicators (RIs) were fully achieved, one substantially achieved, four partially achieved and two not

       policies to actions that positively impact communities and the economy. They came directly from the

       priority actions and next steps identified in the respective policies and were selected in agreement from

       58.          The GoK policies supported by the Operation had a positive poverty and social impact.

       households. The Operation also improved flood and drought management (RI7), which would benefit the

       poor and small-scale farmers who suffer disproportionately from the impact of drought in Kenya. More

       indirectly, the Operation directly supported the country’s more vulnerable populations by contributing to

       the adoption of mechanisms to increase the GoK’s fiscal resilience and capacity to mobilize resources in

       the aftermath of a disaster. By improving DRM (RI3), the activities under the Operation could reduce post-

       disaster disruption of public utilities, such as potable water or sewage systems, of communications, and

       60.         Institutional strengthening and technical capacity building are among the biggest

  V. RISK TO SUSTAINABILITY OF DEVELOPMENT OUTCOMES



       71.         The program outcomes are likely to be sustained but there are institutional capacity and

       resource risks. The activities under the Operation address national priorities and have the ownership of

       the relevant agencies, both of which contribute to their sustainability. Throughout the lifetime of this

       Operation, the institutional and technical capacities of involved government agencies with regards to

       EP&R, water resource management, urban resilience, resilient infrastructure, risk information, and DRF

       were strengthened, which resulted in the adoption the policies and plans. The GoK is also taking steps to

       continue advancing the reform agenda by, inter alia,

        (i) establishing DRM as a standalone sector, jointly with drought, in MTP IV (2023-2027);

        (ii) updating the national Disaster Risk Financing Strategy in line with MTV IV;

        (iii) mandating that all counties have emergency/contingency plans, and setting-up a 5-year timetable

              to update the existing Plans;

        (iv) developing additional county risk profiles utilizing the new methodology;

        (v) sensitizing the relevant stakeholders on the updated National Building Code and Physical Planning

              Handbook; and

        (vi) mainstreaming of disaster expenditure reporting into the Government Reporting System, including

              the capacity building of MDAs and County Governments on the reporting.



       72.         However, for these actions to be sustainable, implementation capacity should be

       strengthened, and adequate resources set aside. Securing the necessary financial resources for

       implementation is a risk that significantly increased given the economic and financial impacts related to

       COVID-19. The WBG continues to support Kenya, but budget constraints limit the space for implementing

 VI. LESSONS AND NEXT PHASE



      A.    Lessons Learned



       73.        Kenya demonstrated considerable fiscal discipline by only making a partial withdrawal for the

       flooding. Kenya only drew down about 35% of total resources from operation with floods, as they

       recognized they were still exposed. This was in part due to the capacity building around DRF expenditures

       under RI9. With COVID-19 they drew down the remaining resources.



       74.        A programmatic approach of climate resilience projects, both IPF and DPFs, may be needed to

       provide continuous, hands-on technical support to DRM and climate change in the medium to long term.



       75.        The implementation of the operation demonstrated important challenges in horizontal

       coordination across national line Ministries, and similarly vertical coordination between national and

       county governments. These challenges were arguably exacerbated by the nature of a Cat DDO’s

       disbursement structure which, as budget support, does not flow to participating line Ministries or sub-

       national governments associated with implementation of the operation, and are not linked to the

       implementation of the defined reform agenda as defined by the operation’s Results Indicators.



       76.          Cat DDOs in IDA countries should be designed with the context in mind. More specifically

      (i) Tailor IDA Cat DDOs to account for the IDA countries’ limited capacity. This was the first IDA Cat

            DDO, and the policy matrix was robust with strong policy actions and many RIs, comparable to an

            IBRD Cat DDO. Given the limited human, technical and financial capacity for IDA countries, one finding

            of the team is that the strength and number of policy actions and indicators could have been reduced.

      (ii) Discuss and agree upon the approvals, budget, and financing to achieve the indicators during

            preparation. An implementation plan, including budget, stakeholders, and approval processes, should

            be developed, before negotiations. Teams should carefully consider whether to say “draft” or

            “adopt/approval” and clearly define the level of adoption/approval in the wording of the RIs as

            adoption/approval may be beyond the control of the Lead agencies.

      (iii) Engage and sensitize line agencies continuously to better understand the Cat DDO, in particular

            during preparation. Working with Governments not familiar with DPFs is challenging. To address this

            the team developed knowledge products on the Cat DDO. The WBG could consider scaling up these

            products to support other Task Teams. Despite this, line agencies remained unclear on the

            implementation modality of the budget support, as well as the value proposition as they would not

            receive funds as they would under an IPF. It is also important to make sure all relevant MDAs are

            included in the list of implementers for the project to ensure their engagement. Another

            recommendation is that on the first engagement in a country with no DPFs, the WBG could consider

            a hybrid DPF/Cat DDO or IPF/Cat DDO to mobilize resources to support the implementation of

            activities under the prior actions to achieve the results indicators.

      (iv) Develop annual intermediate indicators to better track progress of Cat DDOs. Unlike DPFs, Cat DDOs

            have a multi-year implementation period, as such, Cat DDOs should have annual targets to make it

            easier to measure progress (WBG) and track key milestones. For the Government, it would show their

            efforts in terms of processes and activities that they are undertaking towards the final target.

       B.     Next Phase



       77.         Although existing operations address aspects climate resilience, there is a strong rationale

       for a comprehensive DRM and resilience engagement in Kenya, given the challenges faced due to the

       COVID-19 pandemic as well as increasing climate change-related hazards. Under the Kenya Urban

       Support Program (P156777) and the Kenya Integrated Devolution and Urban Support Program (P177048)

       which is under preparation, the WBG continues to provide support to urban resilience and climate

       resilience. Under the Kenya Water Security and Climate Resilience Project (P117635) support is being

       provided to enhance water resource management and build the capacity of institutions to manage

       climate-related risks. In addition, there is WBG TA to support the development of the Urban Resilience

       Strategies and the multi-hazard resilient design standards and maps. Moreover, all WBG projects in Kenya

       include climate co-benefits. However, a DRM project would be beneficial to build on the results achieved

       through implementing and advancing the DRM and climate agenda.

  1.          Overall, the representatives of the Ministries, Departments and Agencies (MDAs) who participated

  2.          About the ICR. The ICR team explained that the purpose of the ICR was to evaluate the

  were addressed. The ICR team added that the lessons learned from the Operation would be used to inform

  future projects in Kenya and future Cat DDOs globally.



  3.           Overall Assessment. The ICR team found that the Operation contributed to building national

  capacity to manage climate and hazard risks by, inter alia, broadening the Government of Kenya’s (GoK’s)

  understanding of climate and hazard risks and enhancing GoK’s capacity to manage these multiple hazards;

  promoting urban resilience as a key factor of economic development; and strengthening the fiscal capacity of

  the GoK to finance effective and timely response following a disaster. Hundreds of stakeholders have been

  sensitized, trained, and capacitated on climate resilience and related tools. However, there were delays in the

  full achievement of the results indicators with three (RIs 1, 7, 10) of ten being fully achieved. In addition, RI 2

  is substantially achieved, and four results indicators have been partially achieved (RIs 3, 6, 8, 9) and two Results

  Indicators (RIs 4, 5) with progress made but not fully achieved. The MDAs noted that was in part due to the

  ambitious nature of the operation, structure of some of the results indicators, procurement challenges,

  legislative processes that have to be followed, lack of involvement of some of entities from inception of the

  operation, the global COVID-19 pandemic, election year 2022 that shortened the calendar of Parliament and

  County Assemblies, and lack of sufficient resources to complete the tasks to achieve the indicators.
