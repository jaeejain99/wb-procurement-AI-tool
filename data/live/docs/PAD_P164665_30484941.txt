I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT



A. Country Context



      1.      The Republic of Ghana is a Lower Middle-income (LMIC) country in West Africa. With a population of

      approximately 30 million, it is considered among the most peaceful and democratic countries in Sub-Sahara Africa.

      Ghana’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth rate accelerated in the 2000s and reached an annual average of

      approximately 9 percent between 2008 and 20121. This robust growth was underpinned by positive terms of trade

      and large investment inflows, particularly in the extractive industries. Macroeconomic conditions worsened after

      2012 as weaker fiscal and monetary policies, terms of trade shocks from lower oil prices and electricity rationing

      slowed growth to an average of four percent between 2014 and 2016. Inflation, which remained above 17 percent

      for most of 2016, dropped to 9.6 percent in July 20172. A large election cycle-related fiscal slippage in December

      2016 pushed the gross public debt from 53 percent of GDP in 2013 to 73.4 percent of GDP in 20163. GDP growth

      was 3.7 percent for 2016, but the current account deficit narrowed to 6.6 percent of GDP in 2016 from 7.6 percent

      the previous year4. Ghana held its presidential and parliamentary elections on December 7, 2016. These elections

      ushered in a new Government formed by the New Patriotic Party (NPP), under the leadership of President Nana

      Addo Dankwa Akufo-Addo. The NPP Government came to power with promises which included macro-economic

      reforms, improving service delivery and strengthening private sector development.

      2.      While 2017 saw a turnaround in macroeconomic performance, reinforcing the Government’s resolve to

      put the economy on a positive pedestal, some challenges remain. The fiscal deficit dropped to 5.9 percent of

      GDP in 2017 from 9.3 percent of GDP in 20165, underpinned by serious fiscal consolidation efforts aimed at

      correcting the large slippages in 2016. Total revenue (including grants) underperformed by 1.1 percent of GDP in

      20176. As a result, the authorities limited expenditures to match the lower revenues: expenditures on goods and

      services were contained, clearance of arrears was lower than envisaged, and transfers to other Government units

      were low. The primary balance improved from a deficit in 2016 to a surplus of 0.8 percent of GDP in 20177. The

      steep fiscal consolidation and the implementation of the Medium-Term Debt Strategy yielded some positive

      results as the debt-to-GDP ratio fell from 73.4 percent in 2016 to 69.2 percent in December 20178. The

      Government also capped transfers to Earmarked Funds at 25 percent of tax revenues. However, unpaid arrears

      remain a challenge: the Auditor General (AG) reported that out of arrears and unpaid claims of over GHS 11 billion

      at end-2016, only GHS 6.3 billion could be validated9; the Ministry of Finance (MoF) states that arrears as of end-

      2016 amount to GHS 3.9 billion. Such arrears will impact, to varying degrees, the ability of Ministries, Departments

      and Agencies (MDAs) to deliver services, as the Government undertakes remedial actions in coming years. The

      Government plans to mobilize significant revenues during fiscal 2019 and later, and to clear all arrears by 2021.

      3.       Ghana achieved significant poverty reduction in the last twenty-five years. Ghana has been at the

      4.       Despite Ghana's economic growth in the past two decades, some challenges remain on institutional

      quality and Government effectiveness. Institutional reforms designed to promote economic development,

      strengthen the investment climate and improve the quality of public management have generally been

      undermined by weak policy coordination and implementation. Reforms, for example those intended to improve

      the quality of public sector performance or improve service delivery, the introduction of service standards, shared

      services, wage reform or decentralization, have not been fully implemented. Inadequate commitment to

      performance management and accountability for delivery has affected the realization of important strategic

      pronouncements by successive administrations. In the process, some important projects have not been

      completed; important policies not fully implemented; and plans for new ones - though grounded in immense

      optimism - are often dropped with changes in administrations. The result: despite a favorable and stable

      environment, democratic consolidation, and human resource (HR) capacity, structural transformation has eluded

      Ghana. Ghana remains in a low-level equilibrium, experiencing marginal economic growth amid persistent

      horizontal inequalities, repeated economic volatility and low levels of private sector participation. To ensure

      economic growth and transformation Ghana needs to focus on tackling the institutional deficiencies that

      undermine the quality of public administration. This could include ensuring efficiency and accountability for

      results in implementing Government programs through rigorous monitoring and evaluation (M&E) and updating

      and enforcing performance standards for delivery of administrative services.

      5.      The Government has proposed several strategic plans to address some of these challenges. The 2018-

      2023 National Public Sector Reform Strategy (NPSRS) that the project supports is one of several strategic plans

      the Government has prepared in recent times. The NPSRS guides the Government’s public sector reform agenda

      through its six pillars: Citizen- and Private Sector-Focused Public Sector; Capable and Disciplined Work Force;

      Strengthened Public Sector Regulatory Framework; Modernized and Improved Work Condition; Strengthened

      Local Governance Structures, and Digitized Public Sector Services and Systems.14 While the NPSRS is an ambitious

      strategy for a five-year period, it is based on the understanding that a strong and effective public sector is critical

      for Ghana’s transformation.

      under Chapter 14 (Article 190 Clause 1a-d) of the 1992 Constitution stipulating that Ghana’s public services shall

7.       The Government recognizes that poor public sector performance can undermine economic growth. For

instance, the 2014 Senchi National Economic Forum expressed dissatisfaction that some public servants display a

lukewarm attitude toward the work they do16. There is evidence of poor capacity of MDAs to formulate and

implement policies to improve service delivery to citizens and firms.17 In Ghana, development plans and programs

have a very poor record of implementation. Many people feel concerned about the waste of manpower and

resources in formulating plans and programs that are not likely to be implemented.”18 Two of the major causes

for the failure of past anti-corruption strategies in Ghana are lack of effective and sustained coordination, and the

insufficient Government commitment to, and limited support for, the implementation of anti-corruption

strategies.19 Furthermore, limited coordination between sector MDAs emphasizes that “one of the problems

identified as a cause of failure of past programs is individual Ministry led programs with … few common linkages”.20

8.      Previous attempts at improving public sector performance have been expansive, covering all facets of

the public sector—with modest results. Previous public service reform and modernization efforts over the last

thirty years have had a very mixed record of success: they tended to be supply-driven, weakly coordinated,

without clearly defined priorities, under-resourced and insufficiently monitored on delivery of results and change

on the ground, resulting in lack of continuity in implementation.

9.       Ghana’s public sector faces an “organizational performance” problem. Despite the availability of a

performance appraisal system, there is a disconnect between the formal structures of managing performance—

which exist—and the functional usage of these structures to drive performance and deliver results. In the absence

of an effective organizational performance management system, it is difficult to keep Government entities

focused on delivering on short- and medium-term goals. MDAs across the public sector have general statements

of vision and goals. However, because of low capacity or inadequate resources, there is little follow-through to

mobilize internal organizational resources (financial and human) to deliver. For instance, in a recent survey of the

public service, 77 percent of respondents reported that inadequate funds and conditions of service were the most

important factors undermining organizational performance.21 For most employees, there is an implicit

understanding that there is no cost to poor performance, and that rewards are not based on performance.

Besides, weaknesses in central agencies’ effectiveness not only affect these agencies’ performance, they also

10.      There is recognition of the importance of M&E in Government, but Ghana’s public sector M&E systems

face many challenges. These include the absence of updated M&E manuals and guidelines; lack of M&E core

competencies within MDAs; largely supply-driven M&E systems; inadequate understanding of results-based

collection and reporting templates and instruments. Ministries have M&E units known as Policy, Planning,

Monitoring, and Evaluation Directorates (PPMED) charged with undertaking M&E functions, but their capacity

varies. The National Development Planning Commission (NDPC) – a constitutional body with M&E functions - lacks

capacity and resources to discharge its M&E role. Lastly, to the extent that M&E systems are increasingly based

on information and communications technology (ICT), coordinating ICT design and implementation with the

National Information Technology Agency (NITA) is challenging. A Ministry of Monitoring and Evaluation (MoME),

established under the Office of the President (OoP), conducts real-time monitoring of strategic programs and

projects. MoME reinforces the national M&E system to ensure effective implementation of priority programs.22

11.      To address long-standing M&E issues, it would be desirable for the Government to undertake two key

sets of actions. High-level endorsement of the role of M&E in improving public sector performance has, in effect,

been done through establishing the MoME. It would now be desirable to: (a) strengthen the institutional capacity

of MoME and related entities and (b) outline and make a strong business case for the use of M&E for results

tracking in MDAs and metropolitan, municipal and district assemblies (MMDAs) and for sectoral decision-making.



12.      The lack of a strong performance culture and the absence of a results monitoring culture affects

downstream performance of frontline entities in the delivery of administrative services. There are three major

weaknesses in the delivery of public services: poor institutional capacity of MDAs to formulate and implement

policies for service delivery; inadequate support from central agencies; and limited coordination between sector

MDAs.23 These structural inefficiencies have contributed to low levels of service quality in frontline entities. The

delivery of basic administrative services is encumbered by bureaucratic processes which cause excessive delays.

For example, obtaining a passport or land title can take up to 12 months.24 Citizens often encounter rent-seeking

by public servants in application processes requiring human transactions. Afrobarometer reports that about 86

percent of Ghanaian citizens believe that Government officials are involved in corruption.25 In many cases, citizens

have to pay informal fees to middlemen (‘goro boys’), purportedly connected to officials, to bypass formal

processes to access services (Box 1).

                                           Box 1 The Elusive Ghanaian Passport

Performance, governance and corruption issues pertaining to passport issuance and renewal by the Passport

Office (PO) are well-known and of long standing. In many Passport Application Centers, stories abound about

applicants waiting for months to obtain a passport through the prescribed process, when others pay middlemen

and obtain their passports within days. The PO issued or renewed 345,575 passports in 201726. The PO stated that

more than 67,000 issued passports have not been picked up by applicants and are lying in Passport Application

Centers in Accra as of August 2018, reportedly because applicants did not provide their correct addresses27.

However, anecdotal information indicates that ‘unpaid’ informal fees to goro boys are the main reason why these

passports – despite being issued - are not handed over to the respective applicants.

The PO states that disaggregated data on passport issuance or renewal (e.g. by location), is difficult to obtain, that

it does not maintain records of complaints received, or track how complaints are handled. In such a situation,

obtaining credible baseline performance information is itself a challenge. In addition, Ghana issues passports valid

for five years, instead of the ten years common for other countries. Despite critics pointing out that issuing

passports valid for ten years could reduce citizen harassment, delays and corruption, this has not yet been done

by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Regional Integration (MFA&RI), of which the PO is a subordinate entity.

Periodic media coverage, other forms of public feedback (e.g. Facebook and other social media postings) and the

World Bank-commissioned 2017-2018 Survey on Administrative Services in Ghana (see Annex 4) indicate high

applicant frustration and high informal costs associated with passport issuance and renewal.

In addition to lack of reliable data on performance and service delivery, the PO regularly features in print28 and

broadcast media due to repeated delays in issuing passports (beyond the PO service standard of 21 days for

normal service and nine days for expedited service29), reported non-availability of passport booklets30, and

equipment breakdowns which can take weeks to resolve.



13.     The introduction of Client Service Charters (CSC) and Client Service Units (CSUs) in MDAs was meant to

address citizen complaints and information requests, but this did not happen. This effort did not translate into

improvements in service delivery because not all MDAs established CSUs. Where CSUs existed, many never

became fully functional due to lack of resources and an unmotivated civil service. Moreover, many CSCs have not

been updated or published. Over time, this increased citizens’ frustration with Government services, and

contributed to loss of public trust in Government (Figure 1). The Management Services Department (MSD) of the

Office of the Head of Civil Service (OHCS) has provided guidance to civil service entities in developing CSCs. The

MSD needs to be strengthened to more effectively assist MDAs to improve CSC quality (by updating CSC formats

and templates) and monitor CSC implementation. The PSC, which oversees public servants, also plans to update

and roll out CSC formats and templates. The OHCS and PSC will, as part of their oversight roles, monitor

compliance with CSC implementation by MDAs within their oversight.

Component 2: Improving Efficiency, Accountability and Citizen Engagement in the Delivery of Selected Services

(US$20.19 million equivalent)

29.      This component supports to improve efficiency, accountability and citizen engagement in the delivery

of selected services by Selected Entities. There are four major sets of constraints to service delivery

improvements, namely (a) upstream challenges, including lack of streamlined and standardized systems and

processes for service delivery and results tracking, and inadequate and unpredictable budget releases for goods

and services; (b) downstream challenges, including limited access points and insufficient MDA institutional

capacity to formulate and manage efficient and accountable processes at the point of service to citizens and firms;

(c) horizontal challenges including limited coordination between sector MDAs; and (d) delays, corruption, lack of

complaints handling and tracking mechanisms and a general lack of public accountability and citizen engagement

pertaining to key public services, across MDAs.

E. Rationale for World Bank Involvement and Role of Partners



      43.     The activities outlined in this project aim to provide key support services not usually amenable to

      sources of financing other than the Government. This project will support the development of core public sector

      systems that are directly linked to the performance of the day to day functions of Government and execution of

      the mandates of ministries. The Government's direct intervention is therefore critical in ensuring that it supports

      the emergence of a strong public sector and the establishment of a strong environment for the execution of the

      National Coordinated Program for Economic and Social Development Policies (2017-2024).

      44.     This project is a continuation of recent World Bank engagement in Ghana aimed at strengthening public

      sector performance and service delivery. The project complements ongoing World Bank-financed projects

      supporting Public Financial Management (PFM), economic governance, decentralization, and competitiveness by

      addressing the functional and structural aspects that could undermine the development objectives of the existing

      projects. Finally, with its long term engagement the World Bank in Ghana has positioned itself as a leading donor

      among other DPs in Ghana for its expertise and experience in supporting public sector reforms.



F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design



      45.      Project design has been guided by global lessons and those from World Bank and development partner

      (DP) engagement in Ghana’s public sector reform agenda. First, client participation in design and implementation

      is crucial. The preparation of this project has been thoroughly consultative where all key stakeholders have been

      engaged in discussions about the scope, components, and costs. Second, interventions should target selected

      organizations. A whole-of-sector focus tends to spread out resources, concentrate interests opposed to reforms

      and - given weak capacity - produce few results. In this project, focus on a few key ministries at the operational

      level, on the one hand, and intensive support of a few agencies critical for administrative service delivery provide

      a sharper project focus. Third, short and medium-term interventions should be anchored in a comprehensive

      strategic framework. The design of the project has been tailored to incorporate this lesson through sequenced

      activities and milestones, some of which are anchored to project results indicators and covenants related to

      implementation. This allows for experimentation and mid-course correction. Finally, target outcomes should be

      ambitious, but realistic. Some previous institutional reform projects have tended to include unrealistic results and

      implementation timelines out of sync with the institutional context in Ghana. This project has focused on modest

      results, sequenced in a way that each builds on the next. Project M&E staff will be trained to collect data and

      progressively measure results and outcomes during implementation at both component and project levels.

A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis (if applicable)



      Technical Analysis



      58.     The project is based on sound technical design informed by experience with implementing public sector

      reform projects in Ghana and elsewhere. This project builds on achievements in implementing public sector

      reforms and on lessons from Ghana’s mixed record in undertaking government-wide institutional reforms. The

      World Bank has appraised the technical design of the project and considers the project to be high-risk but

      potentially high-reward. The project has been designed based on the NPSRS, and other government policies. It

      builds on the findings of recent World Bank analytical work, including a review of governance constraints to service

      delivery (2017); a survey on user experiences with delivery of administrative services (2018); and the Systematic

      Country Diagnostic (2018).



      Economic Analysis



      59.     The project economic analysis considers direct and indirect benefits and costs associated with changes

      in economic welfare arising from the project. The project is expected to generate multiple economic benefits.

      Improved efficiency in service delivery – including through a progressively increasing share of online services - will

      reduce costs for citizens and firms, freeing up their time to engage in productive and income generating activities.

      However, quantifying many of these benefits depends on the availability and reliability of data. Adhering to service

      standards as envisaged in updated CSCs and improved customer orientation of existing public services are

      expected to reduce applicants’ time and travel costs (by reducing visits to offices to access PSRRP-supported

      services) particularly for those in remote areas and from low-income groups. Cost savings may be significant for

      services such as passport issuances and renewals. PSRRP-supported activities to improve PO service delivery, if

      implemented, will result in reduced informal payments and other out-of-pocket costs for applicants, contributing

      to cost savings. Cost savings will also arise for other PSRRP-supported services, but none are likely to approach

      the potential scale or impact as those in respect of the PO (see Box 1).



      60.      The economic benefits from reduced costs for citizens and firms could be significant. Time savings for

      citizens and firms in obtaining services will allow them to engage in productive and income generating activities.

      Assuming 25 services to be improved and a 20 percent time reduction, the net present value of these savings is

      about US$44.6 million in real terms at a 12 percent discount rate, based on estimates for the target services (in

      the absence of reliable data). This is a conservative estimate. The actual benefits associated with lower compliance

      and turnaround costs are likely to be higher because travel time, travel cost and accommodation cost are not

      taken into account due to lack of data. Key assumptions underpinning these estimates include: (a) the benefits

      will accrue in the fifth year of the project; (b) citizens’ time to obtain public services comprises time to receive an

      application, time to fill out and submit an application and all the required documents, and time to follow up and

      finally obtain a final document/service; (c) the number of users for each public service remains constant; (d) the

      estimated average annual wage of GHS 72,415 in 2018, converted into a daily wage for the purpose of the analysis





                                                                                                                       Page 34

The World Bank

Ghana: Public Sector Reform for Results Project (P164665)







(22 working days per month); (e) the annual real growth rate for wages is 2 percent and remains constant; and (f)

the exchange rate is constant and equals GHS 4.74 to US$1 as of August 31, 2018. The time savings will be higher

if estimates cover a ten-year period. The analysis is based on publicly available data.



61.      Economic costs will fall as citizens and firms need to pay no or lower informal fees to intermediaries and

other agents for supporting documentation. The payment of ‘speed money’ and bribes is expected to decline

because a growing number of applicants will be provided services within turnaround times specified in SCSs, there

will be less reason to incur additional informal costs to speed up the process, and there will be a gradual increase

in availability of information about fees and processing steps to citizens and firms. The distorting impact of bribes

causing lower rates of service use forms the real economic cost (the value of the reduced services received by

individuals and firms relative to the economic cost of the resources used to supply these services). Thus, bribes

forgone will represent a substantial improvement in economic welfare of individuals and firms. Individuals will

also benefit from improved transparency and access to information for better decision-making.



62.      The project is likely to generate efficiency gains for the Government. Simplified business procedures and

the ability to access services and information through multiple means (including mobile applications) will result in

reduced revenue leakages from official fees for services, which distort economic behavior. Economic costs of the

project will involve government staff time spent on project implementation, as well as training, communications,

outreach, complaints handling and DLI verification – all undertaken as part of project implementation. However,

the expected economic benefits are likely to significantly outweigh the economic costs of the project.



63.     The project is also expected to generate economic benefits not easily quantifiable. These include better

staff morale in the Selected Entities, especially the service delivery entities, stemming from organizational

improvements, increased entity responsiveness to user needs/priorities (especially to the needs of vulnerable

groups) stemming from the introduction of updated CSCs, training in service delivery, performance monitoring, a

unified NITA-provisioned Help Desk for Selected Entities, multiple channels for beneficiary and citizen feedback

and complaints handling and outreach activities supported by the project. Better data security will ensure

protection of service users’ information. In addition to direct economic benefits, the project will also positively

affect access, quality and timeliness of delivery of the selected services for citizens and businesses. It will also

contribute to improving citizens’ and firms’ actual experiences with, and perceptions about, the public service

delivery system as a whole. This in turn could contribute to increasing levels of satisfaction of citizens and

businesses with the selected services and Selected Entities.

V. KEY RISKS



      86.      The overall risk rating for the proposed project is High, mainly due to high or substantial risk ratings for

      the following categories:

      a)       Macroeconomic risks: The macroeconomic risk is rated substantial mainly due to Ghana’s ongoing

      constraints in revenue mobilization and in controlling its public expenditures, compounded by the overhang of a

      large volume of arrears, as stated earlier. The mitigating actions include incorporating DLIs into the project design

      (to inject predictable releases of resources to participating entities) and inclusion of project activities to support

      some reduction of arrears by Selected Entities.

      b)       Technical design of project: This risk is rated high. The project design, involving sixteen entities including

      the OSM under the OoP, is inherently risky. Mitigating actions have been built into the design, including through

      DLIs, clarity on entity-wise financing and strong oversight, ring-fencing CHRAJ activities to administrative justice

      alone, special audit provisions to ascertain the eligibility of CHRAJ expenditures, and a strong technical and

      coordination role for the OSM and the PMU. The OSM needs to work closely with beneficiary entities since many

      activities will need M&E capacity to achieve results and trigger the DLIs.

      c)       Institutional capacity for implementation and sustainability: This risk is rated high. Ghana has a history

      of uneven accomplishment in consistently pursuing a path of institutional reform, and institutions are fragmented.

      Uneven incentives for reforms remain a key implementation risk. In addition, budgetary realities imply that some

      entities’ budgetary allocations for goods and services – key to service provision for most entities – may be released

      late, or not at all. This could jeopardize achievement of key project results. The likely persistence of inadequate

      resources for operations and maintenance across government constitutes a high risk to sustainability. Mitigating

      measures include provision of resources through DLIs. Over the medium term, PSRRP-supported improvements

      could help entities improve resource mobilization (e.g. through improved collection of fees), lower revenue

      leakages (by moving to non-cash modes of payment) and become more efficient in service provision.

      d)      Fiduciary risks: Fiduciary risks are rated high. The OSM under the OoP is a new ministry and will implement

      a World Bank-financed project for the first time. The Chief Accountant and Procurement Specialist attached to the

      project have sufficient experience. However, there are potential fiduciary risks that could arise from OSM’s lack

      of familiarity with World Bank procedures and operations. The World Bank Fiduciary Team will maintain close





                                                                                                                       Page 40

The World Bank

Ghana: Public Sector Reform for Results Project (P164665)







contact with the OSM/PMU team, involve staff in ongoing specialized training and advisory, and if necessary,

propose additional safeguards to ensure proper management of project funds.

e)       Stakeholder risks: This risk is rated high. The project has numerous stakeholders. The OSM under OoP will

be responsible for implementation, coordination, stakeholder engagement, and public reporting. However, there

is a risk that some key stakeholders might not actively participate in project activities. This could arise from

interests opposed to reforms and modernization, from coordination failures or from lack of cooperation. The

project’s embedded activities to nurture and sustain public feedback and stakeholder consultations will contribute

to mitigating this risk, which will nevertheless remain high even after mitigation.

                       ANNEX 1: Implementation Arrangements and Support Plan



                              Ghana Public Sector Reform for Results Project



 Strategy and Approach for Implementation Support





 1.      Given the number of entities involved, this project will require intensive implementation support. To

 ensure better coordination, implementation will be decentralized to several entities responsible for functional

 areas of the project. These comprise PSC, OHCS, CHRAJ, MoME, NITA, MFA&RI, MESTI, MLGRD, MoI, MoT, DVLA,

 EPA, PO, GIS, BDR, and OSM.

 2.       The World Bank will provide implementation support with a diverse team of World Bank specialists

 who will maintain close engagement with the OSM under the OoP and other selected entities through regular

 visits and through audio and video-conferences. Several core team members are located in the Country Office,

 including the Financial Management and Procurement Specialists. Both will cover review of fiduciary aspects of

 the project. Such close engagement will be useful to anticipate implementation risks, support the Government in

 the technical aspects of the implementation and work closely with DPs.

 3.      World Bank implementation support will focus on helping the OSM under the OoP to ensure that the

 project results monitoring framework generates high quality data; following up on project implementation to

 ensure that all results indicators including DLIs are met in a timely manner; overseeing the validation process for

 DLIs and verification of disbursement; and providing technical support to the Selected Entities.

   Fiduciary Issues – Key Findings, Risks and Implementation Support Plan

       A. Financial Management

  4.      The overall FM risk is High. The FM risk rating will be reassessed quarterly during implementation. The

  OSM under the OoP has never implemented an IDA-funded project. Staff in the OSM under the OoP are not,

  therefore, familiar with the FM and Disbursement guidelines. Additionally, 16 Selected Entities are involved in

  implementation, posing significant risks, including potential ineligible expenditures. To mitigate such risks, the

  FMS will provide intensive training to PMU staff prior to any disbursements and periodically thereafter.

   5.      The project will emphasize the use of country systems in line with the World Bank’s practice of using

   those aspects of the county system deemed reliable. The project’s FM systems will therefore be mainstreamed

   as part of the existing GoG PFM arrangements under the PFM Act (Act 912), 2016.

   6.      The project’s FM arrangements satisfy the World Bank’s minimum requirements under Bank Policy and

   Bank Directive: IPF. The assessment complied with the FM Guidelines for World Bank-Financed Investment

   operations, effective March 1, 2010, and the AFTFM FM Assessment and risk rating principles.

   7.      Accounting arrangements: The overall FM responsibility for the project will be handled by the Head of

   Finance (HoF) with the support of a dedicated Chief Accountant as part of the proposed PMU. The HoF is a civil

   servant from the Controller and Accountant General’s Department (CAGD) in the grade of Chief Accountant. The

   HoF’s primary responsibility is to ensure that throughout implementation there are adequate FM systems in place

   which can report adequately on the use of project funds. In addition, the HoF will maintain oversight responsibility

   with regard to ensuring compliance with financial covenants such as preparing Withdrawal Applications,

   submitting Interim Unaudited Financial Reports (IUFRs), maintaining internal controls over project expenditure

   and engaging external auditors. With the support of the Chief Accountant, the HoF will be responsible for

   maintaining and operating the project’s designated account, assisting in the processing of payments to

   contractors and service providers and verifying and authorizing payments for all contracts and activities under

   this project to ensure that they are eligible under the FA. The Chief Accountant, working under the supervision of

   the HoF, will also be responsible for maintaining and operating the project’s designated account and supporting

   the processing of payments to contractors and service providers and verifying and authorizing payments for all

   contracts and activities under this project. The HoF and Project Accountant will be supported by a mix of qualified

   and partly qualified accounts staff.

   8.      Reporting: The OSM under the OoP uses the Ghana Integrated Financial Management Information

   System (GIFMIS) to process and report on payables. All other accounting transactions are prepared using a

   combination of manual processing and spreadsheets. The OSM under the OoP should consider configuring the

   existing GIFMIS system to process and report on all financial transactions related to this project. This would

   require staff set-up and end-user training.

   9.     Budgeting arrangements: The OSM under the OoP prepares its budget in accordance with budget

   preparation guidelines under the PFM Act 2016 and budget guidelines issued by the MoF. Annual budgeting will

   be done in line with the Government’s existing budget timetable (Medium-term Expenditure Framework

   reasonableness of budgets and monitor project budget performance. This can be done quarterly and the actual

   expenditure can be based on the IUFR as a monitoring tool.

   10.     Funds Flow: The OSM under the OoP will maintain two designated accounts: a US$ account to receive

   funds from IDA and a designated GHS account to draw down funds from the US$ account to pay for consultancy

   services, goods, training and operational costs.

       B. Modalities for Disbursements under RBF Components

   12.    The total resource allocation to be implemented by the OSM under the OoP on the RBF approach is

   US$11.8 million, comprising US$2.30 million under Sub-component 1.4 of Component 1 and US$9.50 million

   under Sub-component 2.3 of Component 2.

   13.     According to the project design, disbursements under this component provide for achievement of the

   DLIs which will be measured and valued in monetary terms for each respective year through a set of identifiable

   and measurable DLRs, specific results achieved in each year of implementation through the set of indicators (DLIs),

   over the project implementation period. These DLIs are considered significant indicators of performance that will

   influence behavioral and policy reforms required for achieving visible outcomes in Ghana’s public sector reform

   agenda. For each sub-component, the respective DLI has been has been disaggregated into a number of

   achievable DLRs, in turn translated into variable prices as the equivalent value for achieving the DLR in each year

   of implementation.

   14.     Meeting the DLRs defined in the Results Framework and Monitoring table and in the FA will constitute

   the primary basis for triggering disbursements under the project. The DLRs have been individually priced, and as

   such the eligible disbursement amount will be the sum of the achieved DLRs multiplied by the unitary monetary

   value (price) as per the disbursement schedule (see Table 4).

   15.       Since this an IPF operation, the underlying principle will be to disburse, after project effectiveness, an

   initial advance. The initial advance shall be based on the forecast funding requirement to potentially achieve the

   set of DLRs in each year based on the approved Annual Work Plan and Budget and based on a review of the Annual

   Work Plan and Budget forecast of the funding required, Subsequently on a half-yearly basis, the Recipient will

   provide satisfactory documentary evidence including (i) acceptable interim unaudited financial report (IUFR), (ii)

   EEP spending reports, and (iii) satisfactory evidence of independent verification of the set of DLRs which have

   been achieved for that reporting period. These reports will then form the basis of documenting the advances to

   the Designated Account. Subsequent advances will be made upon approval of the net cash requirements of

   forecast expenditure as reviewed and approved by the World Bank. Third-party verification by an IVA is included

   in the project management component (Component 4) and details of the independent verification process are at

   Annex 2. They will be further detailed in the POM.

   16.     However, in subsequent years and beyond the first year, only one DLR relating to the prior year can

   remain unmet to allow for disbursements of any further advances against the potential for meeting future period

   DLRs. This means that while noncompliance with a DLR in a period will result in funds associated with that DLR

   being withheld, disbursement associated with the achievement of other DLIs will not be affected. In situations

   where the Project is able to meet any of the DLRs beyond the value of the total advances granted, the Recipient

   can request for such values as a reimbursement into any government account other than the Designated Account.

   17.       Where achievement of a DLR cannot be certified, an amount equivalent to the unitary DLR price will be

   withheld or considered as undocumented and an outstanding obligation on the Recipient. This amount will be

   paid at any later date, during project life, and at the discretion of the World Bank’s task team when such

   achievement can be verified. The task team may consider that a later achievement of DLR performance would

   not qualify for disbursement against the unmet DLR if it determines that the on-schedule achievement of the DLR

   is critically fundamental to achieving the overall objectives of the project. A schedule of the DLI/DLR matrix with

   the indicative DLR value is already included in the project Results Framework and more details are in a table (DLI

   Matrix) in Annex 2.

   expenditures on salaries paid for by the Government or incurred by the respective agencies. Such EEP would be

   verified by the IVA as part of the documentation for verifying the achievement of DLRs.

           C. Procurement

   25.     Procurement under the Project will be undertaken in accordance with the World Bank’s “Procurement

   The head of procurement at the PMU will lead the procurement team.

   27.     The project’s procurement risk is rated High. This is mainly because of the lack of experience and

   inadequate knowledge on the World Bank PF; the nature and values of expected procurements in the Project,

   compared with what the Recipient and procurement team are used to; and the procurement team’s insufficient

   experience with high-value procurements. In addition, the OSM under the OoP has never implemented a World

   Bank-financed project. The World Bank will provide continuous support and capacity-building for the project

   team members. The team will also attend procurement training being offered by the GIMPA on the World Bank

   PF as well as trainings and seminars organized by the World Bank.

   28.     PPSD Summary: The Recipient has prepared the PPSD and the related PP, and the World Bank provided

   clearance before negotiations. The PPSD indicated that there will be numerous opportunities in procurement in

   the delivery of services under the Project, to enable the government to achieve its primary objectives. The

   seamless budget-procurement-contract management module government is pursuing will mitigate payment risks

   and issues, while the macro-fiscal stability policies being pursued strongly by the government will contribute to

   improving the procurement environment to make it more competitive and realistic, and to provide value for

   money. The Project will depend on the experience of strong beneficiary agencies in implementing the

   Project. Procurement activities are detailed and reflected in the PP. The project will leverage technology to

   improve citizen participation, service delivery, monitoring and reporting, such as encouraging beneficiaries to

   send feedback, complaints, and satisfaction ratings through mobile phone messages, mobile applications, web-

   based applications to the Implementing Agency and other Selected Entities including the CHRAJ. Complaint

   review and resolution will be undertaken by the Recipient in line with World Bank requirements in the Recipient

   Regulations as well as under the relevant provisions of the POM. Indeed, a specific project results indicator tracks

   progress on complaint resolution and grievance redress.

                                      ANNEX 3. Financial and Economic Analysis

 Financial Analysis

 1.       Financial analysis of the project considers costs and benefits using a cash flow approach. Financial

 benefits comprise (i) savings from reduction of administrative costs in providing PSRRP-supported services such

 as issuance/renewal of passports, resident permits, environmental permits, driver licenses and vehicle

 registrations; and registration of births and deaths and issuance of birth and death certificates; and (ii) incremental

 increases in government revenue as a result of increased volumes of the selected services provided. These

 improvements will stem from stronger organizational performance at the five front-line PSRRP-supported service

 delivery entities (BDR, PO, GIS, EPA and DVLA) and their parent ministries, which will result in improved efficiency

 in delivering the PSRRP-supported services.

 2.     Increased volumes of services provided are expected to lead to incremental increases in government

 revenue, which are mainly expected to come from:

    Expansion of coverage of selected services – to address the chronic lack of service access points - to Ghana’s

     under-served and remote areas, especially to northern regions and some pockets in the east and south-west

     (see Map at Annex 5 – these coincide with red-shaded high-poverty areas in the map). This is especially true

     for BDR, which has staff-to-population ratios ranging from 1:190,000 to 1:150,000 for under-served regions

     (compared to 1:50,000 or less for urban areas and southern regions). Project activities will enable BDR to

     penetrate such areas and provide access through greater mobility of BDR staff in the under-served regions

     and through ‘instance capturing’ of births and deaths in such areas, in collaboration with health workers. The

     PO and DVLA also plan to open more service access points (such as Passport Application Centers) in currently

     under-served areas, which will expand their respective service coverage.

    Reduced turnaround time for selected services adhering to updated CSCs, resulting in reduced transaction

     costs for citizens, will likely increase demand for such services, in turn contributing to increased government

     revenues. While it is difficult to estimate the likely increase in demand, it could be significant for some services,

     such as registration of births and deaths; issuance of driver licenses and vehicle registrations; and

     issuance/renewals of passports and EPA permits. Currently, the time required to obtain such services, the

     associated uncertainty and informal costs appear to deter significant numbers of potential applicants from

     seeking or obtaining such services.

 3.       While there are significant upfront financial costs, the PSRRP design mitigates some of them. There are

 costs associated with developing and updating CSC formats and templates, business process streamlining and

 automation, improving staff mobility, training of staff and educating citizens in the use of e-services (including

 mobile applications) to access or apply for services, obtain information, provide feedback and lodge grievances

 and complaints. However, the operating costs for the updated CSCs will likely be offset by lower costs due to

 efficiency improvements following business process streamlining, some digitization and greater use of mobile

 applications. In addition, under the PSRRP, another significant element of upfront financial costs will be mitigated

 by relying on NITA-provided ICT-related technical support and services for Selected Entities. NITA has already

 invested in core ICT infrastructure, skilled staffing, and ICT service provision (much of it through the World Bank-

 financed e-Transform Project). Hence individual service delivery entities will avoid significant financial costs for

 goods, services and capabilities (such as network infrastructure, connectivity, data centers, server storage capacity

 and ICT Help Desk functions) which already exist in NITA. The use of NITA will also significantly mitigate the costs

 of employing and training staff with relevant capabilities to utilize and service ICT systems. Over time, PSRRP-

 supported organizational performance and service delivery improvements are expected to increase the number

 of entity staff engaged in client-facing roles to ensure increased customer service commitment and results on the

 4.      Quantification of efficiency gains is not feasible due to lack of data on savings from the use of digital

 infrastructure. Hypothetically, however, the financial benefits will comprise efficiency gains for government while

 the financial costs will be the same as the economic costs. Streamlining business processes and institutional

 reviews will create opportunities to reduce the cost of service delivery by rationalizing services and processes. In

 addition, using NITA infrastructure and facilities will lead to savings on operations and maintenance costs by all

 non-NITA entities participating in the project.

 Economic Analysis

 5.      The project economic analysis considers direct and indirect benefits and costs associated with changes

 in economic welfare arising from the project. The project is expected to generate multiple economic benefits.

 Improved efficiency in service delivery – including through a progressively increasing share of services that can be

 accessed online - will reduce costs for citizens and firms, freeing up their time to engage in productive and income

 generating activities. However, quantifying many of these benefits depends on the availability and reliability of

 data. Adhering to service standards as envisaged in updated CSCs and improved customer orientation of existing

 public services are expected to reduce applicants’ time and travel costs (by reducing visits to multiple

 offices/agencies to access PSRRP-supported public services) particularly for those living in remote areas and from

 low-income groups. In addition, PSRRP-supported accountability improvements (e.g. selected entities publicly

 reporting on service improvement-related targets and achievements; periodic beneficiary and citizen feedback

 through multiple project-embedded channels; improving CHRAJ’s performance in registering and disposing of

 complaints against administrative services) are envisaged to contribute to increased economic welfare including

 through reduced informal payments and bribes.

 6.      Cost savings are likely to be significant for some services (e.g. passport issuance and renewal). The

 PSRRP-supported activities to improve PO service delivery, if implemented, will result in reduced informal

 payments and other out-of-pocket costs for applicants and greater certainty and predictability in service delivery

 times, together contributing to cost savings. Cost savings will also arise for other PSRRP-supported services, but

 none are likely to have the potential scale or impact as those in respect of the PO.

 7.       The benefits from reduced costs for citizens and firms will be significant. Time savings for citizens and

 firms in obtaining services will allow them to engage in productive and income generating activities. Assuming 25

 services to be improved and a 20 percent time reduction, the net present value of these savings is about US$44.6

 million in real terms at a 12 percent discount rate, based on estimates for the target services (in the absence of

 reliable data). This is a conservative estimate. The actual benefits associated with lower compliance and

 turnaround costs are likely to be higher because travel time, travel cost and accommodation cost are not taken

 into account due to lack of data. Key assumptions underpinning these estimates include: (a) the benefits will

 accrue in the fifth year of the project; (b) citizens’ time to obtain public services comprises time to receive an

 application, time to fill out and submit an application and all the required documents, and time to follow up and

 finally obtain a final document/service; (c) the number of users for each public service remains constant; (d) the

 estimated average annual wage of GHS 72,415 in 2018, converted into a daily wage for the purpose of the analysis

 (22 working days per month); (e) the annual real growth rate for wages is 2 percent and remains constant; and (f)

 the exchange rate is constant and equals GHS 4.74 to US$1 as of August 31, 2018. The time savings will be much

 higher if estimates cover a ten-year period. The analysis is based on publicly available data.

 8.       Economic costs will also fall as citizens need to pay no or lower informal fees to intermediaries and

 other agents for supporting documentation. The payment of ‘speed money’ and bribes is expected to decline

 because a growing number of applicants will be provided services within turnaround times specified in CSCs, there

 will be less reason to incur additional informal costs to speed up the process, and there will be a gradual increase

 in availability of information about fees and processing steps to citizens and firms. The distorting impact of bribes

 causing lower rates of service use forms the real economic cost (the value of the reduced services received by

 individuals and firms relative to the economic cost of the resources used to supply these services). Thus, bribes

 forgone will represent a substantial improvement in economic welfare of individuals and firms. Individuals will

 also benefit from improved transparency and access to information for better decision-making.

 9.       The project is likely to generate efficiency gains for the government. Simplified business procedures and

 the ability to access services and information through multiple means (including through mobile applications) will

 also result in reduced revenue leakages from official fees for public services, which distort economic behavior.

 Economic costs of the project will involve government staff time spent on project implementation, as well as

 training, communications, outreach, complaints handling and DLI verification – all undertaken as part of project

 implementation. However, the expected economic benefits are likely to significantly outweigh the economic costs

 of the project.

 10.      The project is expected to generate several economic benefits which cannot be readily quantified. These

 include better staff morale in the Selected Entities, especially the service delivery entities, stemming from

 organizational improvements, increased entity responsiveness to user needs/priorities (especially to the needs of

 vulnerable groups) stemming from the introduction of updated CSCs, training in service delivery, performance

 monitoring, a unified NITA-provisioned Help Desk for Selected Entities, multiple channels for beneficiary and

 citizen feedback and complaints handling and outreach activities supported by the project. Better data security

 will ensure protection of service users’ information. In addition to direct economic benefits, the project will also

 positively affect access, quality and timeliness of delivery of the selected services for citizens and businesses. It

 will also contribute to improving citizens’ and firms’ actual experiences with, and perceptions about, the public

 service delivery system as a whole. This in turn could contribute to increasing levels of satisfaction of citizens and

 businesses with the selected services and Selected Entities.

 11.      PSRRP activities are also likely to have social effects such as increased accountability and transparency

 of functioning of Selected Entities at all levels, and improved protection of service users’ right through the updated

 CSCs, service standards, as well as the protection afforded by CHRAJ and entities’ own grievance redress and

 complaints handling systems.
