I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT



This Project Appraisal Document covers Phase two of the Kenya Green and Resilient Expansion of Energy (GREEN)

Multiphase Programmatic Approach (MPA) program approved by the World Bank’s Executive Directors on June 15,

2023 (P176698, Report No. PAD5130).



1.       Kenya Vision 2030 aims to transform Kenya into “a newly-industrializing, middle income country

providing a high quality of life to all its citizens in a clean and secure environment”. Addressing its infrastructure

deficit has been at the core of Kenya’s development strategy, and it has invested heavily in improving roads, rails,

ports networks, and energy infrastructure. Clean, adequate, affordable, and reliable energy is essential to meet the

goals of Vision 2030. Kenya has committed itself to 100 percent clean energy by 2030 (COP26 Global Summit).

Kenya’s 2020 Nationally Determined Contribution includes a commitment to achieve Vision 2030 through a low

carbon, climate resilient development pathway. It also has an ambitious target to reduce emissions by 32 percent

relative to the business-as-usual scenario of 143 mtCO2e by 2030.



2.       Government of Kenya (GoK)’s goal of universal access to clean, adequate, affordable, and reliable

electricity is critically dependent on a robust transmission system to connect the main generation sources

(geothermal, hydro and wind) in the central Rift valley and eastern part of the country to the major load centers

and to allow for greater connectivity to the regional hydropower resources. Particularly, the load centers in the

western and coastal regions are far from the supply sources (over 400km) and in the absence of adequate

transmission connectivity, local generation based on high-cost fossil fuel is used to supplement grid supply in these

areas. Replacement of the fossil fuel-based generation is a critical element to reducing the cost of supply in Kenya,

which is high in terms of regional standard impeding the competitiveness of Kenyan industries.1



3.       While Kenya has very low greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, it is highly vulnerable to the impacts of

climate change. Kenya accounts for only 0.1 percent of global emissions but is ranked 143 out of 188 countries in

terms of its vulnerability to climate change impact2 (a lower ranking means greater vulnerability). According to

climate projections, the increase in mean annual temperature in Kenya is projected to be between 1°C and 2°C by

the 2050s and by up to 5°C by 2100. Climate and disaster risk screening indicates that Kenya has a high risk of river,

urban and coastal floods, landslides, volcano eruptions, and wildfires, which will add further stress to Kenya's

climate vulnerability. An increase in the frequency and severity of extreme weather events would inflict a heavy

toll on human lives and welfare, with a high risk of damage to the country’s scarce and valuable human and natural

capital. The poorest, most marginalized, and most vulnerable households and communities will be hit the hardest,

as income and health shocks will drive them deeper into poverty. Infrastructure assets including electricity

transmission and distribution networks can be vulnerable to both chronic and acute climate hazards. According to

the World Bank's Lifelines report, the cost of disruption to the power sector due to natural shocks in Kenya was

about 1.14 percent of GDP in 20193.

4.       Kenya is already reaping benefits from its diversified and resilient generation mix and connectivity to

regional hydropower resources that has ensured security of supply in the face of the longest drought experienced

in the country in four decades and the goal of 100 percent clean energy is well within its reach. Installed

generation capacity as of June 2023 stood at 3,312 MW compared to peak demand of 2,170 MW, resulting in a

nameplate reserve margin of 34.5 percent. However, the effective reserve margin is lower due to plant outages,

and lower plant capacity factors from variable renewable energy (VRE) sources, mainly wind and solar. Despite the

reduction of the national hydro generation from 23 percent in 2022 to about 17 percent in 2023 (as of June 2023)

due to a severe drought, fossil fuel-based (thermal) generation did not increase to compensate for it.4 This was

possible due to renewable energy imports from Ethiopia following commissioning of the 500KV High Voltage Direct

Current (HVDC) Ethiopia-Kenya interconnector in November 2022. Under average hydrology, more than 90 percent

of total generation has previously been from renewable energy resources, mainly geothermal (45 percent), hydro

(26 percent), and wind and solar (19 percent). With geothermal costs at ~US¢8.5/kWh, hydro at ~US¢3-5/kWh

(fully depreciated), future competitive auctions for solar and wind expected in the range of US¢5-7/KWh

(depending on size, locations etc.), and with import of renewable energy from Ethiopia (at US¢6/kWh), Kenya has

a unique opportunity in the near future to meet its electricity needs almost entirely from clean energy sources and

at affordable costs.



5.       With the commissioning of the Kenya-Ethiopia interconnector, supported under the World Bank-

financed Eastern Electricity Highway Project (EEHP, P126579), impetus has been given to regional energy trade

through the Eastern Africa Power Pool (EAPP). As the first of its kind in Sub-Saharan Africa, this interconnection is

a flagship of power trade in the EAPP region and provides a key part of the infrastructure needed to connect the

EAPP to the Southern African Power Pool (SAPP). The EEHP is co-financed by the African Development Bank (AfDB)

and French Development Agency (AFD). While the interconnection has a transfer capacity of up to 2,000MW,

transmission system constraints in Kenya currently limit offtake to only 200MW. Urgent investments are needed

to address reactive power deficiencies and increase transfer capacity in the Kenya system to reap the full benefits

from the interconnection.

6.        Recognizing the potential benefits, governments in Eastern Africa5 have committed significant

investments in developing physical transmission infrastructure that will lay the foundations for power trade in

the region. The Uganda-Rwanda and Ethiopia-Kenya interconnectors are completed while five other key

transmission corridors are under construction (Kenya-Uganda, Rwanda- Burundi, Rwanda- Democratic Republic of

the Congo, Kenya-Tanzania, and Tanzania-Zambia). Together with other planned interconnectors, this will result in

all thirteen EAPP member countries being interconnected, allowing power flow from Egypt in the north to Tanzania

in the south. In the medium to long term, there are opportunities for trade with the SAPP through the Tanzania-

Zambia interconnector in the south. These interconnectors will allow the region to better harness and integrate

significant amount of renewable energy resources (hydro, geothermal, wind, and solar) by offering greater system

inertia and balancing support to absorb the unexpected variations in the output of VRE plants. The operation of

the regional interconnectors however needs a robust, secure and reliable transmission grid in Kenya.



7.      Kenya is gradually building an extensive national grid owned and operated by the transmission utility-

Kenya Electricity Transmission Company Limited (KETRACO) with a strategy for increased integration with the

regional network but will require significant investment to cater to the needs. The existing national transmission

grid network (operating at 400 kV, 220 kV, and 132 kV levels), is approximately 9,011 circuit-km, of which about 61

percent is owned by KETRACO, while the rest is owned and operated by Kenya Power & Lighting Company PLC

(KPLC), the single off-taker and distribution utility. KETRACO has been designated as the system operator and a

process is underway to transfer all transmission assets owned by KPLC to KETRACO. The Transmission Master Plan

(2023–2042) estimates that the transmission network will need to grow by a total of 2,199 km in route length

(3,281 km in circuit length) by 2027 and a further 4,311 km in route length (7,850 km in circuit length) by 2042,

essentially almost doubling the existing network. This will require an investment of about US$4.8 billion. The GoK

has so far been able to mobilize only about US$987 million (from various multilateral and bilateral financiers

including several export credit arrangements) leaving a financing gap of US$3.8 billion. Recognizing that the large

transmission investment needs cannot be mobilized from public resources alone, KETRACO has identified nine

transmission lines for a total of about 925km with an approximate cost of US$912 million for development through

Public Private Partnership (PPP) arrangements. KETRACO is also developing some regional interconnection projects

with Tanzania and Uganda to tap into regional hydropower resources, which will reduce the cost of power supply

in Kenya.



8.       Some priority investments in the transmission network are needed urgently to increase integration of

regional hydropower resources, strengthen capacity for transfer of renewable energy from generation sources

to consumption load centers, and improve voltage stability toward meeting Kenya’s goal of 100 percent clean

energy, reduction in the cost of supply, and improvement in supply quality and reliability. In July 2022, Kenya

and Ethiopia signed a Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) which projects that there would be an increase in Kenya’s

import to 400MW in the next three years (from the current 200MW). However, the increased import poses a

significant risk of instability and outages to the Kenya network in the absence of system reinforcement measures.

KETRACO commissioned a comprehensive study on the system needs for reactive power compensation devices

and voltage support. Completed in July 2022, the study established that the operation of the interconnector at

more than 200MW will require system reinforcements comprising of fast acting reactive power and voltage control

devices (STATCOMs) at key substations in Kenya (Suswa and Rabai initially and two more in Western Kenya at a

later stage) are needed. This equipment will stabilize system voltages and protect the system in case of outages in

9.       Constraints in transmission capacity and reactive power compensation in the electricity grid in Kenya are

also limiting transfer of renewable energy to the major load centers resulting in poor supply quality and

reliability and necessitating the use of high-cost fossil fuel-based generation. The main renewable generation

plants (geothermal, hydro, and wind) and the bulk substation of Suswa (the nerve center of the Kenya system that

collects over 50 percent of renewable energy in the country) are located in the central rift valley and eastern parts

of the country and are far from the major load centers. Currently, renewable energy from generation and collection

centers in the rift valley (about 70 percent of total energy generated in the country) is carried to the largest load

center of Nairobi through a 220kV Suswa-Nairobi line, which is overloaded. A second 400kV transmission line

(Suswa-Isinya) has recently been with financing from AFD and European Investment Bank (EIB), but the new line is

currently partly operated at 220kV due to absence of a substation close to Nairobi. Disturbances on the overloaded

Suswa-Nairobi 220kV line has often compromised supply security resulting in a number of national blackouts.

Construction of the proposed 400kV substation at Kimuka will enable the Suswa-Isinya line to be fully operated at

400kV, thus decongesting the 220kV Suswa-Nairobi line and enhancing the security of supply in Nairobi region. This

Suswa-Isinya line connects Kenya-Tanzania interconnector (currently under construction) with Ethiopia-Kenya

interconnector, which will allow for power export from Ethiopia to Tanzania through Kenya once the

interconnector with Tanzania and proposed substation at Kimuka are complete. In addition, the load centers of

western and coastal regions (second and third largest load centers in the country) are supplied via long transmission

lines (over 400km with inadequate reactive power supply) resulting in voltage instability and poor quality of supply

in these regions that have a large number of industries. Most of the existing reactive power compensation devices

in the system do not offer the fast-acting dynamic response needed to mitigate significant voltage fluctuations. As

a result, local generation based on high-cost fossil fuel has to be used to provide voltage support and supplement

grid supply in these areas. Installation of the STATCOMs will improve the voltage stability and allow for dispatch

of renewable energy to coastal region replacing the fossil fuel-based generation helping in achievement of the

country’s clean energy goals as well as reduction in the cost of supply and improvement in quality of supply

particularly for the industries in this region.



10.     Several other constraints in the electricity system need to be addressed for greater dispatch of

renewables. The system has high and inflexible must-run capacity compared to the considerably low off-peak

demand (about 56 percent of the peak demand) (Figure 2). This leads to frequent energy curtailment during the

low load period, including venting of substantial amount of geothermal steam (up to 170MW daily constituting 16

percent of installed capacity). A technical assessment on battery energy storage systems (BESS) supported by the

World Bank has identified that BESS could be effectively used to store geothermal based power during off-peak

period for discharge during peak period, which will allow for full utilization of geothermal energy while reducing

the use of costly fossil fuel-based generation for meeting peak load. The study has confirmed the immediate need

for 400 MW of 1-hour BESS capacity primarily for load shifting but also for grid stability by providing both fast and

back up system reserves. While implementing the first BESS at scale, adequate legal and regulatory framework

including their role in the system, ownership, contractual and remuneration arrangements will need to be

developed for leveraging private participation for meeting the future needs of BESS.



11.      Competitive procurement of future capacity addition of VRE informed by a robust least cost power

development plan will be critical for reducing the cost of supply and meeting the 100 percent clean energy goal.

A large number of projects in solar and wind were under various stages of development under the Feed-in-Tariff

(FiT) Policy, which was first introduced in 2010 to promote renewables. Such additions were based on an overly

II.       PROJECT DESCRIPTION



  A. Project Development Objective and indicators



18.     The project development objective of the second phase of the MPA is to increase the capacity of Kenya power

19.      The outcome indicators to measure the achievement of the PDO are:

      a) Increased capacity of the Kenya system to transfer power through the Kenya-Ethiopia interconnector; and

 B. Project Components

20.      As explained in the Program Appraisal document of the MPA (whose first phase was approved by the World

Bank Board of Executive Directors in June 2023), the second phase of the MPA will finance critical investments in grid

stability equipment (STATCOMs and BESS) and a substation to increase system resilience in Kenya, which will facilitate

increasing imports of cleaner and cheaper energy from Ethiopia, transfer of a larger share of renewable energy in the

Kenya system, and energy trade between Tanzania and Ethiopia through the Kenya system. The project scope is within

the overall scope of the MPA Program. The project will comprise the following components:



21.      Component A: Installation of System Stabilization Equipment (IDA US$84 million). This component will

finance installation of STATCOMs to improve the fault levels, increase voltage stability, and allow for greater import

of cheaper and renewable energy from Ethiopia. One of the STATCOMs will be installed at the existing Suswa

substation where the 500 HVDC Ethiopia-Kenya transmission interconnector terminates while the second STATCOM

will be installed at 220/132kV Rabai substation at the coast region of Kenya. Suswa substation is the nerve center of

the Kenya system with about 50 percent of electricity generated in the country being collected through the substation.

Installation of the STATCOMs at Suswa and Rabai will enable safe operation of the Ethiopia-Kenya interconnector and

will enable Kenya increase offtake to 400MW by November 2025 from the current 200MW as stipulated under the

PPA between KPLC and Ethiopia Electric Power. The STATCOM at Rabai will also provide reactive power compensation

reducing reliance on thermal power plants for energy and voltage support in the coast region. The STATCOMS will

comprise mechanically switched shunt busbar reactors, capacitor banks and static synchronous compensators and

associated substation primary equipment, secondary systems, associated civil works as recommended by technical

studies carried out in 2022 under EEHP. The technical designs and bidding documents for the STATCOMs have been

prepared by a consultant financed under EEHP and the bidding will be launched immediately after credit signing. The

component will also finance project management and supervision consultants. Given that STATCOMs are new

technologies in Kenya and KETRACO does not have experience in operation and maintenance (O&M) of these assets,

the component will finance O&M services including the first preventive maintenance. The supply and installation

contract will include specific requirement for knowledge transfer to allow KETRACO develop in-house capacity for

O&M within three years of commissioning of the STATCOMs.



22.      Component B: Construction of a 400kV Substation at Kimuka (IDA US$29 million). The Kimuka substation

will enable export of power from Ethiopia to Tanzania through Kenya by allowing the Suswa-Isinya line to be operated

at full design capacity. Although constructed at 400kV, only one circuit of the Suswa-Isinya line is currently being

C. Project Beneficiaries



27.      Households and industries in Kenya will benefit from higher import of firm renewable energy from Ethiopia

at a price (US¢6.5/kWh) that is significantly lower than the existing renewable energy sources (US¢7-12/kWh) and

thermal sources (US¢21/kWh). The combination of the cheaper energy from Ethiopian imports and displacement of

thermal energy facilitated by the project will result in reduction of overall generation cost, ultimately benefiting the

end-users through a reduction in tariff. An estimated 3.29 million households (10 million people) will benefit from the

improved quality of supply through reduction of voltage fluctuations, which is 37 percent of the total number of

households connected to the grid in Kenya. Industries at the coast will benefit immensely from the supply quality

improvement supported under the project.

III.     IMPLEMENTATION ARRANGEMENTS





 A. Institutional and Implementation Arrangements



32.      The project will be implemented by three implementing agencies - MoEP, KETRACO and KenGen. KETRACO

will implement Component A (installation of system stabilization equipment (STATCOMs), Component B (construction

of 400kV Kimuka substation) and subcomponent D4 (technical assistance to KETRACO). KenGen will implement

Component C (battery energy storage systems) and subcomponent D5 (technical assistance to KenGen). MoEP will

implement subcomponents D1 (solar auctions preparatory studies), D2 (transaction advisory for solar auctions) and

D3 (sector technical assistance, capacity building, and project management support).



33.      KETRACO, KenGen, and MoEP have experience in implementing World Bank-financed and other donor-funded

projects. Recent World Bank-financed projects include Kenya Electricity Expansion Project, EEHP, KEMP, and Kenya

Offgrid Solar Access Project (KOSAP, P160009). The capacity of the agencies in undertaking technical studies, managing

procurement contracts and E&S standards need strengthening. The agencies have been relying on qualified

international consulting firms to provide the needed services and expertise. Consultants supported KETRACO to

prepare technical studies, designs and bidding documents for the STATCOM and Kimuka substations. These

consultants will support KETRACO in bid invitation and evaluation and supervision of construction activities.

Consultants will be procured under the project for detailed feasibility study of the BESS (building on the recently

concluded technical assessment) and to support KenGen in the procurement process and implementation of BESS.

The STATCOMs and Kimuka substation will be installed on land that is already owned by KETRACO and KPLC. Three

parcels of land owned by KenGen/ KETRACO/KPLC have been assessed and recommended for location of BESS.

Therefore, implementation delays experienced in previous projects associated with challenges in land and right of way

acquisition are not expected in this project.

C. Sustainability



36.     The project is aligned with the GoK’s goal to achieve 100 percent clean energy by 2030. The project is also

aligned with the country’s LCPDP and GoK plan to lower electricity prices and improve supply quality to industries to

enhance the country’s energy security. Currently, the 200MW imports from Ethiopia, at a price comparable to

indigenous geothermal, have proven to be a critical element in lowering the cost of supply. Under the signed PPA

between Kenya and Ethiopia, Kenya is committed to increasing electricity offtake from the current 200MW to 400MW

by end of 2025. The infrastructure being supported under the Project will strengthen the capacity and resilience of

the transmission grid to increase the offtake of electricity imports from Ethiopia, facilitate the integration of more

renewable energy, and improve supply quality for industries at the coastal region. The project is therefore a priority

for the GoK.



37.      Several reform measures have been implemented recently and are planned under the first phase of the MPA

to improve the financial sustainability of the KPLC, that is critical for achievement of GoK’s clean energy and access

goals as well as for facilitating regional energy trade. The reforms have been implemented or committed as part of

the first phase of the MPA and a multi-sectoral series of World Bank-financed development policy operations. A

financially sustainable and operationally efficient KPLC will ensure that KPLC, as the single off-taker, is able to make

timely payments for electricity imports under the PPA and wheeling charges to KETRACO to enable sound maintenance

of the interconnector and facilities constructed under the project.

IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY

      A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis



39.     The activities to be supported under the Project have been informed by system studies carried under the

World Bank-financed EEHP (P126579). The studies, which included a power system analysis of the transmission

network (2021 to 2040), investigated the reactive power constraints on the Kenyan system and identified technical

solutions in the form of reactive power and voltage support devices to resolve the voltage and power quality

constraints and improve flexibility in voltage control to allow for increased import through the Ethiopia-Kenya

interconnector. The technical solution has taken into consideration the need to avoid a recurrence of voltage

regulation challenges in future as the system grows with an increasing share of VRE. The engineering designs and

bidding document for the technical solution for both the STATCOMS and the Kimuka substation have also been

supported under EEHP. The BESS component will be informed by a detailed feasibility study to be supported under

the project building on a technical assessment recently concluded with World Bank support.



Economic and Financial Analysis



40.      A traditional cost-benefit analysis has been carried out to assess the economic viability of the project. The

economic analysis has been conducted for each of the project components where benefits associated with the

component are considered as well as the costs. The analysis also takes into account GHG emission savings. The

project’s Economic Internal Rate of Return is estimated at 63 percent, with a Net Present Value of US$950 million. The

measured gains from Component A (STATCOMS) include displacement of thermal use in Nairobi and Mombasa and

baseload capacity requirements in the system met through import instead of new power plants. Component B (Kimuka

Substation) analysis focuses on the Kimuka 400kV substation to be implemented by KETRACO. Component C (BESS)

economic benefit is the saving of geothermal energy that would have otherwise been wasted. The BESS analysis is for

a period of 12 years, which is the estimated economic life of the BESS. In terms of the costs, the capital expenditure

for each component is taken as the allocation for each component over 2 years. O&M cost is assumed to be 2.5 percent

of the total capital expenditure for Components A and B. Similarly, BESS operation and maintenance (O&M) costs and

decommissioning costs have been derived from the BESS technical assessment.



41.     Since the project is being implemented mainly by KETRACO, a public sector entity, which is dependent on

government budget transfers for its capital expenditure and operating costs, financial analysis for the first two

components, STATCOMs and Kimuka Substation, has not been conducted. The BESS component is estimated to have

a financial return of 10.5 percent.

independent Accountability Mechanism (AM). The AM houses the Inspection Panel, which determines whether harm

Resolution Service, which provides communities and borrowers with the opportunity to address complaints through

dispute resolution. Complaints may be submitted to the AM at any time after concerns have been brought directly to

                                 ANNEX 2: Closing the Gender Gaps in Kenya Energy Sector



1. The Global Gender Gap Index 2023 states gender-based inequalities in Kenya in multiple dimensions – economic

participation and opportunities, education, health, and political empowerment. Kenya ranks 77th out of 146 countries

(57th in 2022) and the percentage of STEM graduates’ attainment is 30.7 percent and 19.4 percent in engineering

manufacturing and construction fields.14 On key indicators such as women economic participation and opportunities,

Kenya’s ranking decreased in 2023 to 16th out of 146 countries (in 2022 it was 6th).15 Such a decline underscores the

need for concerted efforts to facilitate and increase opportunities for women economic participation.



2. The GoK created an enabling legal framework in support of gender equality and women empowerment including

the Constitution of Kenya that enumerates several aspirations and national values: human rights, equality, freedom,

democracy, social justice, and the rule of law.16 Also, the National Policy on Gender and Development (2019) supports

interventions aimed at equitable participation of women, men, girls, and boys in the sustainable utilization of natural

resources for economic benefits including opportunities for carbon trading and provide guidance to mainstream

gender into environment and climate change laws, policies and programs. Similarly, the Gender Policy in Energy

(2019) provides a framework for mainstreaming gender in policies, programs and projects in energy, commits to

strengthening institutional frameworks for the employment of women in energy sector by engendering all energy

policies, plans, budgets, strategies and programs; increasing awareness on gender in the energy sector; integrating

gender in programs, and in monitoring and evaluation and promoting clean cooking solutions and environmental

sustainability. In addition, Kenya’s Employment Act 2012 set out non-discrimination in employment, promotion of

equal opportunity in employment and elimination of discrimination in any employment policy or practice.



3. Despite all these supportive national frameworks, evidence shows that women's economic and labor market

outcomes significantly lag Kenya’s strong regulatory and institutional framework: 30 percent wage earnings gap, 20

percent gap in wage employment gap, 27 percent gap in land/house ownership, respectively and 39 percent gender

gap in mobile internet use.17 The findings of gender gap assessments (GGAs) in two energy sector parastatal

companies shows that the workforce participation of women in Kenya energy sector is low especially in technical

fields and leadership/decision-making positions. The GGA report of KPLC (March 2023) shows that out of the total

staff of 9,527, only 2,171 (23 percent) are women and 7,356 (77 percent). Women in technical fields and leadership

positions are only 9 percent and 16 percent respectively. Similarly, the GGA report of KenGen (June 2023) identified

that KenGen has a total of 2,595 staff and 649 (25 percent) are female and 1,946 (75 percent) are male. Women in

technical fields are only 12 percent and while men dominate all higher levels of jobs under management, women are

placed in the lowest level of employment. Further the GGA identified key gender equality gaps such as absence of

gender responsible unit/department in KenGen, gender responsive HR system and performance evaluation

processes. Interview panels are male dominated and have a very limited and well-defined mentorship policy/

program; training plan/policy that are focused on specific goals such as leadership management skills are not

available. Also fragmented, siloed and competing gender related initiatives such as Pink Energy (women only), Blue

Energy (men only), and Y-Gen (males and females aged 35 and below) found to be one of the challenges. Inadequate

policy and institutional legal framework to prevent and respond to workplace sexual harassment and GBV and lack

of clear implementation plan is found to be another workplace challenge in the energy sector.
