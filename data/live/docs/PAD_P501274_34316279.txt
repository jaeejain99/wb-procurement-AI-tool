    I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT

    A. Country Context



1.      Bangladesh has achieved significant social and economic progress in recent decades though challenges remain.

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth has averaged 6.4 percent between 2010 and 2023. Extreme poverty 1 fell to 5.0

percent in 2022 from 9.0 percent in 2016. However, the pace of poverty reduction has slowed, with widening urban

inequality. Bangladesh faces a substantial balance of payments deficit and rising inflation amid post-pandemic global

economic challenges. The fiscal deficit has widened with rising subsidies and low tax revenues. Real GDP growth is

projected at 5.6 percent in fiscal year (FY) 24, but elevated inflation remains a concern. Structural reforms are essential to

sustain growth, focusing on domestic resource mobilization and overall fiscal policy, further tightening monetary policy

and tackling vulnerabilities in the financial sector. From 2016 to 2022, poverty incidence annually declined by 2.1

percentage points (US$ 3.65 poverty line) and 0.7 percentage points for extreme poverty (US$ 2.15). Currently 30 percent

of the population (51.3 million people) lives in poverty (US$ 3.65). Bangladesh is highly vulnerable to climate change,

ranking seventh in the Global Climate Risk Index.



2.      Bangladesh is currently hosting around one million Displaced Rohingya Population (DRP) 2 from Myanmar. 3

Most are residing in camps in Cox’s Bazar District of Chattogram Division, and 33,000 DRP on the island of Bhasan Char in

Noakhali District. Around 70 percent of the DRP inflow are women and children, and half are less than 15 years of age.

Given the extremely high population density in the camps, livability is a concern, including major air and water pollution. 4



3.       The DRP remain highly vulnerable despite international assistance, which is progressively shrinking. Financing

shortfalls for critical humanitarian services for the DRP and host communities managed by the GoB and the United Nations

(UN) partners have led to scaling back support to DRPs in the camps.



4.      There is no legislation specifically governing refugee and asylum affairs, and the legal status of the DRP in

Bangladesh presents some challenges. Despite not being party to the 1951 Refugee Convention and the Statelessness

Conventions, Bangladesh has ratified the main human rights instruments, and has specified that repatriation of Rohingya

must take place in a voluntary, safe and dignified manner. Government policy directives limit DRP movement outside the

camps, hampering access to formal education, wage-earning employment or trade, and Bangladeshi businesses are unable

to invest, operate, or hire Rohingyas in the camps.



5.        Since the initial DRP inflow in 2016, there has been some progress in policy positions regarding the DRP. The

2022 UN-GoB agreement on skills development has created formal mechanisms by which Rohingya are able to engage in

skills training and paid activities. Livelihood opportunities have gradually expanded to include broader service delivery

(including education), and approval of production centers to manufacture products for distribution within the DRP camps.

Aside from services, improvements have been made in accepting construction of durable infrastructure particularly for

public goods, e.g., some double-story education facilities, reusable material for shelters, and vehicular roads to improve

    B. Sectoral and Institutional Context



6.        The GoB has demonstrated commitment and support for the DRP as the humanitarian emergency has evolved

to a protracted crisis. In the past seven years, the GoB, with support from development partners including the World

Bank, have constructed quality infrastructure, including: disaster shelters, road networks, community gardens,

warehouses, waste management systems, solar photovoltaic (PV) standalone street lighting systems, solar PV water

pumping systems, and solar PV nano-grid systems. Efforts made by the GoB and Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH)

partners (including the World Bank) have led to improved access to WASH facilities (including functional sanitation

facilities) in some parts of Cox’s Bazar’s DRP camps and host communities. Socio-economic outcomes of DRP are poor but

improving over time. Host community outcomes are also improving over time, notably for education and employment.



7.      However, critical challenges persist for the host communities and DRP in Cox’s Bazar in fulfilling basic needs

due to the scale and length of the crisis. A significant fraction of the population still does not have adequate access to

basic WASH facilities. Around 96 precent of DRP and 54 percent of host community households have access to only

limited/unimproved sanitation facilities. Shared WASH facilities contribute to health, safety, and gender-based violence

(GBV) related issues. Half of DRP households do not have electricity. Camps are at higher risk of infectious diseases such

as diarrhea and acute respiratory infections. Several DRP camps in Cox’s Bazar lack fecal sludge and solid waste

management systems. Population growth continues to raise pressure on natural resources in Cox’s Bazar, highlighting the

need to improve waste management and to minimize environmental impact.



8.      The declining support from the international community has led to critical gaps in basic services and

infrastructure delivery. The annual 2023 Joint Response Plan (JRP) for the Rohingya Humanitarian Crisis 5 was only half

funded out of the US$876 million needed. Critical basic services of WASH and Site Management and Development, which

includes Disaster Risk Management (DRM), were only 43 percent and 15 percent funded, respectively. 6 Cross-sectoral

World Bank teams identified needs of host communities and DRP by location, as detailed below. 7



9.      The extended presence of the approximately one million DRP continues to put pressure on already poor areas,

affecting livelihoods, services, and social cohesion. Almost 80 percent of the host community population near Cox’s Bazar

camps believe the DRP have adversely affected safety levels in their neighborhood. The DRP outnumber the host

communities in Ukhiya and Teknaf 2 upazilas by a factor of three, and the DRP population has grown by around 20,000 in

just one year. Host communities are concerned about environmental degradation, damage to infrastructure and natural

resources, price fluctuation, and labor competition.



10.       To reduce the stress on Cox’s Bazar, the GoB has facilitated the voluntary relocation of more than 30,000 DRP

to Bhasan Char since 2021. Bhasan Char, an island located in the Bay of Bengal in the Noakhali District, emerged in 2006

as an island formed of Himalayan silt and sediments. 8 The island is primarily inhabited by the DRP. The host communities

living near Bhasan Char are in the Sandwip and Hatiya islands in the Chattogram and Noakhali Districts, around 6 and 30

km away. The mainland coast in Chattogram is approximately 30km away and interaction with the DRP is limited. The GoB

facilities supported by a 1.8MWp centralized and standalone solar PV based power system. In October 2021, the GoB and

and skills/vocational trainings are more open in Bhasan Char compared to Cox’s Bazar, livelihood opportunities are

limited. 9 Further support from the international community is necessary to strengthen basic infrastructure (e.g., WASH,

transportation, energy and lighting, shelters), especially its operations and maintenance (O&M).



11.      Both Cox’s Bazar and Bhasan Char are highly vulnerable to climate and disaster risks. In Cox’s Bazar, cyclones,

flash floods, storm surges, landslides, and fires are identified as disasters that are very likely to happen. 10 Under a relatively

modest climate scenario (the SSP3-7.0 scenario from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change), a five-year event

of largest five-day cumulative precipitation (which is a significant driver of flood risks) would happen 1.24 times more

frequently by 2050. Multiple disasters have impacted Cox’s Bazar. In 2023, a massive fire in Camp 11 in March affected

nearly 16,000 DRP and fully damaged 2,664 shelters. Cyclone Mocha in May 2023 further affected all 33 camps and Host

Communities through heavy rains, strong winds, and subsequent landslides. More than 400,000 Bangladeshi nationals

and around 930,000 DRP were impacted, 11 with nearly half of DRP reporting damaged or destroyed shelters. In the Host

Communities, 37 waterpoints were completely destroyed while 516 tube-wells and 707 latrines experienced partial

damage. The extreme monsoon rains in August 2023 further triggered flash floods, submerging much of Cox’s Bazar.

Around 75 kilometers of roads throughout Cox’s Bazar were identified as potentially affected by the floods. 12 Owing to its

location, Bhasan Char is highly exposed to storm surges.



12.      Vulnerabilities for women and girls are further exacerbated during disasters due to the aforementioned gaps

in basic services, especially in DRP camps. Lack of infrastructure, such as WASH facilities with functions for adequate

menstrual hygiene management as well as limited access to sanitary napkins, lead to significant health and safety risks

such as reproductive issues and urinary tract infections. Menstrual hygiene is poor: 51 percent of adolescent Rohingya

girls use old cloth, 11 percent wear underwear without disposable pads, and only 26 percent washed their hands before

and after changing their pads. Only 12 percent have a primary understanding of menstrual hygiene. The absence of

adequate sanitation facilities has repercussions on health, economic prospects, and educational outcomes.



13.      Furthermore, gender gaps in accessing information on disaster preparedness efforts and early warning systems

persist. One of the key drivers of this gap is that the majority of community mobilizers in DRM are men. A gender-sensitive

approach, where women facilitators can effectively engage and communicate with women and girls to enhance their

understanding on disaster preparedness, is critical. Women are also underrepresented in leadership positions for disaster

management, leading to barriers in mobility and accessing productive assets and finances, especially in the recovery

phases.



14.      DRP camps in Cox’s Bazar: Needs for basic infrastructure and their maintenance are high, especially for WASH

47.      EMCRP and its additional financing provided key lessons which HELP has integrated. First, ensuring benefits for

both Host Communities and DRP is essential to mitigate social tension that is on the rise due to the protracted nature of

the crisis. Second, a framework approach for the detailed selection of investments is effective, as it allows for flexibility

and adaptability in the dynamic environment with multiple stakeholders and development partners. Third, engagement

with development partners and UN agencies at both policy and technical levels is critical to implement activities in complex

and evolving fragile environments. Strong coordination with government agencies and development partners at both

strategic and operational levels will be pivotal to prevent duplication of activities and maximize benefits of interventions.

Finally, spatially targeted investments through a multi-sectoral approach with clear coordination and communication

processes across agencies are necessary. The project will consider the differential spatial and demographic vulnerabilities

of the DRP and Host Communities, such as households with persons with disabilities and female-headed households.



48.     The project leverages key recommendations from the 2023 World Development Report and 2022 Cox’s Bazar

Inclusive Growth Diagnostics. The number of disasters affecting camps of displaced populations is on the rise globally.

Many displaced groups are concentrated in disaster-prone areas and climate change hotspots, which are not considered

suitable for human settlements. As suggested in the Inclusive Growth Diagnostics report, HELP and ISO projects will

together focus on early investment in productive potentials (such as improvements in basic infrastructure), while

strengthening human and productive capacity and expanding the economic ‘pie’ opportunities.



49.      Lessons have been integrated from Bank projects across sectors and regions. The Refugees and Host

Communities Support Project (P164748) and Chad Territorial Development and Resilience Project (P177163) in Chad,

Integrated Water Management and Development Project (P163782) in Uganda, and the Development Response to

Displacement Impacts Project in the Horn of Africa Phase II (P178047) highlight the crucial importance of investing in

climate-resilient, multi-sectoral infrastructure, in protracted displacement crisis, while also providing technical assistance

and skills training for sustainable operations.



50.     Learning from good practice in rural Bangladesh, build-operate models will be applied for the WASH

interventions in Host Community areas to attract and sustain private sector participation. The activities will align with

the institutional policies and frameworks for WASH in Bangladesh and strengthen service delivery mechanisms for

sustainability. For Host Communities, water tariffs, including metering, will be introduced. This will contribute to improved

water consumption during water scarce drought periods and ensure cost recovery for the piped water schemes’ financial

sustainability. DPHE will engage contractors to build and operate water schemes for the project duration through a

performance bond, issued jointly by the Union Parishad (local government) and DPHE. After five years, the local

governments and water user committees will be responsible for O&M, including the appointment of a private operator.

The role of the GoB, especially, DPHE, as a backstopping agency to support local service providers, will be explored in O&M

planning. In the DRP camps, O&M will be done through the project budget during the implementation period. Water

management committees will be formed from user groups and trained on regular O&M. The committees will be linked to

relevant government authorities, NGOs, and UN agencies operating in the camp areas to address longer term

sustainability.

53.     The proposed project will strengthen sustainable design features and materials at the implementation level,

to provide the DRP with a dignified, purposeful, and productive life in the medium-term. Thus, the Bank will continue its

engagement with the GoB in coordination with development partners to seek ways forward at the policy level on

sustainability.

55.      The operation is aligned with the goals of the Paris Agreement on both adaptation and mitigation.

 Adaptation Risk Assessment and Reduction: The project has been screened for climate change impacts and disasters. The

overall exposure to extreme precipitation, flood, storm surge, lightning, and landslides is considered high in the project

locations. Given that climate change may increase future risks posed by such hazards, the project took into consideration

climate-resilient design features to improve access to basic services as well as increase the adaptive capacity of the Host

Community and DRP. Specifically, the project will establish climate-resilient water supply and sanitation systems and all-

weather roads; and provide training to first responding engineering agencies on climate and DRM strategies. Overall, the

project will equip agencies with adaptive measures and enhance the capacity of GoB to plan and coordinate response

during current and future disasters. These measures will reduce material physical climate risks to an acceptable level.

Mitigation Risk Assessment and Reduction: The project is not at a material risk of hindering the country’s transition to a

low-GHG emissions pathway. For example, the project will support expansion and rehabilitation of resilient water supply

powered by solar PV pumping systems, integrated solid waste management, rehabilitation of landing stages for inland

water transport, an afforestation program, and strengthening institutional system activities under Component 3, which

are Universally Aligned as per the MDB List of UAL activities (among other activities). Furthermore, the project will support

the activities with low mitigation risk; for example: renewable energy interventions with hybrid energy generation from

solar power and a BESS backed up by fossil fuel; and rehabilitation of rural roads with minor enhancement of capacity for

all weather accessibility to rural community 34. Though the remaining activities of components 1, and 2 are not in the MDB

UAL, there are currently no alternative means to achieve the Development Objectives with lower GHG emissions. These

activities are expected to lower GHG emissions and support Bangladesh’s efforts to reduce its carbon footprint using

nature-based solutions in construction process.



Economic analysis

56.     The economic analysis has been carried out on a cost-benefit framework, a methodology that compares

scenarios ‘with’ and ‘without’ the project. The analysis considered capital investments in and anticipated benefits from

WASH facilities, disaster-resilient infrastructure, improved connectivity through roads, bridges/landing stages. The

benefits encompassed public health benefits associated with reduced incidents of waterborne diseases, time saving due

to on-premises water supply, human lives saved by shelter during disasters, time saved due to improved connectivity in a

with-project scenario. Additionally, the shelter will serve as a year-round learning center; however, the economic

advantages of such centers were not factored into the analysis. Some other benefits were challenging to quantify; for

instance, improved WASH facilities could decrease stunting among children, enhanced connectivity could stimulate the

local economy and offer better healthcare access—these aspects were not accounted for. Benefits like improved living

standards, well-being, environmental quality are intangible and difficult to measure and thus not included in the analysis.

 75.     Grievance Redress. Communities and individuals who believe that they are adversely affected by a project

 supported by the World Bank may submit complaints to existing project-level grievance mechanisms or the Bank’s

 Grievance Redress Service (GRS). The GRS ensures that complaints received are promptly reviewed in order to address

 project-related concerns. Project affected communities and individuals may submit their complaint to the Bank’s

 independent Accountability Mechanism (AM). The AM houses the Inspection Panel, which determines whether harm

 occurred, or could occur, as a result of Bank non-compliance with its policies and procedures, and the Dispute

 Resolution Service, which provides communities and borrowers with the opportunity to address complaints through

 dispute resolution. Complaints may be submitted to the AM at any time after concerns have been brought directly to

 the attention of Bank Management and after Management has been given an opportunity to respond. For information

 on how to submit complaints to the Bank’s GRS, visit http://www.worldbank.org/GRS. For information on how to

 submit complaints to the Bank’s Accountability Mechanism, visit https://accountability.worldbank.org.



 76.      A project specific GRM will be established and implemented under the following operating principles: i) all

 cases received should be recorded; ii) resolutions must be communicated to the complainant; iii) all cases will be

 monitored through its completion; and iv) accessibility to people with disabilities, children, and other vulnerable groups.

 A Camp-level GRC will be set-up for each camp and will be responsible for reviewing each case and providing a

 resolution. In accordance with the resolution, the grievance cases will be referred to the service provider or relevant

 agency for the implementation of corrective measures. If grievances cannot be resolved by the camp-level GRC, these

 will be raised to the Project Director (who must also inform the Bank). Should any grievance be complex enough that it

 cannot be resolved through the Project GRM (for camps) it will be remitted to the RRRC’s office for resolution. In the

 host community, local level GRCs members will be identified for all relevant upazilas who will convene the committees

 when and where grievances are reported. Grievances can be received through site managers, complaint drop-boxes at

 all construction sites, and additionally for host communities at union parishad office, upazila level office of the

 respective IAs. If UN agencies are engaged in the camps, their GRMs can also be used. All relevant postal and email

 addresses and phone numbers (calls, short message service, online message, voice recording, picture), location of local

 offices of IAs will be published in GRM leaflets and distributed widely at all public facilities and community gathering

 points, other communication channels detailed in the Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP) in local languages. The

 addresses and phone numbers provided to the communities will also be used to deliver feedback to the GRCs regarding

 the responsiveness of the GRM and the experience of the complainants which will be duly recorded (see SEP). A set of

 grievance categories will be produced to prioritize cases as well as facilitate their redressal. Any SEA/SH or GBV related

 complaint associated with the Project received at any level will be directly reported to the Gender and GBV Specialist

 of the PIU maintaining appropriate protocols and confidentiality, and the cases will be dealt with in accordance with

 the Gender and SEA/SH Action Plan.

supervision, and incentives to enhance the skills, systems, and performance of the project managers and staff, and (iii)

leveraging successful practices under the ongoing EMCRP.



80.     Sector policies and strategies risk is Substantial. Although the operating environment is adequate, risks remain

such as the overcrowding of the hosting area, limited access to services and livelihoods, secondary movement,

communication, and GBV, among others. The Bank will continue to closely monitor the situation and actively engage in

dialogue with the GoB and partners on sector policies and strategies.



81.     Stakeholder risk is Substantial due to the extensive coordination required within and across multiple

humanitarian and developmental projects and actors at both policy and implementation levels (see Annex 1 for overall

coordination mechanisms). There are also risks related to the participation, involvement, and support of the beneficiaries,

communities, civil society, and other relevant actors. The coordination risk will be mitigated by supporting coordination

functions of the GoB (through the RRRC and the National Task Force) and leveraging aforementioned coordination

mechanisms. The participation and support risks will be mitigated by promoting stakeholder engagement and

communication throughout the project cycle and providing clear and timely information and feedback mechanisms. The

project will take a proactive approach to information sharing and communication with relevant stakeholders through the

SEP.



82.      Fiduciary risk is Substantial due to the many agencies involved, the number and modality of the interventions,

and the intricacy of activity selection. Mitigation measures include hiring additional capacity, training, streamlining the

procurement and disbursement processes, and monitoring the financial flows and expenditures. While several risk-

mitigating measures have been incorporated into the design of the FM implementation arrangements, a number of

additional measures will also be taken to improve overall FM capacity and performance. Such measures include (i) hands-

on-implementation support for procurement and FM management; (ii) utilizing integrated budget systems, maintaining

real-time accounting entries, separating ledger codes for each source of financing, and preparing quarterly interim un-

audited financial reports to better monitor expenditures; (iii) quarterly progress monitoring reports, providing the status

of the technical preparation and procurement process of each procurement activity against the approved procurement

plan, identifying the bottlenecks, and recommending corrective measures; and (iv) undertaking an annual risk-based

internal audit by an independent audit firm. Residual risks will be managed through proactive and continuous supervision

and close dialogue with the GoB.



83.      Environment and social risks are Substantial, owing to the nature and locations of the operations and the

activities, some of which are located in areas exposed to disaster risks. Please refer to Section IV.D. Project activities will

be screened against an exclusion list, elaborated in the Annex to the ESMF and referred in the ESCP, to screen and avoid

interventions with significant E&S risks. The implementing agencies have experience in project implementation under ESF

and safeguards. The comprehensive environmental impact assessment will support further understanding and design of

mitigation measures for E&S risks. A climate and disaster risk assessment is being conducted to understand the exposure

of project areas to environmental risks. The fragile context, rural location, mix of local and outside labor inflow, and the

proximity of project locations to schools or areas where women and young girls have increased mobility, pose risks for

SEA/SH. To mitigate such risks, a GBV/Gender specialist within each IA will be assigned. A robust SEA/SH responsive GRM

will be established for each IA through which cases will be referred to mapped GBV service provider. A code of conduct

will further be developed and signed by workers and an SEA/SH Action Plan will be developed and implemented.



84.      Other: Refugee protection risk is Substantial. The DRP are heavily concentrated in 33 camps in Cox’s Bazar and

in Bhasan Char with restricted mobility and limited opportunities for livelihoods, which the project will enhance. With the

decline in humanitarian assistance, economic conditions within the camps are depressed, and there has been a noticeable

deterioration of the security situation. In the event of new and additional DRP inflows into Bangladesh, there could be

coordination issues in Bangladesh that inadvertently stress the adequacy of the protection framework, such as the report

of an incident of refoulement in April 2024. 39 The World Bank is engaged with UNHCR on an on-going basis as they monitor

the situation. It is not indicative of a de facto or de jure changes in the GoB policy commitments to provide the DRP with

international protection, and hence, does not affect the adequacy of the refugee protection framework at this time The

mitigation measures for refugee protection include the World Bank’s continued engagement with relevant GoB authorities

(including the RRRC), continued coordination with UNHCR (and other relevant UN agencies), the promotion and expansion

of GRM mechanisms, and increasing the number of touch points of contact for the DRP for the prevention and responses

to protection issues. In the event that the country no longer has an adequate refugee protection framework, as assessed

by UNHCR, remedial actions include suspension of financing, as noted in Article IV of the Financing Agreement.

                                   ANNEX 1: Implementation Arrangements and Support Plan







                                           COUNTRY: People's Republic of Bangladesh

                                         Host and Rohingya Enhancement of Lives Project



Implementation Arragements



1.     LGED, DPHE, RHD, BPDB will implement the project through four PIUs and ERD, as coordination agency, will have

the CCU.



2.    Following the Government’s Rules of Business, the PIUs will report to their respective Ministerial Project Steering

Committee (PSC), chaired by the respective Sr. Secretary/Secretary. LGED and DPHE will be chaired by one PSC under Local

Government Division, MoLGRDC. The PSC for RHD will be chaired under the Road Transport and Highways Division,

MoRTB. The PSC for BPDP will be chaired under the Power Division, MoPEMR.



3.      The PSC will include representatives from ministries, divisions, departments/agencies that are part of overall

implementation, coordination and strategy. The PSC will be responsible for: (i) providing implementation advice and

operational guidance; (ii) reviewing financial and physical progress; (iii) resolving implementation problems; and (iv)

providing any other necessary direction for effective implementation. The PSC will meet at least every six months.



4.       Overall, the project will use existing arrangements of the GoB and all implementation on ground will be

coordinated through the existing government mechanism (Figure A1). The existing Development Partner/Multi-Lateral/Bi-

Lateral/UN Agencies coordination mechanism in the field regarding the Rohingya inflow will be through the Inter Sector

Coordination Group (ISCG) and coordinated in Dhaka by the Strategic Executive Group (SEG). There will be inter-agency

field level coordination on project activities with the RRRC, the ISCG, and the implementing agencies.



5.       Policy coordination at the national level will be by the National Task Force, which is a Cabinet approved inter-

ministerial body with secretarial services provided by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. National level coordination will be

through MoDMR and field level coordination through the RRRC. This is in-line with the overall coordination and

communication arrangements in the country regarding the Rohingya crisis. The Refugee Cell and its field level team

represented by Camp-in-Charges/Refugee, RRRC will also be supported by qualified service provider(s), including

specialized agencies, to coordinate and administer day-to-day activities. Following the existing government mechanism,

for the Host Community, district level coordination will be by the Deputy Commissioner and upazila level coordination will

be by the Upazila Nirbahi Officer. ERD will carry out its functions and responsibilities as CCU during implementation as

with the rest of the portfolio of projects and as per the rules of business.

6.       Each project implementation agency will have a Project Implementation Committee (PIC), chaired by the head of

agency (Chief Engineers of LGED, DPHE, RHD and Chairperson of BPDB), which will assist in the supervision of the

respective components. The PIC is expected to include relevant representatives from ministries, division,

departments/agencies. The PIC will ensure that implementation follows both Government and Bank rules and regulations.

Specifically, the PIC will be responsible for: (i) supervising and reviewing implementation and providing necessary advice

for timely delivery; (ii) monitoring and evaluating implementation progress and suggesting necessary course corrections;

(iii) resolving issues and bottlenecks that may emerge during implementation; (iv) facilitation coordination and

convergence with other line ministries, division, and departments/agencies; and (v) keeping the PSC apprised on overall

performance and key issues relating to the project.



7.      At the project level, considering the multiple implementing agencies, LGED will be responsible for having a

coordination role. To streamline monitoring efforts across multiple implementing agencies, LGED will be responsible for

reporting the M&E of activities from all the implementing agencies. LGED will coordinate with the implementing agencies

and prepare monitoring reports as per the project components and will share with the World Bank quarterly.



8.      DPHE will be the implementing agency of Component 1 and parts of Component 3 related to WASH. DPHE PIU will

have a dedicated Project Director.



9.       LGED will be the implementing agency of Subcomponent 2.1 and parts of Component 3 related to basic services

and infrastructure. LGED PIU will have a dedicated Project Director. BFD will sign an MoU with LGED as grants to undertake

activities on afforestation (part of Subcomponent 2.1). DoE will sign an MoU with LGED as grants to undertake an

environmental impact study (part of Subcomponent 3.1). The flow of funds and implementing agency for these activities

will be LGED. The MoU modality with BFD, DoE, will be streamlined and flexible, ensuring that full responsibility for

14.      Procurement risks include involvement of multiple implementing agencies, possible delays in procurement due to

the lack of technical preparation (such as Terms of References, technical specifications, and cost estimates), inordinate

delays in bid evaluation and contract award, slow decision making and approval processes within the Government. The

risks will be mitigated through capacity building and technical assistance, combined with the implementing agencies’

experience with large and complex projects funded by the World Bank, GoB and other development partners. Other risk

mitigation measures will include: (i) assigning full time qualified procurement official in each PIU; (ii) hiring of qualified

procurement consultants on a need-based approach; (iii) hiring design and supervision consultant for technical

preparation of procurement documents and supervision of contract implementation; (iv) using the government’s

electronic procurement system for all procurements, where applicable; (vi) Preparing quarterly procurement progress

monitoring reports by each IA and sharing it with the World Bank, providing the status of the technical preparation and

procurement process of each procurement activity against the approved procurement plan, identifying the bottlenecks,

and recommending corrective measures; (vii) Providing weekly updates during bid evaluation (8 weeks after bid opening)

and during contract award (4 weeks after BER approval) and (viii) providing comprehensive fiduciary training to relevant

DPHE, LGED, RHD, and BPDB officials.

25.     Key criteria in the selection and appraisal of selected investments are as follows. The investments will improve
