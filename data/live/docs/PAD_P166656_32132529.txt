       I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT



       A. Country Context



   1.      With its 97 million people and over US$2,500 per capita gross domestic product (GDP) (2018),

   Vietnam is globally recognized for its transformational socio-economic progress since the introduction

   of the Doi Moi reforms in the late 1980s. Vietnam’s per capita GDP grew at an average annual rate of

   about 5.5 percent between 1990 and 2018, making it one of the fastest-growing economies in the world

   during the period. The proportion of the population living below the national poverty line fell below 10

   percent in 2016, down from close to 60 percent in 1993. In terms of shared prosperity, the growth in per

   capita consumption for the bottom 40 percent of the population was about 6 percent annually, compared

   to 4 percent for the total population during the 2010-2016 period. On human development, Vietnam has

   experienced substantial improvements in key health and education outcomes: today’s population is far

   healthier and better educated than 30 years ago.



   2.      As Vietnam aspires to become an upper middle-income country by 2035, its productivity needs

   to increase continuously, which requires greater production and effective use of high-skilled manpower

   and science, technology and innovation (STI) 1. Global and national megatrends are posing challenges to

   Vietnam’s development aspiration but also provide the opportunity for the country to use its higher

   education system as a platform to transform the quality of the skilled workforce and the relevance of

   research and technology transfer. First, rapid technological advances, technology-driven automation, and

   platform marketplaces mean that the demand for routine tasks, mostly in low-skilled jobs, will likely fall

   while non-routine tasks requiring advanced cognitive, digital, and socio-emotional skills will grow (World

   Bank’s 2019 World Development Report on “Changing Nature of Work”). Second, the rise of the

   knowledge economy and global integration will increase the demand for competencies and skills required

   for high-value jobs in the global value-chain of manufacturing, services, and research and development

   (R&D). Third, demographic shifts such as the rise of the middle class, increased urbanization and aging

   population will lead to higher education aspirations of Vietnamese students and their families (World

   Bank, 2018 “Vietnam Future Jobs”). Fourth, climate change-related risks that could hamper socio-

   economic development call for adaptation and mitigation measures which require greater use of

   knowledge and research. Finally, the ongoing COVID-19 outbreak has caused severe disruptions and

   resulted in social and economic impacts to a wide range of sectors, including extended closures of

   educational institutions. Vietnam’s higher education system and its universities have the opportunity to

   be at the forefront in responding to the current crisis by (i) leveraging digital technology to deliver distance

   learning, (ii) enhancing equity for a more inclusive higher education sector, and (iii) collaborating with

   government and industry to find innovative solutions to the public health response, economic recovery,

   and educational sustainability.



   B. Sectoral and Institutional Context



   3.      There is a disconnect between Vietnam’s remarkable achievement on equitable economic

   growth and human development, on the one hand, and the performance of the higher education

   system, on the other hand (Vietnam Higher Education Policy Note, 2019). Vietnam ranks 48 out of 157

   countries on the World Bank’s human capital index (HCI), the highest among middle-income countries. A

   Vietnamese child born today will be 67 percent as productive when she grows up as she could be if she

   enjoyed complete general education and full health. Of the three sub-components in the HCI, Vietnam

   comes out especially strong with respect to access and quality of general education. Vietnam’s average

   years of schooling, adjusted for learning, is 10.2 years, second only to Singapore among ASEAN countries.

   However, Vietnam’s higher education system is not ready to capitalize on this huge potential of young

   people coming out of general education, for three main reasons. One, Vietnam’s access to higher

   education, as measured by gross enrolment rate in higher education, is below 30 percent, one of the

   lowest among the East Asian countries. This is too low for a country where only 11 percent of workforce

   has a post-secondary education degree. Two, Vietnam was ranked in the bottom third of the 140 countries

   listed in the 2018 Competitiveness Index on skills-relevance of university graduates 2 as reported by

   employers in respective countries (Global Competitiveness Report, 2018). Three, the level of research and

   technology transfer is low compared to regional peers such as the Philippines, Indonesia, Thailand,

   Malaysia, and China. The number of citable documents per 1 million inhabitants of Vietnam is higher than

   the Philippines, but lower than Indonesia and much lower than Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand. When

   measured by H-index, that captures both the quality and impact of research output, Vietnam ranks below

   all these countries. On technology transfer, Vietnam has a low output at 1.24 patents per million of

   population, lower than the Philippines (1.35), Thailand (3.16), less than 10 percent of Malaysia (30) and

   less than 1 percent of China (233). For a country with ambition to become a knowledge-based economy,

   it is indispensable to further improve the quality and relevance of and access to higher education.



   4.      At the system level, the Government of Vietnam (GoV) has pursued several measures to improve

   performance, including passing an amended Higher Education (HE) Law in 2018, formulating a HE Master

   Plan 2021-2030 3, and initiating a subsector-wide Program on HE quality improvement in 2019. At the

   university level, the GoV is committed to prioritizing capital investment in a few key universities, including

   those financed by the World Bank under the Support for Autonomous Higher Education Project (SAHEP),

   the New Model University Project (NMUP), and the proposed Vietnam University Development of VNU-

   Hanoi, VNU-HCM, and UD project (VUDP). The main challenges faced by key universities are described

   below.



   5.       University infrastructure – consisting of university campuses, buildings and associated facilities,

   information and communication technology (ICT) infrastructure, etc. - is critical to achieving a university’s

   mandate to produce high-skilled graduates, generate cutting-edge research, and engage closely with the

   community and the region. Vietnam universities, catering to a large student population and located in

   large metropolitan centers such as Hanoi City, Ho Chi Minh City, Da Nang City, are facing increasing

   pressure to move away from “crowded” locations to suburb areas. There is an expectation from the key

   stakeholders (the government, the cities, students, parents, staff) that any newly built or upgraded

   infrastructure will be modern (modern amenities and cutting-edge facilities and equipment); integrated

   (shared and interconnected facilities); smart (where appliance systems and ICT infrastructure “talk to each

   6.         Digital technology: Vietnamese universities, in general, lack robust foundational infrastructure

   and strategic enabling pillars – good governance and effective and sufficient financing - necessary to

   establish and take advantage of the digital and disruptive technologies to improve the quality of teaching,

   learning, and research. Existing ICT infrastructure (networks, databases, equipment) are siloed, often

   inefficient and incompatible across member institutions. A centralized hosting infrastructure is missing,

   connectivity networks have relatively low bandwidth, and facilities necessary for e-learning are limited.

   Smart classrooms/computer labs are not fully standardized. There are no High-Performance Computing

   (HPC) facilities to support advanced research, and surprisingly many universities are not connected to

   Vietnam Research and Education Network (VinaRen), the National Research and Education Network

   (NREN), thus denying researchers access to a global research network.



   7.       University governance: In terms of institutional governance, Vietnam has been moving in the

   right direction by introducing autonomy reforms, but several challenges remain: (i) under-developed

   internal quality assurance mechanisms, and the capacity to evaluate institutional performance (e.g.,

   through systematic and rigorous student and employer engagement surveys, research output, and

   technology transfer results); (ii) an inadequate talent management system which impedes universities’

   ability to bring in and retain the best university administrators, top faculty, and researchers (from the

   Vietnamese diaspora and foreign universities), offer fast-track tenure for promising young researchers

   and excellent teachers, and attract high-quality students for PhD programs and international students at

   all levels; (iii) national and regional universities that are organized as umbrella universities with a loose

   network of member universities are not yet effectively sharing the financial and scientific resources

   dispersed across separate member institutions; and (iv) under-developed institutional accountability

   mechanisms, accompanied by fragmented management and information systems on university finance,

   academic affairs, human resources and physical infrastructure, and by the absence of an integrated data

   system.



   8.       Engagement with the private sector: The low level of linkages with employers inhibits

   Vietnamese universities from providing curricula relevant to the labor market needs and renovating

   pedagogical methods, including through internships and other work-based learning arrangements.

   Research collaboration and technology transfer with the industry is also under-developed, partly

   reflecting the country’s less than vibrant innovation ecosystem.



   9.      Gender: While overall student enrolments in Vietnam universities are quite equitable (54 percent

   are female), women are significantly under-represented in Science, Technology, Engineering and

   Mathematics (STEM) fields (less than 30 percent of total students), research outputs (1 in 3 authors of

   publications on Web of Science journals are female), and leadership positions (fewer than 10 percent of

   senior faculty and university leaders are female).

   11.      COVID-19. The emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic at the final stages of project preparation

   has underlined the relevance of this project to Vietnam. Despite strong containment efforts, GDP growth

   for the first quarter of 2020 was the slowest since the corresponding figure for the first quarter of 2009.

   Tourism, trade and transport sectors have been hardest hit, with the loss of jobs and livelihoods rapidly

   increasing. All levels of the education system experienced considerable disruptions during the 13 weeks

   when schools were closed, causing significant negative impact on learning and skills acquisition. The pause

   in schooling puts vulnerable students at risk of dropping out and never returning to schooling and puts a

   strain on the financial status of educational institutions and their staff. Universities are no exception. Most

   campuses were closed for 13 weeks, with some universities offering remote learning during that period.

   However, there were quality and equity concerns because of inadequate digital infrastructure, learning

   management systems, and course adaptation and assessment mechanisms. The economic downturn,

   particularly if a new wave of COVID-19 hits Vietnam, will mean households will have more difficulty

   affording university tuition fees, requiring institutional management to be proactive and dynamic, and

   with the likely consolidation of institutions to promote sustainability. To build more resilience in the higher

   education system, the proposed operation has a strong focus on distance learning through suitable

   digital/disruptive technologies. Institutional reforms on adaptability and sustainability are also important

   elements in this project. Finally, the project will invest in priority areas of industry-university collaboration

   and research which will contribute to finding innovative solutions to not only the public health response

   to the COVID-19 outbreak but also on jump starting the economic recovery more quickly in Vietnam.

    II. PROJECT DESCRIPTION



   Selection of Universities



   16.     The proposed VUDP will support three national universities, which were selected based on

   several considerations. The first reason is the country’s ambitions to develop research-intensive

   universities that can hold their place in regional and international research networks. The three

   universities are: Vietnam National University-Hanoi (VNU-HN), Vietnam National University-Ho Chi Minh

   City (VNU-HCM), and the University of Danang (UD). VNU-HN and VNU-HCM are the nation’s leading

   research universities and UD is the top research institution in the central part of the country. Furthermore,

   because they are structured as umbrella universities with several member institutions representing a wide

   range of academic disciplines, these three universities have a huge potential for the development of the

   kind of multidisciplinary research and training programs that are needed to address national, regional,

   and global challenges in innovation and in skills requirements. Importantly, these three universities

   provide a good opportunity for the government to pilot the implementation of key reforms on education,

   research, and governance as identified in the recently amended HE Law and the upcoming HE Master Plan

   (2021-2030).



   17.     Each of the three universities is located in one of the most important cities of Vietnam and has

   developed close links with major industrial and/or agricultural companies, as well as public authorities

   and communities. This is aligned with what is known about the positive contribution of higher education

   to regional development. Studies conducted in the context of OECD’s institutional management program

   show that higher education institutions (HEIs) have a direct economic impact as employers, customers,

   and suppliers of goods and services, as well as through the effect of staff and student expenditure on

   18.      In addition, HEIs play a crucial role in developing the capacity of regions to compete in the global

   achieve regional balance in the development of the strongest pillars of the higher education landscape.



   19.       For largely historical reasons, the two VNUs hold special positions in the Vietnamese higher

   education system. As a result, they benefit from special autonomous status that gives them more

   flexibility than the other Vietnamese public universities. Instead of reporting to the Ministry of Education

   and Training (MOET), they are under the direct authority of the Office of the Prime Minister. They have a

   higher degree of autonomy (organizational and academic autonomy, management of research programs)

   and can work directly with ministries, ministerial level organizations, governmental bodies, and people’s

   committees of central cities and provinces. It is expected that UD will also receive a certain level of

   autonomy for the proposed project financing and implementation arrangements.



   20.      Finally, historically the three universities have not received sufficient funding allocation from the

   campus in Hoa Quy-Dien Ngoc district has so far received only 6 percent of the VND 7,000 billion (about

   US$300 million) that was approved in 1997; VNU-Hanoi in Hoa Lac received only 8 percent of VND 25,000

   billion (about US$1.06 billion) approved allocation; and VNU-HCM in Thu Duc has completed 40 percent

   of the approved investment plan. The proposed project investment will provide catalytic financing to the

   three universities to build additional infrastructure in key priority areas.

   B. Project Components



   26.     To achieve the PDO, the project is organized around three areas of project interventions: A1.

   improved quality of teaching and learning; A2. research excellence and knowledge transfer; and A3.

   governance and project management for each of the three targeted universities which constitute the

   three project components: Component 1. Vietnam National University Development – VNU-Hanoi;

   Component 2. Vietnam National University Development – VNU-HCM; and Component 3. Development

   of Technical Infrastructure and Essential Facilities – UD.



   27.      Intervention Area 1: Improved Quality of Teaching and Learning. The project seeks to improve

   the learning-teaching environment by investing in (i) construction of teaching and learning physical

   infrastructure (buildings, lecture halls, classrooms, labs, digital technology, and associated facilities); (ii)

   provision of cutting-edge appliances and equipment; and (iii) capacity building of universities to adopt e-

   learning, innovate pedagogical methods, carry out program accreditation and enhance partnerships with

   the industry. Infrastructure construction, appliances and technology systems of physical learning-teaching

   infrastructure will use climate-friendly “green design” elements to reduce carbon emissions and be more

   resilient to potential climate change risks such as floods and storms (further details are presented in the

   climate co-benefits section). Infrastructure will also use inclusive “universal design” so that new/upgraded

   buildings, sidewalks, toilets and other amenities are accessible for all including those with disabilities. On

   digital technology, the universities will invest in (a) standardized integrated infrastructure including smart

   lecture halls, classrooms and computer labs, (b) integrated central data centers with cloud services and

   high-speed campus networks, (c) e-libraries, (d) leveraging on existing e-learning platforms such as the

   Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) to implement distance-learning at scale, and (e) piloting an

   adaptive learning in selected training programs. A well-developed distance learning system, supported by

   appropriate digital infrastructure platform, will have the potential to reach a large number of students

   beyond the three beneficiary universities and will contribute to building a resilient and an inclusive

   teaching-learning system to respond to crises such as the current COVID-19 outbreak which resulted in

   prolonged closure of universities and other education institutions. The three universities are expected to

   collaborate on establishing a shared platform for e-learning/distance learning system. The universities will

   also invest in building the institutional capacity and staffing/faculty competencies to (i) adopt distance-

   learning, adaptive learning and other innovative teaching-leaning methods, (ii) strengthen university-

   industry partnerships for curricular revision, student job placement and graduate employability tracking

   system, and (iii) strengthen internationalization of academic programs and increase the number of

   programs internationally accredited.



   28.      Intervention Area 2: Research Excellence and Knowledge Transfer. The project aims to achieve

   research excellence and enhance knowledge transfer by investing in (i) construction of modern inter-

   disciplinary research infrastructure (research center/institute buildings, labs, experimental

   buildings/rooms, classrooms etc.); (ii) provision of cutting-edge equipment and high-end technology (lab

   strong and interdisciplinary research groups in respective priority fields, and to collaborate with national

   and international institutions, industry and government agencies on research and technology transfer.

   Similar to the teaching and learning infrastructure, research infrastructure will have “universal design” for

   inclusive accessibility and “green design” for improved climate-benefits through more efficient energy use

   and buildings more resilient to weather vulnerabilities. On digital technology, the universities will invest

   in network connectivity, VinaRen connectivity, multi-user standardized computer labs, multi-purpose HPC

   typhoons, and earthquakes. Relative to other regions in Vietnam, the Mekong Delta Region is

   E. Rationale for Bank Involvement and Role of Partners



   51.      The main rationale behind GoV’s decision to invest in the three public universities is the national

   goal of elevating their quality and relevance, which in turn will have direct and indirect positive impact on

   the socio-economic development of each region of university location and the country as a whole. As

   Vietnam aspires to become a knowledge-based economy, these three flagship universities can contribute

   significantly to moving towards that goal. While the proposed project is estimated to generate large

   economic benefits (as seen from economic analysis findings), one cannot expect significant private

   contributions to capital-intensive projects like this one because of market failure: public good nature of

   Research, Development and Innovation (RDI) expenditures leads to under-investment from the private

   sector; long-term benefit nature of this project means credit-constrained individuals and transaction cost-

   shy investors/enterprises are unlikely to invest easily. Well-performing private universities such as Royal

   Melbourne Institute of Technology (RMIT), FPT university (member of the FPT Group, the largest

   information technology company in Vietnam), Duy Tan university, and the recently established

   VinUniversity provide limited services in terms of scale and inclusion (they only cater to a small share of

   population and exclude many academically bright but economically poor students), and impact (skilled

   manpower, R&D outputs are relatively small). This provides justification for investment in large public

   universities to address the market segment that the private universities cannot reach.



   52.     The main added value from the Bank’s involvement comes from its global knowledge, track record

   and convening power in supporting key higher education reforms combined with the Bank’s familiarity

   with Vietnam’s higher education system. The knowledge transfer role inherent in both the preparation

   and implementation stages of the proposed project will be substantial. This would include facilitation of

   knowledge exchange between external experts and the beneficiary universities, provision of information

   on good practices and lessons learned from previous education reform efforts in Vietnam and worldwide,

   assistance in sequencing project interventions, and support for building core capabilities within the

   government and the universities. Importantly, the project will also benefit from the Bank’s engagement

   in the higher education policy support activities, including in the government’s preparation of the

   upcoming higher education masterplan (2021-2030). Lessons learnt from other ongoing lending projects

   – NMUP and SAHEP – are incorporated in the VUDP design. Furthermore, as indicated earlier the three

   universities under the VUDP are expected to play a crucial role in each region/city’s socio-economic

   development through provision of skilled manpower and research and innovation. Therefore, VUDP can

   benefit from and provide benefits to the Bank’s engagement (projects or analysis/policy) in other sectors

   such as agriculture and food-safety, urbanization, environment, energy, health, digital development,

   private sector, inclusion, etc. The Bank loan financing will act as catalytic funds to jump-start the

   investments needed for the three targeted universities, and additionally may open doors for other

   international/development agencies and external investors in the form of public-private partnerships

   (PPPs) to partner with these three universities.

   F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design



   54.      Technical design: The project design is informed by recommendations from the recently

   completed Higher Education ASA, particularly on strategies, policies and incentives for universities to

   enhance their teaching, learning, research quality and governance structures. The design also draws upon

   the lessons learned from other Higher Education projects in Vietnam, such as NMUP and SAHEP.

   Specifically, intervention area 1 on improved quality of teaching reflects lessons from NMUP and SAHEP

   in the areas of international accreditation of academic programs, linkages with industry partners, use of

   student satisfaction surveys to monitor improvement in the teaching-learning experience, and

   building/upgrading of classrooms. Similarly, intervention area 2 on research excellence will build on the

   experience of SAHEP and NMUP on investments in new/upgrading of labs, cutting-edge equipment, and

   building strong research teams in priority areas. Technical Assistance (TA) experience in NMUP and SAHEP

   on institutional policies and establishment of an integrated management system is reflected in VUDP.

   Importantly, global experience on innovative approaches to improve teaching, research and governance

   are also incorporated. For example, World Bank-supported schemes on graduate tracking system (GTS)

   from Romania and Belarus will be useful for VUDP to put in place a system on graduate employment

   measurement. The successful experience of Arizona State University (in USA) on the use of disruptive

   technology has informed VUDP’s decision to pilot adaptive learning in selected courses. University-

   Industry partnerships from Korea (such as that between Samsung Electronics with top universities such

   as Yonsei, Sungkyunkwan and Korea Universities) will be useful for the VUDP universities as they seek to

   update their training and research programs. High-end central research facility in Smart manufacturing at

   the Indian Institute of Technology-Delhi and Peking University’s (China) Clinical Medicine Plus X hub for

   precision medicine and health big data can serve as useful lessons for the VUDP universities in building

   their own technology hubs/parks.



   55.     Implementation: Since the bulk of the IDA credit will finance campus construction and equipment

   purchases, lessons on implementation arrangements and TA from NMUP, SAHEP and other infrastructure-

   heavy projects (such as urban projects) are reflected into VUDP’s implementation arrangements.

         lessons from SAHEP and recent urban projects will be reflected in VUDP’s implementation readiness

    III. IMPLEMENTATION ARRANGEMENTS

   A. Institutional and Implementation Arrangements



   56.     Management structure. VNU-HN, VNU-HCM and UD will be responsible for implementing the

   VUDP project activities described under Components 1, 2 and 3, respectively. The implementation of

   these components will be independent from each other. In terms of project owners, each university will

   establish a PMU with clear terms of reference and adequate competent personnel and ensure smooth

   coordination between the PMU and the member institutions. VNU-HN and VNU-HCM are their own line

   agencies while MOET is the line agency for UD. The responsibilities of line agencies will be to (a) approve

   the POM for each implementing agency; (b) review and approve the project annual work plan and

   procurement plan that are prepared by the PMU; and (c) oversee respective component implementation

   and providing support to address implementation bottlenecks. For the UD component, MOET will

   delegate the implementation responsibility to UD, and retain its limited role on oversight and approval of

   annual work plans.



   57.    Role of the PMU: The project will be implemented by a PMU to be established at each of VNU-

   responsibilities, including:



       a. Preparation of: the overall work plan; annual work plans with activities, timeline, required inputs,

       b. Implementation of activities including, but not limited to, financial management, procurement,

          safeguards, training, consulting services, goods and civil works.

       c. Project monitoring and evaluation (M&E).

       d. Coordination with concerned member universities and departments to ensure that project

          execution is aligned with their strategic development plan.

       e. Preparation of report(s) on project implementation progress and results monitoring using the

          agreed reporting format and language.

       f. Compliance with the provisions of the Financing Agreement between the Socialist Republic of

          Vietnam and IDA.

   B. Results Monitoring and Evaluation Arrangements



   60.     Results framework: The project’s PDO indicators will measure improvements in the quality of

   teaching and research. The intermediate results (IR) indicators will measure the progress in design and

   construction of infrastructure and facilities, improved training and research methods and approaches, and

   enhanced institutional and governance capacity. Both PDO and IR indicators will be monitored/measured

   for each beneficiary university. For a given PDO or IR indicator, VUDP project performance will be

   measured at the university (component) level, and aggregated (simple sums for three numbers) or

   averaged (simple averages for three proportions) at the overall project level. The PDO indicator on

   research outputs will be disaggregated by the gender of the author8 and this gender-based indicator is an

   intermediate result indicator. Details on result indicators are provided in section VI.



   61.      Source of information/surveys: University-specific data for PDO indicators 1 (international

   accreditation) and 2 (research output) are drawn from indicators on which the Universities are routinely

   required to report to MOET. For the intermediate result indicators at each university, which capture

   progress on implementation of project activities, information will be collected directly from the

   implementing entities (Universities and respective PMUs). A survey to trace students’ satisfaction with

   Technical Analysis



   66.      The proposed project design is aligned with the amended HE Law that emphasizes (i) teaching and

   learning renovations through enhanced quality and relevance of curriculum and faculty; (ii) governance

   reforms, particularly on institutional autonomy and accountability in academic affairs, organizational and

   staffing areas, and financial and asset management; and (iii) upgrading national universities to contribute

   towards achieving the country’s goals to improve human resources and productivity in priority economic

   sectors. Furthermore, the project builds on the World Bank’s “Improving Performance of Higher Education

   in Vietnam: Strategic Priorities and Policy Options” in support of the development of the Vietnam Higher

   Education Master Plan 2021-2030. In particular, the design incorporates recommendations, including

   international good practices, in the following areas: (a) Fostering innovations in teaching and learning

   practices, collaboration with international universities and university partners, taking advantage of digital

   and disruptive technologies, and quality assurance processes; (b) the Centers of Excellence approach of

   investing in infrastructure and research capacity of flagship universities; and (c) Enhanced governance

   and sustainable financing for umbrella university organizations.



   67.      The project activities are largely focused on enhancing infrastructure, equipment, technology and

   governance to improve the quality of teaching and research. Newly constructed and/or renovated

   buildings (lecturer halls, labs, other general infrastructure, etc.) are expected to incorporate good

   practices from national and international projects on building “sustainable university of the future” and/or

   “green university”. Provision of cutting-edge equipment and digital/disruptive technology will subscribe

   to criteria of effectiveness (ensuring researchers have the best facilities) and sustainability (energy

   efficiency and future-proof). Governance reforms are expected to enhance each beneficiary university’s

   organizational, human resource and financial capacity. To ensure that universities have adequate

   commitment, capacity and resources to implement the project activities and achieve the PDO, the project

   will put in appropriate mitigation measures as described in the risks section. Finally, experience from

   national and international university projects/programs are incorporated in the project design (examples

   provided in the lessons learned section).

   investments. The economic costs of the project comprise (i) VUDP investment financed by the

   Government and the participating universities; (ii) the direct costs and foregone earnings to students

   associated with pursuing university education. The CBA can derive present discounted value (PV) of

   benefit and cost streams to estimate net present value (NPV) of benefits. The analysis also takes into

   Red river. The Tich river runs exclusively within Vietnam territory. Moreover, the scale of potential

   pollution is essentially close to zero given the relative size of the wastewater treatment plants and the

   volume of Red River flow. Therefore, since the activity will not cause appreciable harm to other states,

   VUDP falls within the exception to the notification requirement specified in paragraph 7 (c) of OP 7.50 on

   International Waterways and the exception has been approved by the Bank management.

   92.      Land Acquisition and Resettlement will be necessary for the construction of infrastructure at UD.

   95.     Temporary Project Induced Labor Influx and gender-based violence (GBV). During the

   construction of the various infrastructure works, contractors may mobilize workers from outside of the

   considered low. However, there are potential social risks for communities living in the project area related

   to worker conduct and these risks include sexual assault and harassment, alcohol and drug abuse, and

   sexually transmitted diseases. Particularly vulnerable to risks of sexual assault and harassment are female

   residents and students who are living and studying in the project areas. These risks may become evident

   during the construction phase and will be mitigated through measures included in the ESMP and bidding

   documents for the construction enterprises (e.g. worker codes of conduct, community engagement, a

   GBV-sensitive grievance redress mechanism (GRM), and separate facilities for men and women). PMUs

   will be responsible for the implementation of mitigation measures related to labor influx and GBV to

   communities surrounding sub-project areas. The mitigation measures will be contractually enforced

   through the inclusion of codes of conduct in the bidding documents and the inclusion of necessary

   requirements in the C-ESMP. Independent environmental and social monitoring agencies will conduct

   monitoring the implementation of mitigation measures and report to the Bank on a semi-annual basis.

    V. KEY RISKS



   102. The overall project risk is rated as Substantial given the substantial risk ratings for several

   categories, including Macroeconomic, Institutional Capacity for Implementation and Sustainability, and

   Environment and Social and high for Fiduciary



   103. Macroeconomic risk is rated Substantial. Although macroeconomic performance has been

   strong, there is increased uncertainty due to the ongoing COVID-19 virus outbreak and its potentially large

   impact on economic growth, fiscal revenues and other macroeconomic indicators. In addition, the process

   of budgeting in the MTIP for 2021-2025 of the Government will start early in the third quarter of 2020

   requires the GoV to take immediate action once the project is approved with Universities and the MOET

   to work closely with the MPI on budget allocation annually.



   104. Institutional capacity risk is rated substantial because the capacity of the three universities to

   prepare and implement the proposed project is modest at best and the three universities are at different

   degrees of readiness. While all three universities have had experience in implementing activities financed

   by IDA, they have not implemented large-scale civil works, both of which require in-depth knowledge of

   the Bank fiduciary and safeguard policies. The project preparation documents were prepared using

   counterpart funds (CF), which may not have been sufficient to attract the most qualified international

   consultants. As a result, there is a need to pay extra attention to the quality of campus basic and detailed

   designs for construction work. At implementation phase, start-up delays are common in the Vietnam

   portfolio for reasons that generally include: (a) late MTIP fund and CF allocation; (b) delayed signing of

   the SAs between MOF and the universities; (c) insufficient CFs for land acquisition and resettlement and

   difficulties in land acquisition, compensation, and resettlement processes; (d) low procurement readiness

   requiring multiple reviews at document preparation stages; and (e) weak contract management and

   complicated and lengthy appraisal/approval procedures. To address these risks, several measures will be

   put in place. These are also informed by lessons from ongoing and past projects.

     a. By project effectiveness, each PMU will be fully staffed with competent individuals and officials that

         meet the qualification requirements acceptable to the World Bank;

     b. PMUs will be supported by quality TA on technical design, financial management, procurement,

         contract management and safeguards. PMU staff will be provided with relevant training on these

         areas. Joint learning events will be organized for the three PMUs to maximize knowledge sharing.

     c. Pre-procurement activities will be accelerated before project effectiveness to facilitate timely

         procurement of key packages in first year of implementation. These efforts will include prioritizing

         the preparation of procurement packages not requiring land acquisition in Year 1, early preparation

         of bidding documents, and the early drafting of TORs (particularly for the TA activities);

     d. Land clearance and land resettlement (for UD) process to be planned and implemented with

         sufficient fund allocation and stakeholder consultations.

     e. PMUs and the Universities will report regularly on (i) annual budget allocation of each university to

         cover the CF commitments through the annual work plan which will require a Bank’s NOL prior to

         execution; (ii) delays in the release of MTIP and counterpart funds; (iii) other emerging project

         challenges; and

     f. Strong commitments from Universities and the MOET have been secured to ensure project

         management support and monitoring of construction during implementation.

   105. Moreover, in the current structure in which loosely connected member universities operate under

   the umbrella universities, the three beneficiary Universities may not be fully taking advantage of the

   existing talent, knowledge and capacities dispersed across many separate member institutions. VUDP

   addresses this issue partly by supporting a university-side shared teaching, research and ICT infrastructure

   and facilities. The project will bring the member universities of the three national universities closer to

   each other (through representation at each PMU) and to achieve the overall university objectives on

   teaching-research and governance. The three universities’ internal governance are also quite different

   from each other, thus posing additional challenges for the Bank during project implementation support.

   Lessons from ongoing and past projects will inform the nature and frequency of such supervisions. In

   addition to the regular bi-annual project implementation support missions, monthly project monitoring

   meetings with each University, and extensive implementation and technical support to the PMUs, would

   be warranted especially in the initial stages of project implementation.



   106. The fiduciary risk is rated high (FM risk is substantial and Procurement risk is high) mainly because

   the three universities are neither familiar with the Bank’s FM and procurement requirements particularly

   related to large civil work nor experienced with execution of World Bank financed projects. Additional FM

   risks include lack of clarity of the delegated FM authority from MOET to UD and inadequate budget

   allocation (MTIP and counterpart funds) to universities. Additional procurement risks include potential

   preference to using national procurement rules and procedures, potential fraud, corruption and collusive

   practices as evidenced from experience with similar World Bank-financed projects and inadequate

   contract management capacity. FM and Procurement risks and their mitigation measures are described

   fully in section IV.B Appraisal Summary on FM and Procurement assessment.



   107. Substantial environment and social risks primarily relate to moderate safeguard impacts and

   risks, and substantial institutional risk relating to weak capacity of the implementing agencies who do not

   have prior experience in managing ODA-funded project, specifically managing environmental and social

   risk and impact. To mitigate this, PMUs will be required to assign dedicated qualified staff for Safeguards

   and all staff of implementing agencies should be involved in training on implementation of safeguards and

   management of risks and impacts. The Project will also hire Independent Monitoring Consultants to carry

   out: (i) capacity building for the PMU including assisting PMU in setting up environmental and social

   management systems and providing training for staff on monitoring and reporting; and (ii) independent

   monitoring to verify compliance to ESMPs, RAP, GBV and labor influx. The Bank team will also conduct

   regular implementation support mission and provide hands-on training for PMU staff. Social risk rating of

   the project is also Substantial. Mitigation measures also include (i) preparation and implementation of

   RAP for the UD subcomponents; (ii) construction of resettlement site within subcomponent ward for

   relocated households; (iii) addition to compensation, a livelihood restoration program for severely

   affected households to be prepared and implemented; (iv) consultation with different stakeholders,

   including affected people; (v) training on Gender-Based-Violence GBV for contract workers and local

   communities; (vi) signing codes of conduct with contract workers; and (vii) establishment and

   implementation of a grievance redress mechanism.



   108. COVID-19 Risks: The ongoing COVID-19 pandemic is likely to affect the project in a number of

   ways. With further extended closure of universities and social distancing measures, a number of project

   readiness activities such as advance procurement, detailed construction design, and land

   clearance/resettlement may be delayed. Universities will face challenges in mobilizing their staff and in

   particular the expert consultants in a timely manner. Moreover, the revenue losses for the universities

   from extended campus closures (tuition, service fees and R&D/advisory service fees, and other ancillary



                                                                                                        Page 42 of 73

    The World Bank

    Vietnam University Development of VNU-Hanoi, VNU-HCM, and UD (P166656)





       [parking, cafeteria] service fees) will put a severe strain on the universities’ financial position in the short

       run at least. The universities addressed the academic training issue with distance learning modality but

       like many universities in Vietnam and elsewhere, they were not prepared. As pointed out in the country

       and sectoral context as well as project description sections, VUDP offers a critical opportunity for the three

       universities to invest in digital infrastructure and platform to implement distance learning at scale,

       enhance a medical school facility, and re-orient their research and technology focus under the priority

       fields on health science, biomedical and pharmaceutical technologies to help the country build resilience

       in anticipation of the next wave of COVID-19 and future pandemics.



       109. Climate Change Screening. A Climate & Disaster Risk Screening was conducted, and the project

       was found to have moderate risk to climate change and disasters overall. Given that a high proportion of

       the country’s population and economic assets are in coastal lowlands and deltas, the World Bank has

       ranked Vietnam as among the five countries likely to be most affected by climate change. The proposed

       project beneficiary universities in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City are in the planned area away from the city

       center and unlikely to be affected by high risks associated with typhoon storm surges, sea-level rise and

       flooding. UD (in Da Nang) may be subject to these risks at a moderate level. Nevertheless, the project

       design on campus building and associated infrastructure for three universities will incorporate climate

.      and disaster risks factors, and other mitigation measures.

                       ANNEX 1: Implementation Arrangements and Support Plan



 1. The World Bank’s implementation plan highlights the support to implementation of the risk

 mitigation measures as identified in the Systematic Operations Risk-Rating Tool (SORT) and build on

 implementation experience of completed and ongoing education projects to ensure achievement of the

 PDO. The support will focus on (i) helping the Universities to unblock operational bottlenecks and building

 implementation capacity; (ii) ensuring compliance across fiduciary, social, and environmental domains;

 and (iii) providing technical advice and support to strengthen the technical quality of implementation. The

 extent of implementation support that can be provided will depend on the mobilization of complementary

 resources.



 2. Within the technical domain, the implementation support focuses on technical assistance to, among

 others, (i) campus design and construction and lab equipment; (ii) embracing new technologies in campus

 design and innovative teaching; and (iii) modern university governance. In terms of operational domain,

 the largest share of the World Bank’s implementation support will go to the IDA-financed activities on

 construction and equipment. With respect to strengthening compliance, technical assistance will be

 needed as described in the relevant sections of the Appraisal Summary.



 3. While implementation support will be provided throughout the project implementation, it is

 anticipated that more intense support will be needed at the two points in time: during the first 12 months

 (from approval to effectiveness and through early implementation) and at midterm review. With land

 clearance and resettlement taking place for the University of Danang and detailed designs at all three

 universities, coupled with getting the subsidiary agreements signed, implementation support in the first

 year of the project will focus on creating project implementation momentum through institutional

 capacity strengthening in project management, preparation for first construction, equipment and

 consultant packages, and technical assistance for technical design using new technologies.



 4. Close project supervision will be undertaken by a multidisciplinary team consisting of safeguards,

 fiduciary, and technical specialists. Most of the team members have been involved in supervision of

 education projects. Project supervision will continue emphasis on foreseeing future issues and realistic

 action plans. The World Bank’s implementation support plan consists of scheduled full supervision

 missions every six to 12 months and short review missions focusing on problem solving and timely follow-

 up monitoring. Both task team leaders are based in Vietnam who will enable daily project implementation

 support. A midterm review conducted after about three years of implementation will identify and

 implement any structural changes. Enhanced fiduciary supervision is foreseen given the complexity of the

 operation, particularly at the start of the operation. Enhanced safeguard supervision may be needed

 during the first three years of the project when civil works and resettlement monitoring will constitute a

 substantial workload.



 5. Technical expertise will be maintained throughout project implementation and increased as needed

 during critical implementation periods to review and provide recommendations. Active involvement of

 the task team members and the PMUs in daily work will help reduce design and implementation risk.

                                    ANNEX 2: Financial and Economic Analysis



 Financial Analysis for VNU-Hanoi



 1. Assumptions: The main assumptions are as follows: (i) tuition fees increase by 3 percent annually

 during 2021-2026 and by 5 percent during 2027-2042; (ii) revenues from state allocation do not grow in

 2020 and 2021, decline by 5 percent annually during 2020-2026, and are further cut by 10 percent during

 2027-2042, (iii) revenues from business and services provision increase by 2 percent during 2020-2022

 and (iv) university will receive up to 90 percent of their counterpart funding would be allocated from the

 state budget. The assumptions also take into account potential negative impact of Covid-19 pandemic on

 university revenue sources. Under these assumptions, affordability analysis is performed by analyzing the

 university’s Development Fund and Discretionary Fund which reflects the free cash flow that could be

 used for new investment and debt repayment. Furthermore, the university will commit at least 25 percent

 of after-tax margins as annual allocation to Development Fund during 2020-2042, 45 percent of

 Development Fund allocation would be earmarked for Discretionary Fund during the VUDP project life

 2. Revenue: VNU-HN obtains revenue 17 from state budget allocation, tuition fees, student services, and

 other business and services activities. State budget allocation currently makes up the vast majority of total

 revenues, followed by tuition fees. Despite the projected gradual cut in allocation from the state budget,

 the revenue of VNU-HN is expected to increase steadily during the period from 2021 to 2025, particularly

 to support the upgrading of both infrastructure and non-infrastructure aspects of teaching, learning, and

 research. Revenue from state budget allocation accounts for about 51 percent in total revenue. However,

 it is projected to decrease by 5 to 10 percent annually based on the Autonomy agenda of the university.

 Tuition fee income is calculated based on the number of students, detailed in the VNUHN’s Strategy and

 Development Plan, and the agenda for tuition fee increase, allowed to increase by at most 10 percent per

 also take into account potential impact of household income loss on students’ ability to pay. VUDP will

 support VNU-HN to upgrade their teaching and training infrastructure and therefore, the tuition increases

 VUDP-induced improvement in institutional governance). The total operational expense is high compared

 to total revenue, being about 88 percent in 2018 but projected to decline to 77 percent in 2042.

 ECONOMIC ANALYSIS



 1. Economic benefits: Economic benefits of the VUDP project are estimated using the conventional

 approach of education and include the following components 18:

     a. For VUDP beneficiary students: incremental earnings of the beneficiary students in the presence

 of VUDP interventions;

     b. For Vietnam’s economy (regional and national): increase in the economy’s total output as a result

 of VUDP’s R&D investments.



 2. Private returns: The economic benefits from VUDP would be the incremental earnings of university

 graduates during the project cycle that benefit from VUDP interventions. In the base-case scenario, VUDP

 is expected to result in 6.5 percent points increase (equivalent to half-a-year training premium) in annual

 earnings for each VUDP beneficiary graduate compared with his/her earnings in the absence of VUDP. In

 general, there are two major channels through which a university/HE degree improves individuals’

 earnings. First, pursuing higher education improves individuals’ skills, making them more competitive,

 employable and ear more in the labor market. Second, having a higher education degree provides

 signaling on the graduate’s higher abilities to the employer 19 and will receive the premium in the labor

 market, independent of skill improvement.



 3. We estimate the private returns to HE degree in three steps, using data from the 2019 Vietnam

 Enterprise Survey of Skills and Innovation (ESSI). First, the effects of HE on individuals’ marketable skills

 are estimated using a Mincerian-type human capital function (Mincer, 1974) 20. The outcome variables are

 three important dimensions of skills on the Vietnam labor market – literacy skill, creativity and innovating

 skill, teamwork and collaborating skill. The analysis estimates an expected increase (in percent change) in

 individuals’ skills when they pursue HE instead of going straight to the labor market after high school

 completion. Second, the effects of HE on individuals’ lifetime earnings are estimated through an improved

 version of Mincerian earnings function 21. At the individual level, the analysis estimates (i) the returns of

 individuals’ incremental skills resulting from HE attendance on their earnings, and (ii) the returns of HE

 degree, net of the skill effects, on their earnings compared with the base-group of high-schoolers.



 4. The findings are shown the Table A.2.4 below. For every university graduate, the individual (and

 hence the society) would annually gain an amount equal to 52.2 percent 22 of annual earnings that the

 student was receiving before going to university, of which 6 percent arising from improvements in literacy,

 creativity, and collaboration ability; and the remaining 46.2 percent arising from having the university

 degree. For a university graduate, this would amount to about US$2,100 per year (current value). This is
