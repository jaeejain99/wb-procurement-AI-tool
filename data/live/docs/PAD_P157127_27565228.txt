I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT



 A. Country Context



        1. Vietnam has made remarkable achievements in poverty reduction and growth over the past 30

           years. Since the launch of economic reforms, known as Doi Moi, Vietnam has attained middle

           income status. Vietnam has also achieved most of the Millennium Development Goals. Its per capita

           gross domestic product (GDP) growth since 1990 has been among the fastest in the world. Vietnam

           has realized stable and inclusive growth, which has resulted in tangible gains for Vietnamese people,

           economically and with regard to the non-income dimensions of welfare.

        2. Despite its achievements, Vietnam still faces some development challenges. About 82 percent of

           the near poor and 84 percent of the ‘bottom 40’ percent of Vietnam are found in rural areas. About

           70 percent of the bottom 40 percent are located in four regions: Red River Delta (RRD), eastern

           Northern Mountains, North Central Coast (NCC), and Mekong Delta. Large numbers of the near poor

           are also found in the RRD and the Mekong Delta regions. Moreover, environmental stresses

           threaten the sustainability of Vietnam’s growth. Past growth has imposed significant environmental

           costs, including rapid depletion of natural resources, compromising their services and use as a

           productive asset.

        3. Vietnam is also extremely vulnerable to climate change, including sea level rise and extreme

           weather events. Vietnam’s long coastline, geographic location, diverse topography, and variable

           climate contribute to it being one of the most hazard-prone countries in the region. Vietnam’s

           exposure is compounded by the fact that a high proportion of the country’s population and

           economic assets are located in coastal lowlands and deltas. Extreme climatic events such as storms,

           heavy rainfall, and flooding have caused damage to boats, housing, coastal vegetation, and

           aquaculture facilities. They have also significantly affected poor rural households as a large share of

           their income comes from climate sensitive activities—crop cultivation, livestock, forestry, fishing and

           agriculture.1 The expected change in climate will likely affect these activities and also threaten

           important natural services — such as water availability and ecosystem services — affecting the poor

           in multiple ways.

        4. The Government of Vietnam (GoV) has historically invested in physical structures to build

           resilience of its coastal areas. Approximately 1,400 km of dykes are directly exposed to the sea in 29

           provinces and cities across the country. Historically, sea dyke development has been focused on

           northern and central Vietnam, which are the areas most exposed to typhoons. These sea dykes are

           meant to protect 630,000 ha of agricultural land and about 8.7 million inhabitants. The effectiveness

           of these systems has declined over the years, and their shortcomings have become more apparent.

           Afforestation and reforestation of coastal forests, such as mangroves, are alternative approaches

           that offer notable benefits such as reduced cost of dyke maintenance, and improved coastal

           resilience and livelihoods.

        5. Coastal communes need support to enhance their resilience to climate change, as vulnerability to

           sea level rise, climate variability and severe storms is projected to increase. Inaction will have

           consequences for several sectors, ranging from the energy sector to agriculture, and the poor given

           the overlap of poor households’ sources of livelihood and their vulnerability to flooding. In parts of

           Vietnam’s coast, where winds and storms are major threats, coastal forests can assist in reducing

           the impact of these winds and also contain sand dune expansion.





B. Sectoral and Institutional Context



        6. The GoV has approved various plans, strategies, and decrees to use coastal protection forests to

           improve the well-being of these rural poor and reduce their exposure to climate risks. The Prime

           Minister’s Decision 120, dated January 2015, approved the plan for coastal forest protection and

           development in response to climate change. This set as targets (a) the protection of the existing

           coastal forest area of 310,965 ha and (b) the establishment of new plantation of 46,058 ha. The aim

           is to increase the coastal forest coverage from its current level of 16.9 percent to 19.5 percent by

           2020. The resilience objective and importance assigned to coastal forests is also reflected in Ministry

           of Agriculture and Rural Development’s (MARD’s) Targeted Program for Sustainable Forest

           Development 2016-2020 (TP-SFD). The TF-SPD aims to continue to manage, protect, develop, and

           sustainably use forests and land zoned for forestry development, while increasing total forest

           coverage to 45 percent by 2020. The TP-SFD includes, among others, the following priority scheme:

           to protect and develop coastal forests to respond to climate change. The Government also adopted

           Decree 119/2016/ND-CP on management, protection and development of coastal forests,2

           underscoring the importance of these forests for resilience to climate change.

        7. While Vietnam’s forest cover has increased overall, coastal forests have been severely degraded.

           Mangrove forests have decreased by nearly one-third, from 408,500 ha in 1943 to 270,000 ha in

           2015. In parts of Vietnam, coastal forests have been converted to land uses that are more lucrative

           in the short term and often unsustainable. Limited coordination across sectors in spatial planning,

           weak management practices, and severe weather events have also caused coastal forest

           degradation. The drivers of mangrove deforestation range from industrial scale aquaculture, to

           harvesting for fuelwood, to development of infrastructure that alter the hydrological conditions

           needed to sustain the mangrove systems. Coastal forests in sandy soil areas (referred to as sandy

           soil forests in this document) found in the NCC, are often degraded or converted due to

           encroachment from agriculture.

        8. The GoV has taken several measures to improve planning in coastal areas, including areas with

           coastal forests, but implementation is lagging behind. There are Government decisions3 that adopt

     an ICZM Program in specific provinces, present a detailed ICZM strategy, and stipulate that

     ministries, ministerial-level agencies, and Provincial People’s Committees (PPCs) of coastal cities and

     provinces must develop ICZM programs and that all relevant companies, organizations and

     individuals must adhere to the requirements of ICZM. There is also the National Coastal Zone Action

     Plan (2016–2020), which helps implement priorities in the ICZM Strategy. The action plan

     emphasizes the sustainable use of natural resources in coastal zones and guides coordination among

     key sectors. It also recognizes that the bulk of coastal management will be carried out at the

     provincial level, calling for better vertical integration among all levels of government.

     Implementation of these decisions has been limited because of the lack of data and limited

     provincial capacity to develop well-integrated plans.

 9. Coastal forests are primarily classified as protection forests and most of the coastal forest lands

    are under the direct management of the Government. Coastal forests are often managed by the

    Commune People’s Committees (CPCs) or Protection Forest Management Boards (PFMBs) and SUF

    Management Boards. Some coastal protection forests have been temporarily assigned to

    enterprises or tourism organizations for management. Individuals, households, and communities

    have very limited tenure rights over coastal forest land and limited access to coastal forests.

 10. Incentives are often lacking for local stakeholders and investors to protect and restore these

     coastal forests for long-term benefits. Local stakeholders (that is, the Government, communities,

     and private stakeholders) face a trade-off when considering investing in the protection of coastal

     forests. The trade-off is between short-term tangible local gains (for example, from converting

     coastal forest areas for cultivation or infrastructure such as, ports or fishing piers, or illegal

     exploitation) and longer-term public goods (for example, resilience to climate change and

     opportunities to sustainably harvest or benefit from non-timber products). These stakeholders do

     not receive tangible benefits from the public goods, making it important to provide incentives for

     long-term management and protection of coastal forest systems. It is also necessary to raise the

     awareness of provincial governments about the importance of coastal forest systems for their

     growth and employment agendas.

 11. Restoring coastal forests is complex. Mangroves require close attention to their intertidal position

     and how they are exposed to various ocean and atmospheric climate change drivers, which

     increases their vulnerability to climate change. Mangroves are sensitive to excessive sea level

     fluctuations, with too much flooding causing mangroves to ‘drown’ and too little affecting their

     productivity and possibly replacing them with salt marsh or cyanobacterial communities. Restoring

     mangroves, therefore, requires matching the species composition with soil salinity and humidity and

     investing in preparing site conditions that are within the control of the project. In the RRD, it will

     also requires working with and addressing changes in the landscape caused by dams, roads, and

     dredging of channels that are altering tidal flow patterns.

 12. Vietnam has examples of successful mangrove restoration in various locations, though mostly at a

     small scale. The Deutsche Gesellschaft für International Zusammenarbeit (GIZ) GmbH supported

     mangrove rehabilitation in Soc Trang, Bac Lieu, and Kien Giang Provinces. These activities

     successfully tested, in difficult sites, different strategies to enhance the protective function of

     coastal mangrove forests. Non-governmental organizations (NGOs) such as the Danish Red Cross,

     Japanese Red Cross, and UK Children's Fund successfully supported the partial planting of mangrove

     forests. Over 20,000 ha were planted along the Northeastern and North Delta estuaries for dyke

     protection. National universities and local organizations have been actively involved in identifying

     suitable species, and providing expertise and knowledge to mangrove restoration efforts, bolstering

     these initiatives.

 13. Vietnam has also made investments in planting sandy soil forests, though activities at scale have

     been limited. The Project for Afforestation on the Coastal Area supported the planting of Casuarina

     equisetifolia and auxiliary species of Acacia crassicarpain in coastal areas affected by wind and

     saltwater. In a few sites, with low cost inputs and labor, pilot efforts have been successful in

     stabilizing sand dunes with the planting of casuarina trees. Casuarina is the species of choice

     because it is fairly tolerant of poor soils and weather. Implementation of similar initiatives at a larger

     scale will require coordination among local farmers, local government and forest managers. It will

     require long-term commitment, financial and technical resources, and incentives for all parties

     involved.

 14. Future efforts to restore coastal forests will need to adopt the lessons from these previous

     experiences. Some key lessons include: (a) planting only where coastal forests previously existed; (b)

     matching tree species with site conditions; (c) using high quality seedlings; (d) aligning planting and

     maintenance techniques with site characteristics; (e) investing in improving site conditions; and, (f)

     planting and management activities, incentives for forest and land managers and provincial

     governments to commit to managing the natural asset for key environmental services and economic

     benefits.

III. PROJECT DESCRIPTION



 A. Project Components



        29. The project has three technical components in addition to a fourth component on project

            management, monitoring, and evaluation. The first component modernizes the technical know-

            how and approaches used for planning, supplying seedlings, and financing coastal protection forest

            management and planting. The second component adopts a holistic approach that links the

            biophysical, physical, and management interventions for managing and planting mangroves and

            sandy soil forests. The third component focuses on augmenting the economic benefits derived from

            well-protected coastal forests, with the aim of reducing pressure to convert these natural systems.

        30. Component 1 will be implemented at the central level. Components 2 and 3 will be implemented at

            the subnational (provincial, district, and commune) level. The first year of activities will not include

            any physical works or investments that require the development of safeguard plans.

  Component 1: Enabling Effective Coastal Forest Management (IDA: 0; GoV: US$3 million)

 31. This component will support the implementation of scalable procedures and adoption of tools to

     improve coastal forest management. The activities associated with this component will build the

     technical know-how and make the investments needed to modernize the approaches used to

     address three key constraints—overlapping spatial plans, lack of adequate supply of quality

     seedlings, and long-term financing for managing coastal protection forests.

 32. In Vietnam, spatial plans are often difficult to implement. Spatial planning generally refers to

     master plans or strategic plans for land use or socioeconomic development for the longer term (10

     or more years). The plans for different sectors often offer contradictory guidance because they are

     not coordinated. The Government is, as part of its Support Program to Respond to Climate Change

     (SPRCC) supported under the Climate Change and Green Growth development policy financing (DPF)

     series, promoting coordinated and integrated spatial planning for coastal areas. The Government

     has established multisectoral committees at the national and subnational levels for Integrated

     Coastal Resources Management (ICRM). This makes it an opportune time to invest in technologies

     and practices needed to help coordinate across different sectoral planning processes.

 33. The second constraint stems from the demand for high-quality seedlings often exceeding supply

     capacity. This is because the infrastructure and technical capacity for expanding quality seedlings

     production are limited and outdated. MARD has recently taken steps to improve seedling

     production, including the development of a national plan and the issuance of technical guidelines4

     for specific species that regulate conditions for seed sources and specifies technical methods for

     harvesting propagules and producing seedlings. There is now the need for investments to

     operationalize the elements of the plan.

 34. The third challenge is the need to increase financing for coastal protection forest management.

     Currently, management and planting are primarily financed by state- or donor-supported programs

     and projects. Effective management is, therefore, subject to the Government cost norms and

     budget constraints. Mobilizing private support to manage coastal protection forests for their

     ecosystem services could help secure long-term financing.

C. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design



       54. The lessons that have informed the design of this project are from similar World Bank financed

           projects that have been or are being implemented internationally and in Vietnam. Lessons were

           extracted from projects implemented in Bangladesh on coastal forests; ICZM projects in India;

           desertification and coastal protection projects in China; projects on productive alliances in the Latin

           America region; and wetland, climate resilience, and forest management projects in Vietnam.

       55. Several key lessons are extracted from these comparable projects. The relevant lessons are on (a)

           technical aspects of planting—including the importance of phasing planting activities to first

           ‘stabilize’ areas where planting will be happening (for example, use of straw checkerboards in sandy

           soil areas) and ensure the land areas for new planting have necessary characteristics (for example,

           accretion areas have the necessary depth); (b) adopting a holistic approach to coastal resilience that

           blends natural and physical measures for protecting coastal areas; (c) promoting local ownership by

           providing local stakeholders with tangible monetary or nonmonetary benefits from project





                                                                                                             Page 23 of 102

      The World Bank

      Forest Sector Modernization and Coastal Resilience Enhancement Project (P157127)







            activities; (d) assisting with the formation of effective productive alliances by conducting a detailed

            analysis of value chains, targeting beneficiaries based on their ability to comply with markets, being

            demand-driven, and encouraging cofinancing to augment ownership; and (e) using simple but locally

            proven approaches to implementation in an environment where capacity is relatively weak.

        56. The technical lessons informed the design of the three technical components of this project. The

            technical lessons on phasing planting, prioritizing stabilization of coastal areas, and the adoption of a

            holistic approach from projects in Bangladesh, China, India, and Vietnam are reflected in Component

            2 in how the sites are assessed, the design of planting and structures are linked, and investments are

            made in soft measures to assist with stabilizing planting areas and augmenting the survival of the

         64. The project activities focus on key areas of support for delivering on the Government’s objective

B. Technical



       79. The project design is drawn on the global and regional good practice on coastal forest restoration.

           The work done in South Asia, China, and Latin America regions offers valuable lessons that have

           been factored into the project design. This includes lessons on the importance of site assessments in

           shaping investments (both for physical infrastructure and planting coastal forests), consideration of

           factors within and beyond the control of the project (for example, weather patterns), the

           implementation arrangements for managing the coastal forests, and the importance of economic

           benefits from the protected forests.

       80. The project design involves an approach that is considered to be technically sound. Detailed

           planting designs for each site will be developed following a comprehensive site assessment. The

           planting design will specify technical standards and implementation requirements to ensure planting

           success and long-term survival. Parties contracted for planting will be accountable for adherence to

           the agreed standards and, if required, responsible for replanting the following year to meet the

           planting and survival targets. Measures are also being taken to use high-quality seedlings from

           suppliers and, as part of that, to use local nurseries to reduce damage during transportation and

           guarantee the seedling is adapted to the local conditions.

       81. The sequencing of activities during implementation has been detailed in the POM. There is a

           limited planting season in the project sites. The team, therefore, has prepared a schedule to ensure

           that the planting-related activities (for example, site assessments, validations, detailed design of

           planting works, field checks, and acceptances) are implemented in a specific time frame, so that

           planting can proceed successfully. This schedule is reflected in the POM. The planting will be done in

           batches to accommodate seedling supply constraints, and the first planting batch will cover the area

           that can be done with a readily available quantity of seedlings and is expected to be modest. During

           that time, the team will be disseminating information on seedling requirements (for example,

           quantity, species, and specifications) to potential seedling suppliers (it is anticipated that 1 year is

                                 ANNEX 1: DETAILED PROJECT DESCRIPTION



                                     Annex 1: Detailed Project Description

            Vietnam: Forest Sector Modernization and Coastal Resilience Enhancement Project



   1. Vietnam is extremely vulnerable to climate change, including sea level rise and extreme weather

      events. Vietnam’s long coastline, geographic location, diverse topography, and variable climate

      contribute to it being one of the most hazard-prone countries in the region. Storms and floods are,

      in particular, responsible for economic and human losses. Vietnam’s exposure is compounded by

      the fact that a high proportion of the country’s population and economic assets are located in

      coastal lowlands and deltas. Extreme climatic events such as storms, heavy rainfall, and flooding,

      damage boats, housing, coastal vegetation, and aquaculture facilities. Vulnerability to sea level rise,

      climate variability and severe storms that have been observed in several coastal areas and are

      projected to increase have consequences for several sectors, ranging from the energy sector to

      agriculture.

   2. The GoV has historically invested in physical structures to build resilience of its coastal areas.

      Approximately 1,400 km of dykes are directly exposed to the sea in 29 provinces and cities across

      the country. Historically, sea dyke development has been focused on northern and central Vietnam,

      which are the areas most exposed to typhoons. These sea dykes are meant to protect 630,000 ha of

      agricultural land and about 8.7 million inhabitants. The effectiveness of these systems has declined

      over the years and their shortcomings have become more apparent. Afforestation and reforestation

      of coastal forests, such as mangroves, can offer notable benefits as they reduce the cost of dyke

      maintenance while improving coastal resilience and livelihoods. In parts of Vietnam’s coast where

      winds and storms are a major threat, coastal forests can reduce the impact of these winds and also

      contain sand dune expansion.

   3. Existing tracts of intact and mature mangrove forests can play a vital role in coastal protection.

      Mature mangroves can mitigate wave and storm impacts and stabilize mudflats while protecting

      near-shore water quality. The trunks and above-ground roots of mangrove forests influence the

      hydrodynamics and sediment transport within forests. These mangroves also dissipate the incoming

      wave energy and reduce erosion rates. Analysis done in the RRD found that high-density six-year-old

      mangrove forests provided significant wave reduction due to drag force on the trees. Other

      evidence reveals that the existing mangroves in the northern part of the country are not effective in

      providing similar levels of wave protection to the coastline because of their current forest structure

      and width.

   4. Terrestrial coastal forests in sandy soil areas (referred to, for purposes of this project, as sandy soil

      forests) can also play an important role in the stabilization of the coastline, which is vulnerable to

      storm impacts. In the central region of Vietnam, the sandy coasts are vulnerable to erosion from sea

      water surge, strong winds, and high waves. In the coastal tidal flats, the areas with natural

      mangrove forests are small (for example, near river mouths or along channels in the coastal area).

      The approach adopted in Vietnam to date to curb the coastal erosion in provinces vulnerable to

      sand and wind storm erosion has been to plant protective casuarina forests on the coast. Casuarina

      is a tree species that can grow fast and withstand strong wind. It has a wide and deep root system

      helping limit the erosion of the sandy coast that results from sea water surge, strong wind, and high

      waves.

The World Bank

Forest Sector Modernization and Coastal Resilience Enhancement Project (P157127)







   5. The success of investments to restore both mangrove and sandy soil forests throughout Vietnam

      and, more specifically, the RRD and north central provinces has been mixed. Past examples,

      however, offer valuable insights for new efforts to restore coastal forests. Lessons from successful

      restoration of mangroves and sandy soil forests by the Red Cross in the RRD and numerous projects

      financed by the German and Japanese Governments include

              a. The ‘bottom-up’ approach that involves comanagement or approaches that support

                  participation of communities in ‘forest management’ are effective because they provide

                  secure access to the forest resources;

              b. Importance is given to identifying private financing for restoration efforts or amending

                  the existing cost norms because these norms constrain the management approach and

                  limit incentives;

              c. The planting and tending activities are conducted over a period of five years; thus, the

                  workload of years 2, 3, and 4 can be heavy and require a significant amount of seed

                  supply and competent labor for planting, tending, management, and monitoring;

              d. The planting is a seasonal activity (one planting season per year) and the coastal areas

                  are further characterized by flood frequency. It is, therefore, important to factor in

                  climate parameters when planning implementation and monitoring and ensure all

                  preparatory activities are done on time to enable planting during the appropriate

                  season;

              e. Due to the seasonal characteristics of project implementation and the capacity of

                  providers who provide research, design, and seedling supply services, it is important to

                  develop procurement/bidding plans of goods and services that recognize these

                  characteristics of project implementation and markets and identify ways to maintain

                  competitiveness; and

              f. The characteristics of local people living in the coastal areas of Vietnam’s central region

                  are such that their livelihood activities are dependent on the sea and fisheries.

                  Therefore, the project should have plans/approaches for livelihood transformation

                  through the protection and sustainable development of coastal forests.

   6. The PDO is to improve coastal forest management in the Project Provinces. Improved

      management includes (a) developing necessary measures to support effective spatial planning of

      coastal forests; (b) ensuring arrangements for effectively managing the resource base; (c) adoption

      of appropriate silvicultural measures for protection, planting, and enrichment planting of coastal

      forests; and (d) providing incentives to local households, communities, enterprises, and the

      Government to maintain and protect the resource base.

   7. The project focuses on three elements that are central to sustainable restoration of coastal

      protection forest: (a) putting in place conditions to maintain the needed inputs (for example, good

      planning and seedlings) and financing to restore coastal protection forests, (b) investing in the

      planting and silvicultural management practices and in physical works/structures needed to

      augment the survival and effectiveness of coastal forests, and (c) diversifying and increasing the

      benefits at the local level from managing coastal protection forests. The project will use modern

      systems for monitoring change, expand Payments for Forest Environmental Services (PFES),

      promote improved planning, and use quantitative data analysis to determine suitable sites and

      levels of investment. It will also use modern technology to increase revenue generated from

      managing coastal protection forests and promote integrated value addition. The proposed

      methods/approaches that modernize forest management and revenue generation have been



                                                                                                      Page 44 of 102

The World Bank

Forest Sector Modernization and Coastal Resilience Enhancement Project (P157127)







      previously tested in countries similar to Vietnam. Their application in Vietnam, through other World

      Bank supported projects, has also proven to be cost-effective and successful.

   8. The project has four components including the component on project management and M&E. The

      first component aims to augment the technical know-how and modernize the approaches used for

      putting in place key conditions for effective management and restoration of coastal protection

      forests. These conditions include improved spatial planning of coastal forests, access to quality

      seedling supply, and financing the management of the coastal forest systems. The second

      component adopts a holistic approach to using forests for coastal protection. The approach

      integrates the biophysical investments in new plantings and enrichment of planting of coastal

      forests with both investments in physical works and supporting structures and investments in

      management practice. With a holistic approach, the interactions among the different investments

      and practices are considered. The aim is to ensure that the investments and approaches reinforce

      each other. Adopting a holistic approach will help augment the survivability of the coastal forests

      that are planted by the project. The second component also invests in community-based

      management of existing coastal forests to augment the interest and support for these forests

      among local households. The third component focuses on diversifying and augmenting the

      economic benefits derived from well-protected coastal forests, with the aim of reducing the

      pressure to convert these natural systems.



       Component 1: Enabling Effective Coastal Forest Management (IDA: 0; GoV: US$3 million)

   9. This component will support activities that address three constraints to improved management of

       coastal protection forests: the limited capacity to integrate the spatial planning of coastal

       protection forests with other sector plans; the shortage of quality seedlings; and limited access to

       private financing.

   10. Although spatial planning is occurring in Vietnam and there are efforts to coordinate among

       sectors, it is often difficult to implement spatial plans. In Vietnam, spatial planning generally refers

       to master plans or strategic plans for land use or socioeconomic development for the longer term

       (10 or more years). The plans for different sectors sometimes offer contradictory guidance, making

       them difficult to implement. This is largely attributable to the way these plans are prepared—they

       are developed separately and often reflect subjective opinions and interests of one sector or

       province without consideration for the linkages across sectors. The plans are often prepared in

       compliance with administrative procedures, yet are not always based on analysis of the long-term

       inventory or needs of emerging markets. The latter is largely because the analysis and forecasting

       capacity is weak.

   11. The Government is, as part of the SPRCC supported under the Climate Change and Green Growth

       DPF, promoting coordinated and integrated spatial planning for coastal areas. The Government

       has established the multisectoral National Coordination Committee for ICRM. It is also supporting

       the establishment of councils/committees that coordinate planning processes across sectors at the

       central level. The committee is coordinated by the MONRE with the participation of other line

       ministries including MARD; the MPI; the MOF; and the Ministry of Culture, Tourism, and Sport. At

       the provincial level, coastal provinces are setting up their ICRM committee for coordination across

       sectors and among provinces. This makes it an opportune time to complement the DPF support with

       investment in best practices and technologies for improving the integration of spatial plans.

   12. Another key constraint faced by coastal protection forest restoration initiatives is the limited

       availability of high-quality seedlings. When there are large planting programs, demand for high-



                                                                                                         Page 45 of 102

The World Bank

Forest Sector Modernization and Coastal Resilience Enhancement Project (P157127)







       quality seedlings often can exceed supply, especially if programs do not include investments in the

       infrastructure and technical capacity for expanding seedlings production. MARD has developed a

       long-term plan for expanding quality seedling production through 2020. It includes the

       establishment of smallholder nurseries and the development of a national nursery system. The plan

       also notes that where the private sector lacks the capability, the Government and industry should

       provide leadership in the development of effective seed pathways that can supply high-quality seeds

       to small nurseries. MARD has also issued technical guidelines7 on conditions needed for seed

       sources, which specifies technical methods for harvesting propagules and producing seedlings for

       specific species. Vietnam is also exploring high-technology production of quality seedlings—

       including through the use of tissue culture, similar to what is happening in Thailand. There is the

       need to invest in operationalizing these guidelines and upgrading current propagation of trees and

       production of quality seedlings.

   13. There are different suppliers of quality seedlings. Nationally, seedlings are obtained from

       Government forest seedling companies that collaborate with the Forest Science Institute of

       Vietnam. Regionally, they are supplied through Government forest seedling companies, under the

       Forest Science Institute of Vietnam (part of MARD) and seedling production units of the Vietnam

       Forestry Corporation and Vietnam Paper Corporation (part of the Ministry of Industry and Trade). At

       the province level, seedlings are provided through state enterprises, provincial forestry research and

       extension centers, and private enterprises. With regard to species choice, the short-rotation species

       are the most widely available in Vietnam, including acacias. While there are robust reasons for

       considering native tree species, including the greater environmental benefits, they present greater

       challenges with regard to seedling production because of the limited research done on these

       species.

   14. In Vietnam, there is considerable scope for improving seedling production and management of

       seedling nurseries. There is the need to address some of the challenges resulting from limited and

       outdated infrastructure and technical capacity for expanding seedlings production and weak

       management of existing seedling production systems. There also is the need to provide guidance on

       seedling production for a broader range of species, including native species. Improving existing

       seedling production, including the production of native species, would help improve the

       management of coastal protection forests. There also is room for improvements in nursery designs

       including the use of shading systems, seed storage and germination, formulation and sterilization of

       potting mix, measures to prevent root deformation in potted seedlings, watering, nutrition, seedling

       protection, and discarding of overgrown seedlings. There also is the need for the promotion of

       quality control or best management practice within nurseries.

   15. A third constraint is the need for additional sources of financing for managing coastal protection

       forests. The PFES program has been a source of financing for planting forests in Vietnam’s

       watersheds, but similar payments have not been collected for coastal protection forests. The GoV

       has 20 legal instruments—Decrees, Prime Ministerial Decisions, and Circulars—that form the legal

       basis for the PFES implementation. The PFES can be obtained for the protection of natural landscape

       beauty and biodiversity for ecotourism, forest carbon sequestration, and the provision of spawning

       grounds, sources of feeds and natural seeds and use of water from forest for aquaculture. These

       three services can be obtained from coastal protection forests. It, however, has been challenging to

       implement these payments due to limited awareness, discrepancies in the calculation of services,

       and lack of understanding of who should pay for the services.

         hydrological boundary conditions and then using other key parameters, including tidal water levels

         (tidal curves, statistical parameters, and type of tidal water), waves (significant/maximum wave

         height, mean period, and directions), currents (tidal currents and ocean currents), storms

         (frequency, strength, duration, and wind), storm surges (storm surge and water levels), weather

         (temperature and precipitation), morphodynamics (soil types, sediment transport rates, and

   45.   The degree of exposure was a major criterion for determining the necessary interventions and

         structures. Some of the proposed mangrove planting areas on soft and muddy soils are in locations

         that are exposed to the forces of the sea such as waves and tidal currents. In those locations, waves

         and (tidal) currents, during extreme events and during minor storm surges, erode the soil around

         the planted mangroves and limit the success of the planting measures. The more exposed the

         location, the stronger the protecting structure must be. Most of the proposed planting areas on

         sandy soils have an elevation higher than 5 m above sea level or are located on the landward site of

         the coastal dune belt. Here the planting does not require additional structural measures to augment

         survival of the newly planted sandy soil forests. The coastal dunes serve as the primary coastal

         protection systems of those areas and also protect the planting areas.

   46.   The interventions needed in the soft and muddy soil differ from what is needed in the sandy soil.

         In soft and muddy soil areas, proposed structures include hard structures such as breakwaters and

         soft measures such as bamboo fence. Both have been proved effective in Vietnam, in similar coastal

         protection efforts. In sandy soil areas, minor measures of dune protection such as brushwood fences

         to decrease eolian sediment transport for new planting areas will be used. As there will be no

         beachfront planting, no near-shore coastal protection measures are required in sandy soil areas.

       involving coastal forests: (a) revenue generation from the goods produced by coastal forests (for

       example, timber and non-timber, including integrated aquaculture) and (b) revenue generation from

       the services generated by coastal forests (for example, PFES and ecotourism). The grant financing

       can be used to obtain support that would help implement the business plan. This could include

       accessing technological packages, providing skills training, conducting works, receiving technical

       assistance, and so on. The activities supported by the grant must be directly linked to business plan

       implementation. The expenses will need to be eligible expenditures, including advisory services and

       training of members of the community group, goods, materials, and works to improve production

       and productivity and any postharvest processing undertaken as part of the partnership. The role of

       the members of the partnership will be elaborated in a joint business plan.

   57. The competitive grant will be administered by the PPMU and will be similar in approach to the

       productive partnerships model used in the Competitive Agriculture Project in Vietnam. It would

       involve the following steps:

               a. Training of local facilitators (including female facilitators) on how to support community

                    groups to apply for the competitive grants.

               b. Outreach to community groups on the competitive grant process and opportunities. As

                    part of this process, community groups will receive information on the application

                    process and the requirements to submit an expression of interest (EOI). The outreach

                    effort will also facilitate networking that would help bring together community groups

                    and local business (for example, agribusiness) and service providers (both public and

                    private). Special efforts will be made to reach women’s groups.

               c. Launch of the call for proposals for productive partnerships focused on the two

                    categories of income-generating activities mentioned above.

               d. Provision of support, by local facilitators, to communities interested in submitting an

                    EOI. The EOI will follow a template that will require a clear rationale for how the

                    proposed activities will reduce pressure on coastal forests and incentivize their

                    improved management. It will need to delineate the areas that the community groups

                    are managing and the membership of the community group. It will have to describe the

                    proposed activities and how these activities are linked to livelihoods, markets, and

                    private enterprises and help further manage the area. The EOI should also describe

                    briefly any challenges community groups face in the commercialization of their

                    production or adding value. Potential private sector enterprises or target markets

                    should be listed, but groups are not required to have established agreements with

                    enterprises at the time of application.

               e. Creation of a review panel that has representation from all the districts in the province

                    and key stakeholder groups and drafting of guidelines regarding the procedures for

                    selection (including procedures for handling conflict of interest).

               f. Review of proposals against agreed criteria for selection (for both the EOI and full

                    proposal). The criteria for reviewing the EOI and full proposal are elaborated in the POM

                    and include criteria such as alignment with the project objectives, viability (including

                    legal and institutional), and identification of suitable local private sector partner. The

                    criteria for the full proposals are more detailed and include additional criteria such as

   59. Within the targeted districts, investment packages will be available for eligible activities such as

       extensive livestock production. Parties that are interested in applying for the investment packages

       potential areas of support are listed below:

               a. For improved profitability of extensive aquaculture, the support could help increase the

                   productivity of the harvest of wild shellfish. Two strategies to improve the profitability

                   increasing productivity by planting larvae in wild environments and (b) increasing selling

                   price to traders and other purchasers.

               b. For climate-smart intensive aquaculture that reduces the environmental impact of

                   intensive aquaculture and lowers costs for producers, two economically viable strategies

                   could be (a) introducing polyculture aquaculture systems and (b) improving resource

                   use through sensors and information technology.

               c. Promoting safety standards in livestock production, especially pork, to help increase

                   value added for producers. Health and safety standards are a primary concern for pork

                   consumers after several stories of toxic residues found in meat. Consumers are willing

   61. This subcomponent will finance the upgrading of critical infrastructure for production, processing,

       and marketing of goods and services from coastal protection forests. This subcomponent would

       address some of the common critical infrastructure bottlenecks in the production, processing, and

       marketing of goods and services from coastal forests. This could include minor upgrading of rural

       roads or other public productive infrastructure that would increase productivity and market

       efficiency—training centers, piers, signage, and so on (it would not support new infrastructure that

       is for private use such as private processing or private storage facilities). The emphasis would be on

       the support to smaller-scale upgrading works, often at the level of village roads, and often utilizing

       community efforts, backed by project resources and technical support. The funded upgrades to

       infrastructure would complement investments made under other components by improving the

       condition of critical public infrastructure and services. An example of the latter would be through

       the upgrading of training centers at the district level that would support the role of local youth in

       monitoring the health of coastal forests and their benefits for storm protection. Any of the

       investments supported will have to take into account the project-specific recommendations for

       addressing gender issues.

   62. The funds available for productive infrastructure would be used to

                 a. Support investment opportunities through minor upgrading of basic rural infrastructure

                    and services, especially in areas with high potential for benefiting local communities

                    involved in sustainable protection of coastal forests;

                 b. Enhance the economic benefits to the communes that are actively protecting coastal

                    forests by providing jobs that involve a range of skills, especially for young people. The

                    orientation will be toward jobs that are linked to the protection of coastal forest

                    restoration to the extent possible (for example, jobs related to monitoring and analyzing

                    data on coastal forests);

                 c. Contribute to revenue from economic activities (such as tourism) by increasing market

                    access;

                 d. Create opportunities for technology transfer and technical knowledge sharing; and

                 e. Manage any potential negative environmental impacts and ensure the protection of the

                    coastal forests.

   Project Institutional and Implementation Arrangements



   1. The MBFP in MARD has been assigned as the project owner. The MBFP will be responsible for

      monitoring and guiding project implementation, and facilitating coordination among different

      implementing agencies at national and provincial level. The MBFP will assign a CPMU to administer

      the project. Considering the amount of investments occurring at the subnational level, the PPCs will

      assign the provincial DARDs as the project owner for activities at the province, district, and

      commune level. The DARD in each province will, in turn, either establish a new or use an existing

      PPMU to administer the project activities at the provincial level.

   2. The CPMU will be responsible for overall implementation, coordination, reporting, FM,

      procurement, and M&E of the activities that are under the central ministries responsibility. It will

      also coordinate with the PPMUs on activities that are the responsibility of the Provinces. The CPMU

      will be composed of a director, vice director or coordinator, chief accountant, accountant,

      coordinator, planning officer, safeguard policy officers (one of whom will also have the skills in

      gender issues), infrastructure officer, forest officer, M&E officer, driver, and administrative assistant.

      The CPMU will receive technical assistance from a team of consultants on safeguards and M&E.

      They will also assist the CPMU to work with the provinces to implement the project in accordance

      with the project design. The CPMU will access technical specialists from various technical

      departments in MARD including forestry, aquaculture, water, climate change, and environment.

      These specialists will be engaged as needed. For the implementation of Component 1, VNForest will

      provide technical advice to MARD. VNForest will maintain adequate human resources on the project

      team during project implementation, similar to what they did during project preparation. VNForest

      will coordinate with the CPMU and other technical departments and research institutes of MARD to

      implement the planned activities under Component 1.

   3. The CPMU, as part of its coordination with PPMUs, will provide support on procurement, FM,

      M&E, safeguards compliance, and communications. The CPMU will assist the PPMUs ensure their

      procurement and work plans comply with the requirements of the World Bank, before they submit

      these documents to the World Bank. The CPMU’s role is not an approval or no-objection role, and is

      solely focused on providing support to improve quality before submission.

   4. The PPMU will be in charge of day-to-day implementation activities at the provincial level

      (specifically for Components 2 and 3). This will include (a) consolidating the investment plan of the

      province; (b) preparation of detailed technical engineering designs, safeguards documents,

      implementation plans, and the PP; (c) complying with fiduciary (procurement and FM) and

      safeguards requirements at the subproject level; (d) operation and maintenance of the project

      account; and (e) M&E of subproject implementation. Each of the PPMUs will be fully staffed with

      qualified and experienced staff in all the necessary areas, with particular emphasis on fiduciary and

      safeguards aspects. Staff positions in the PPMUs will be similar to the ones found in the CPMU.

   5. While most of procurement, FM, and disbursement tasks will be handled by the PPMUs, a large

      number of activities will be implemented at the district and commune level. A DWG will be

      created with members from district authorities and technical sections working to assist the

   14. Institutional arrangements for procurement. At the national level, MARD is the project line agency,

       The project owners for these subprojects are the eight provincial DARDs. The DARDs will have

       oversight responsibility for the respective subproject implementation. A PPMU has been established

       including procurement and contract management of goods, works, non-consulting services, and

       consulting services that are directly related to their respective subcomponents.

   16. DWGs and CWGs will respectively be established in each of the participating districts and

       communes to facilitate day-to-day implementation. However, apart from identifying their needs as

       input for the PPMUs, the working groups will not be involved directly in procurement activities

       under the project.

   17. PCRA. A PCRA for the CPMU and a sample of the eight PPMUs implementing the project was

       conducted by the World Bank in October 2016 and updated in December 2016 and found that the

       CPMU and the eight PPMUs have procurement capacity in place and considerable experience with

       World Bank procurement under the old guidelines. However, they are not familiar with the World

       Bank’s new Procurement Regulations and have limited contract management experience. The

       assessment identified the following main procurement risks to the project implementation (i) lack of

       clarity in roles, responsibilities and accountability of the many IAs and supervising Ministries’

   Social Safeguards

   29. The project carried out an SA that entailed qualitative/quantitative methods to gather

       information in the project area. These included a socioeconomic survey (August–September, 2016)

       and a qualitative research conducted through in-depth interviews with household representatives

       and officials (provincial, district, and commune levels). The SA provided information on the local

       population profile, their productive activities, public services provision, and current land use.

       Overall, in the project area, agriculture, forestry, and fishery are the key sectors with agriculture

       being the main local income source (56.06 percent), followed by fisheries (31.20 percent) and the

       forestry sector (12.74 percent). According to the SA’s survey data, 47.3 percent of households are

       classified as near-poor or poor households, with an average income of VND 1,902,600 per month,

       lower than the national average registered (2015 data).

   30. According to the findings of the SA, in the communes located in coastal areas, EMs account for

       approximately 22,088 peoples (mostly Thai, Tay, and Dao EM peoples) representing 0.61 percent

       of the total population. EMs are settled mainly in the Quang Ninh and Thanh Hoa coastal districts.

       Results from the SA showed that EM communities living in communes along the coastal line of

       project provinces are settled in the coastal region since 1970–80s. They are bilingual (speaking

       Vietnamese/their own languages) and maintain their own distinct cultural and social characteristics.

       The SA identified potential land impacts as a result of (a) acquisition of agriculture land of 236

       households encroached in the degraded forest used for agricultural production, scattered and with

       small pieces of land (average about 200 m2 per household); (b) land acquisition potentially required

       for repairing and upgrading of small-scale infrastructure works (rehabilitating and upgrading roads

       and dikes, dredging canals and creeks, and repairing culverts under dikes); and (c) potential local

       impact on livelihoods during the transition from agriculture to forest.

   31. Based on the findings of the SA, the project triggered the Indigenous Peoples (OP 4.10) and

       Involuntary Resettlement (OP 4.12) policies.

   32. Indigenous Peoples (OP 4.10). This policy is triggered as there are three EM groups namely Thai,

       Tay, and Dao living in the project areas of Quang Ninh and Thanh Hoa Provinces. As required by

       OP/BP 4.10, an EMPF was prepared. The EMPF provided with the procedures and guidelines for the

       two EMDPs for those to be prepared in the second year of the project implementation stage. A

       comprehensive SA will inform the preparation of the two provinces’ EMDPs. These Plans will reflecti

       the agreements reached during the FPICs with community members and the EMs’ broad community

       support will be confirmed.

   33. Involuntary Resettlement (OP 4.12). The proposed project activities are unlikely to require any

       significant land acquisition and/or physical relocation. Component 2 investments could acquire very

       small areas of garden and crop lands for upgrading of small-scale infrastructure works to

       improve protection of coastal forests, so land impact would be minor and not involve physical or

       economic displacement. The project’s Component 3 supporting the development of coastal forest

       ecosystem services, will support livelihoods improvement in the Quang Tri, Quang Binh, and Thua

       Thien Hue Provinces to those affected by the recent pollution disaster, with potential relocation

       involved. The RPF of the project was hence developed to ensure all PAPs will be compensated at the

       replacement cost and assisted with restoration measures to help them improve or at least sustain

       living conditions of prior-project condition. The RPF will guide the preparation of subproject-specific

       RAPs during the project implementation stage.

       acquisition assessment will be determined during project implementation. Voluntary land

       donations by the beneficiary communities or individuals might occur, when donations are based on

   35.   The proposed project activities may also involve restrictions of access to forests and forest

         products for local communities to coastal protection forests. A PF was prepared in a participatory

         manner to screen and guide procedures on all project activities to identify, assess, minimize, and

         mitigate potential adverse impacts on local livelihoods. The PF sets the conditions under which

         OP/BP 4.12 will be applied in the project; outlines the principles and procedures to be followed if

         negative social impacts occur; and seeks to prevent eligible individuals, households, and

         communities from becoming worse off as a result of the project. The PF also identifies the roles and

         responsibilities and capacity-building and budget requirements to effectively implement these

         mitigation measures. Component 3 is designed to support economic benefits from coastal forest

         ecosystem services through local economic activities such as aquaculture under the forest canopy

         along with sustainable forest management; develop agroforestry models in the coastal forest area

         (including livestock and beekeeping); support the preliminary treatment and processing of fishery

         products to increase revenue generation; and develop the market chains of agriculture, forest, and

         fishery products in the coastal areas). It is anticipated that this component would support

         investment in an integrated system in ecological aquaculture production in the mangroves and

         other activities in the sandy soil forests to increase their added value. It would also aim to support

         livelihood improvement and career change and ensure stable lives for local people in the four

         provinces affected by the recent environmental pollution disaster (Ha Tinh, Quang Tri, Quang Binh,

         establishment of the stable markets for the sale of aquaculture products grown under the mangrove

         canopy and for developing models of ecological tourism along with sustainable coastal forest

         management and protection. As no physical displacement is anticipated, there will be no need to

         relocate villages or households. Consultations conducted during the SA showed a strong interest

         within local communities to undertake comanagement of protected areas with the PFMBs. Specific

         action plans following the procedures specified in the PF were also developed to provide support for

         the development of sustainable or alternative livelihoods.

   44. The project would mainly involve the following physical investments under Components 2 and 3:

       (a) planting and restoration of coastal forests (mangrove and coastal inland protection forest); (b)

       upgrading and repair of coastal dykes and embankments; (c) construction and repair of (forest) rural

       roads and irrigation canals; (d) construction of forestry-related infrastructure and infrastructure to

       help improve monitoring and implementation of protection activities such as forest nurseries,

       watchtowers, and ecological aquaculture facilities; (e) support for extensive aquaculture systems in

       mangrove forests or facilities on marine breeding to improve cultivation techniques toward

       sustainability and maintain or improve productivity, product yield on the basis of forest protection

       and development, and environmental protection; (f) support smart aquaculture systems adapting to

       climate change; (g) support agricultural cultivation systems in NCC provinces such as growing safe

       vegetables, fruit trees, livestock, and poultry; (h) support the production bases producing vegetable

       crops at localities; and (i) support the development of tourism based on environmental protection

       and forest protection in coastal areas. Civil works under Components 1 and 4 will be small including

       very small works associated with nurseries and renovation of offices and/or construction of small

       office buildings.

   45. The overall environmental impacts from the proposed activities are expected to be largely

       positive. The project supports the implementation of good practices for restructuring the forest

       sector, which will result in improved operation effectiveness of forest economic entities, enhanced

       cooperative management and economic linkage models in forestry (for example, sustainable forest

       management certification), and integrated spatial planning for coastal areas. Potential investments

       to increase the mangrove cover are expected to reduce coastal erosion and provide erosion

       protection to farms and villages located in sensitive coastal areas, which contribute to enhancing

       climate resilience. The afforestation and reforestation activities proposed under the project will

       result in carbon sequestration. Financing for sustainable forest enterprises also contributes to

       increasing carbon sequestration, improving plantation design and management, and encouraging

       good forest practices (long-rotation timber plantations and other techniques).

   46. Potential negative environmental and social impacts of the project are expected to be moderate,

       and most of them will be temporary and localized and can be mitigated. Therefore, the project has

       been categorized as a Category B project. The main activities/subprojects that could create negative

       impacts are forest plantation and restoration; construction, rehabilitation, and/or operation of small

       infrastructure works; and support to livelihood improvement.

   47. Potential impacts of plantation and/or protection of coastal forests and mangroves during pre-

       planting and planting phases under Component 2 are considered moderate and could be

       mitigated. These include impacts due to land acquisition and resettlement; relocation of household

       graves; safety risk related to UXOs; and construction-related impacts such as increase in air

       pollution, noise, water pollution, waste generation, traffic safety risk, and disturbances to local

       residents. Road construction, operation, and maintenance activities under the component may

       cause erosion and adversely affect water quality, disrupt subsurface hydrologic flow, and bring

       water to the surface in new areas or destabilize sensitive hill slopes, which may cause slope failures.

       Impacts during the operation stage of the plantation and infrastructure under this component are

   49. Natural Habitats (OP 4.04). The project triggers this policy as it will be implemented in coastal forest

       areas. Afforestation and reforestation activities may have potential impacts on natural habitats if

       poorly planned or implemented. Based on the current project design, it is unlikely that the

       subprojects would significantly convert or degrade natural habitats as the subproject sites are

       generally lands/mangroves and coastal inland protection forests that have already been converted.

       During implementation, when the subproject sites are identified, surveys and studies will be

       undertaken to assess the potential impacts on specific ecosystems. In case it is determined that the

       subproject will involve degradation of the natural habitat, the ESMP will include mitigation

       measures acceptable to the World Bank.

   50. Forests (OP 4.36). The project triggers this policy as it involves coastal forest

       protection/rehabilitation activities aiming to restore coastal landscapes, enhance resilience of inland

       farming systems, and reduce vulnerability to the impacts of sea level rise and coastal erosion.

       Subproject activities will include reforestation, rehabilitation, and planting of mangroves and coastal

       inland forests in targeted areas including construction, upgrading, and/or rehabilitation of small

       infrastructure considered important for increasing the survival rate of young mangroves and

       seedlings. Currently, no specific areas have been identified, but it is expected that the project will

       cover about 69,000 ha scattered across 257 communes in 47 districts of the 8 provinces. Forest

       Management Plans will be prepared following the provisions of the policy with regard to smallholder

       and community forest management for all reforestation, rehabilitation, and planting activities

       undertaken as part of the project and for any other infrastructure and livelihoods (including

       ecotourism) related activities that may affect the forests. Mitigation measures will be put in place to

       protect or rehabilitate the forests and species, especially during construction and operation.

       may involve relocation of graves, which are also considered PCRs, and thus this policy is triggered.

       Some civil works may also include excavation activities, which may result in chance finds. The

       ‘chance find procedures’ will be included in the ESMPs and civil works contracts.

       establishes the requirements for subproject safeguard screening, impact assessment and

       preparation and clearance, and safeguard implementation, supervision, monitoring, and reporting.

       subproject, and they will be included in the BDs and contract documents and will be closely

                 ANNEX 4: FINANCIAL AND ECONOMIC ANALYSIS (INCLUDING GHG ANALYSIS)





            Vietnam: Forest Sector Modernization and Coastal Resilience Enhancement Project



   Economic Analysis



   1. The modernization of the forest sector in the coastal region initiated by Component 1, and the

      improvement of project management and M&E supported by Component 4 will result in

      significant benefits. However, these benefits are mostly intermediate benefits that contribute to

      improving the efficiency and sustainability of the project as a whole and Components 2 and 3 in

      particular. Therefore, these expected benefits are difficult to measure directly and can be seen as

      parts of the benefits of Components 2 and 3.

   2. The expected and measurable benefits of the project will be derived from Components 2 and 3.

   3. The economic analysis examined two scenarios: (a) a without-project scenario, which describes the

      current situation and assumes no interventions; and (b) a with-project scenario. The financial

      analyses were carried out by accounting for direct benefits derived from the project. The economic

      analysis took into account both direct benefits and indirect benefits brought to society by the

      project. In addition, sensitive analyses were also applied for examining the project efficiency against

      risks that may happen.

   4. Assumptions underlying the economic and financial analysis are as follows:

           (a) Discount rate: 5 percent which is proposed by the World Bank



           (b)   Project life span: 31 years (life span of the credit)



           (c)   The values of carbon sequestration: the social value of carbon is US$30 per tCO2e in 2015

                 based on the World Bank guidance for project appraisal, and assumed to remain

                 unchanged in the future



           (d)   Rate of success for coastal and mangrove forest plantation and enrichment: 80 percent



           (e)   Only 50 percent of the project areas of mangroves can provide fishery service and 30

                 percent of the project coastal mangrove areas can be utilized for recreation and tourism



           (f)   Rate of success of implementing C3 livelihood models after the support from the project

                 ended: 30 percent



   5. Summary indicators. Estimations using the aforementioned assumptions indicate that the project is

      both financially and economically feasible. With regard to financial analysis, NPV (5 percent) and

      percent) and EIRR are US$610,474,611 and 57.4 percent, respectively (see table 4.3). The high

      values of the economic indicators are explained by the high values of carbon sequestration provided

      by mangrove and coastal sandy forest.
