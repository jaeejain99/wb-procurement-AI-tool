    I.     STRATEGIC CONTEXT





    A. Country Context



1.      Bangladesh experienced rapid social and economic progress in recent decades, reaching lower middle-income

status in 2015. Stable macroeconomic conditions drove 6.4 percent average annual real Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

growth between 2010 and 2022. Over the same period, poverty and extreme poverty declined by 19.6 and 6.8 percentage

points to 30 percent and 5 percent, respectively. 1 However, the pace of poverty reduction slowed, and urban inequality

has widened.



2.        Bangladesh navigated the COVID-19 pandemic shock with prudent macroeconomic policies, but now faces a

persistent balance of payments deficit and rising inflationary pressure. Import suppression measures narrowed the trade

deficit in the first half of FY24, moving the current account into surplus. However, a financial account deficit has widened,

driven by a contraction in net trade credit and medium and long-term loans. Domestic policies exacerbated the impacts

of external pressure, including the continued use of multiple exchange rates. As of April 2024, gross international reserves

declined to US$ 19.9 billion (3.3 months of prospective imports). Authorities requested support to restore external

balance, including an IMF program approved in January 2023. Inflation remained elevated at 9.8 percent in March 2024,

driven in part by increased administered fuel and electricity prices and depreciation of the taka.



3.      The fiscal deficit stood at 4.4 percent of GDP in FY23. Tax revenues remained among the lowest in the world at

an estimated 7.3 percent of GDP in FY23. Expenditure growth accelerated with higher subsidy spending as a result of

elevated commodity prices, while revenues declined with lower imports. Capital expenditure was led by infrastructure

megaprojects, with rationalization of other projects in FY23 to slow the growth of the budget deficit.



4.      Real GDP growth is expected to decelerate to 5.6 percent in FY24 before returning gradually to its long-term

trend. Inflation is projected to remain elevated in the near term and gradually subside with monetary policy tightening

and import price stabilization over the medium term. External sector pressure will ease gradually, supported by resilient

export growth and ongoing import restrictions. The fiscal deficit is expected to remain within 5.0 percent of GDP, with a

moderate increase in revenues. However, downside risks are rising. Import restrictions may result in disruptive gas and

power shortages that dampen growth. The pace of monetary and exchange rate reforms may be insufficient, leading to

persistent inflation and further depletion of FX reserves. Financial sector vulnerabilities may widen if enhanced asset

measurement, supervision, and enforcement measures are delayed.



5.       Structural reforms are needed to support a faster pace of growth over the medium term. Priorities to reach

upper middle-income status by 2031 include building a competitive business environment, diversifying exports, increasing

human capital, building efficient infrastructure, deepening the financial sector, and attracting private investment. These

reforms will support international competitiveness as Bangladesh prepares for graduation from the Least Developed

Country (LDC) status in 2026, which will gradually lead to reduced concessional financing and preferential market access

for its exports.



6.     Bangladesh is at low risk of overall and external debt distress in the January 2023 joint IMF-World Bank Debt

Sustainability Assessment. Bangladesh is not currently subject to Debt Limits Conditionality under the Sustainable

Development Finance Policy. In its most recent Staff Report, the IMF stressed the need for Bangladesh to accelerate its

7.       Bangladesh faces a high level of vulnerability to the effects of climate change. The Global Climate Risk Index

ranks it as the seventh most affected country in 2000-2019, 2 with high susceptibility to extreme weather events such as

inundated by floods. Addressing these climate risks will support sustainable economic development and prevent

vulnerable populations from being left behind.



 B. Sectoral and Institutional Context



8.      The transport and logistics system of Bangladesh is characterized by high cost, high emissions, high dependency

on roads, and weak multimodal connectivity. It is undermined by a weak policy environment, hampering the efficiency

of customs and border management. Logistic costs are high in most sectors and account for up to 48 percent of sales. The

transport system, which experiences extreme congestion and relies heavily on fossil fuels, contributes to 15 percent of

the country’s greenhouse gas emissions and accounts for 65 percent of fossil fuel consumption according to the

Bangladesh Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR, 2022). These challenges result in high trade costs,

constraining Bangladesh from playing a more active role in regional integration as seen by its low levels of trade with its

neighboring countries. Bangladesh can increase exports by 20 percent along with a corresponding increase in real wages

by reducing logistics costs by 25 percent. 3



9.       Bangladesh’s economic growth significantly depends on its maritime capacity and connectivity. The viability of

the ready-made garments (RMG) sector, which accounts for over 80 percent of the country’s total exports and employs

more than half of its manufacturing workforce, is underpinned by maritime access to its primary markets in Europe and

North America. Substantial efforts to modernize port infrastructure and associated services are thus critical, not only to

sustain the country’s export competitiveness but also to meet its growing need for manufacturing inputs and to satisfy

the rising consumption levels of its population.



10.      Bangladesh’s capacity for international trade depends on Chittagong 4 Port, which faces significant capacity

constraints. Chittagong Port is Bangladesh’s principal seaport and gateway to global trade, handling over 90 percent of

the country’s international trade volume and 98 percent of container traffic. Despite an initial design capacity of only 1.7

million twenty-foot equivalent units (TEUs) annually, the port’s capacity has been continuously stretched, reaching 3.26

million TEUs in 2022. This extreme demand-capacity imbalance has resulted in severe congestion (including high berth

occupancy rates) and operational inefficiencies. As a result, vessels face prolonged anchorage wait times of two to seven

days, unreliable berthing schedules, and average turnaround times of five days, in stark contrast to the two-day average

of Indian ports.



11.    The 2022 Container Port Performance Index (CPPI), which is based on the total port time of container ships,

ranks Chittagong 307 out of 348 ports globally. Without expanding capacity, a significant percentage of exports will not

be able to leave Bangladesh on time to meet delivery requirements, while similar delays in receiving essential

manufacturing imports will disrupt production timelines. Trade and traffic trends suggest that the port’s capacity is

12.     The capacity constraints at Chittagong Port underpin the country’s poor maritime connectivity. As per the Liner

Shipping Index of the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development, Bangladesh’s average 2022 score of 15.4

compares poorly to regional peers, namely Viet Nam (79.8), Sri Lanka (71.4), India (62.6), and Pakistan (36.2), and is in the

same league as countries with only a fraction of its annual trade volume. The low ranking on connectivity is partly

attributable to physical constraints from the port’s location on the tidal reaches of the Karnaphuli River, such as low

draught, tidal limitations, nighttime navigation restrictions, and restrictions on the overall length of vessels. The port can

only receive small feeder vessels during limited hours of the day; nearly 80 percent of the total calls to the port are from

feeder loops. The port’s inability to accommodate larger vessels on a 24/7 basis directly increases time and costs, making

Bangladesh less competitive than countries like India and Sri Lanka that can ship directly to international markets.

However, expanding Chittagong Port’s capacity to accommodate larger vessels at the existing location is not a viable

option due to the significant investments required to overcome its varied natural limitations, such as a shallow bar at the

entrance of the river and restrictive river channel geometry.



13.      In view of the above, developing a deep-sea port at an alternative location in or near Chittagong is a clear and

imperative strategy for Bangladesh to cater to its growing trade volumes in an efficient, cost-effective, safe, and resilient

manner and will bolster regional integration. This strategic investment is crucial for Bangladesh’s economic future and

its position in global trade, promising a significant contribution to national economic strength and global competitiveness.

In addition, with Bangladesh giving access to India to use Chattogram Port for transit and cargo vessels, regional

integration is further advanced. The introduction of a modern and competitive port in or near Chittagong will contribute

to regional cooperation between Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, and Nepal (BBIN) and supports the provision of reliable and

economical transit services for the landlocked northeast of India, Nepal, and Bhutan. Such an undertaking complements

the regional infrastructure and policy improvements under the Accelerating Transport and Trade Connectivity in Eastern

South Asia Program and the Bangladesh national logistics policy work, all of which support regional connectivity in the

BBIN countries.



14.      Beyond cargo handling capacity, the Bangladesh maritime sector is also facing other equally pressing

challenges. To begin with, Bangladesh maritime ports have mostly adopted the traditional ‘tool port’ model, which limits

operational flexibility and efficiency. Until late 2023, Chittagong Port was the only major port in South Asia that had not

adopted a ‘landlord port’ model,5 which is crucial for attracting private investment and improving port operations. Second,

limited hinterland connectivity continues to make imports and exports costly and unreliable. For example, as Bangladesh’s

primary gateway port, Chittagong Port faces increasing challenges such as inadequate road networks, underdeveloped

rail connections with a mode share of only 3 percent, and severe congestion, all contributing to increased logistics costs.

Several World Bank-financed projects support the Government of Bangladesh (GoB) in improving the overall transport

network performance. 6 Finally, the paucity of skilled labor and the limited talent pool to serve the expected exponential

growth in port services impede Chittagong Port’s future growth. The current gap in staff training and professional

development hinders operational efficiency, despite the significant workforce employed at the nation’s ports. Investing in

15.      The GoB’s strategic focus on maritime infrastructure aims to address these challenges through significant

reforms and investments to enhance port capacity, including through private sector participation. The Government’s

‘Vision 2041’ outlines comprehensive plans for the country’s seaports to adapt to changes in the international maritime

sector and includes port capacity expansion, modernization, and increased private sector involvement. The Chittagong

Port Authority (CPA) has initiated steps to update the port tariffs that were last set in 2008. In terms of capacity addition,

the Government’s major initiatives comprise the development of a deep-sea port at Matarbari with a proposed capacity

of 800,000 TEUs (with Japan International Cooperation Agency [JICA] financing), the Patenga Container Terminal (PCT) at

Chittagong with a capacity of 500,000 TEUs, the new Laldia Container Terminal with a proposed capacity of 700,000 TEUs,

and the Bay Terminal at Chittagong with a proposed capacity of 3.6 million TEUs. Even with these new developments, it is

expected that the forecasted Bangladeshi container demand will exceed the planned container capacity by 2039/40.



16.      The Bay Terminal, the GoB’s flagship initiative to expand capacity at the Port of Chittagong, represents a

cornerstone in Bangladesh’s efforts to boost port capacity, operational efficiency, and climate resilience. Located in the

Anandangar/Sandwip channel in the Bay of Bengal, west of the existing Chittagong riverport terminals and close to existing

road and rail links to Dhaka, the Bay Terminal will accommodate larger vessels and improve cargo-handling efficiency. It

comprises a Multi-Purpose Terminal (MPT) to be developed with the possible investment of an international terminal

operator (ITO) and two container terminals to be developed by ITOs under the landlord model. The new terminal is a

significant step toward modernizing the country’s maritime port infrastructure and improving its connectivity to

international markets. It is estimated that the Bay Terminal will potentially have the potential to capture about 36 percent

of Bangladesh’s container volumes from the start of operations.7 The introduction of the Bay Terminal as a modern facility

to be operated by leading ITOs is projected to save the Bangladeshi economy over US$363 million annually by enhancing

efficiency and reducing vessel turnaround times.



17.     The Bay Terminal will be developed as a climate-resilient port to address climate vulnerability and the projected

impacts of climate change on coastal infrastructure. This is in keeping with the Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100, which

emphasizes the need for resilient port infrastructure to mitigate the adverse effects of climate change, ensure the

continuity of operations, and facilitate response to disasters through, for example, the receipt and delivery of emergency

aid. The expected adverse effects of climate change will be mitigated through proper engineering design and disaster

management planning.

30.     The investments outlined in Component 1 are a first step in catalyzing investment in the overall Bay Terminal.

Subcomponent 1(a) supports the construction of a 6 km rubble mound breakwater on an existing sandbar 1.4 km offshore,

topped with X-block-plus armor units. 13 The construction of the breakwater structure will stabilize the offshore sandbar

through concrete piling and the installation of vertical drains. Scour protection measures will be undertaken to enhance

the integrity of the breakwater base and its resistance to erosion and climate-exacerbated risks, ensuring the operational

continuity of the port. Capital dredging supported by Subcomponent 1(b) will deepen the depth alongside the berth to

15.5 m and the entrance channel to 15 m relative to chart datum (CD). The existing depth alongside the berth varies

around 8–9 m CD, and the capital dredging depth in the basin is expected to be 6–7 m below the existing depth with an

expected volume close to 30 million m3. The capital dredging will be 5,000 m long and 600 m wide, and the entrance

channel is around 1.6 km long and 500 m wide. The seabed at the entrance channel is naturally deep as it extends into the

anchorage area and will require less dredging. While maintenance dredging is not covered by IDA financing, initial support

may be provided for sedimentation monitoring to gather essential data for developing a sustainable dredging strategy.

Under Subcomponent 1(c), navigational aids will be installed along the access route to enhance vessel safety,

complemented by necessary updates to the existing VTMS at the Bay Terminal, which may include disaster risk

management and emergency response capabilities.

 F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design



38.     Transitioning to a landlord port model requires continuous support. In other parts of the world, such as Sub-

Saharan Africa in the 2000s, where there had been a lag in the introduction of a landlord port management model, delays

were ascribed to factors such as local labor unions, suspicion of the motives of external partners, opposition to the

introduction of the private sector among public servants, and weak institutional frameworks with little regulatory

capacity.14 The World Bank’s experience in Tanzania and Mauritius underscores that effecting change under such

circumstances requires continuous support. 15 The World Bank will closely support the implementing agency and

continuously monitor potential implementation problems for early intervention, as needed.



39.     IFC’s experience in port sector projects has generated lessons that inform the project design. The main lessons

relevant to the Project are:



            (a)   Port projects are almost always exposed to market risk, and realistic port market projections that fully

                  consider the project location, competing facilities, and how the industry operates are key to success,

                  especially in the development of greenfield ports; and

            (b)   Ensuring political buy-in for a PPP is key to ensuring that governments adhere to the concession agreements.



40.     The World Bank’s close engagement in the ongoing feasibility study update responds to lesson (a) while the

Project’s Support for Port Efficiency Improvements and Capacity Development component responds to lesson (b).



41.     Given the complexity of the larger Bay Terminal development and the involvement of private ITOs, the CPA

needs to be sufficiently staffed. It might need external assistance, such as advisors, throughout the preparation,

procurement, negotiations, and award of large contracts and concessions, as well as during project implementation. The

Project’s implementation arrangement will ensure that the PIU is adequately staffed with the necessary expertise.



42.      The World Bank-financed Chittagong Port Project (P009426), which was approved in 1982 and closed in 1992,

identified that streamlining the GoB decision-making process for procurement would have been more efficient and would

have avoided the five-year delay in project implementation by delegating the authority for project implementation to a

joint committee consisting of selected senior officials of the Ministry of Shipping (MoS) and the CPA. The project’s

institutional arrangements reflect this lesson.

 A. Technical, Economic, and Financial Analysis



Technical



50.      The design of the Bay Terminal Phase one is ongoing (marine port infrastructure, terminals, hinterland

connectivity). A detailed feasibility study for the Bay Terminal was completed in 2017 and has been updated in line with

the updated port layout, bathymetric information, hydrodynamic data, and traffic forecast. This process is supported by

feedback from the World Bank and experts in marine engineering and port operations. The fundamentals of the feasibility

study - the port layout and key issues raised by ITOs such as phasing of the two container terminals and tariff adjustments

to ensure overall project viability - have been finalized and are acceptable. A key input of the technical assessment was

the recently conducted navigation simulation, which confirmed the soundness and operational efficiency of the proposed

port basin layout. The World Bank is satisfied with the marine layout design and overall development plan of the Bay

Terminal.



51.     The IDA-financed port marine infrastructure is strategically positioned behind a natural sandbank. The Bay

Terminal location has been chosen based on the naturally sheltered area provided by the sandbank situated 1.4 km

offshore of the port, creating a natural channel for the port basin. It is also close to the primary road and rail connections

to Dhaka, the country’s main economic center. While deeper draught locations might be available more than 60 km to the

south near Cox’s Bazaar, connectivity to these locations is underdeveloped and plans are already under way for the deep-

sea Matarbari Port development near Cox’s Bazaar. A breakwater is proposed atop the sandbank to bolster its resilience

against climate change-exacerbated risks such as cyclones, storm surges, erosion, and sea-level rise, thus enhancing the

continuity of operations at the port. The breakwater design is currently being refined and is awaiting validation through a

comprehensive series of numerical and physical modeling in a wave tank, which is scheduled for completion by December

2024. This process will ensure a technically robust and optimized design. Engineering and resilience measures are being

considered in the design to further strengthen and stabilize the sandbank, as well as to enhance the resilience of the

breakwater structure. The capital dredging work is being designed to achieve a navigational depth of 15 m CD post

dredging to provide safe and efficient access to the terminal for Panamax container vessels and 75,000 deadweight

tonnage bulker vessels. This will leverage economies of scale by enabling access of larger vessels, which will significantly

boost the efficiency of goods transportation to and from Bangladesh and affirm the Bay Terminal’s role as a key logistical

node in regional and global trade networks. The proposed width of the capital dredging alongside the berths is 600 m, an

increase from the initially proposed width of 300 m, to enable safe navigation and berthing by reducing strong tidal

currents through the basin; which are expected to worsen due to morphological changes of the seabed and exacerbated

by erosion, increased monsoon precipitation, sea level rise, and cyclones.

54.      Assessment of climate mitigation risks. The Project activities are on the list of Universally Aligned transport

activities, as outlined in the Joint Multilateral Development Bank Assessment Framework for Paris Alignment, and the

necessary eligibility conditions are met. The port will not handle or transport coal or other fossil fuels, and there is no

material risk of contributing to deforestation, and there will be no direct impact on mangrove areas. However, indirect

impacts on nearby mangrove areas may occur from associated facilities during port operations, and necessary mitigation

plans will be established as needed. Currently there is no planned oil and gas (O&G) terminal at the Bay Terminal. If it is

considered in the future, additional investment would be needed in the breakwater and access channel to expand the

port. The Project is financially and economically viable without the inclusion of revenues from an O&G terminal, and the

breakwater and access channel financed by IDA will not create any specific favorable conditions for the transport of fossil

fuels as compared to other cargo.



55.     Assessment of climate risks and proposed adaptation measures by the Project. In the absence of adaptation

measures incorporated by the Project, climate and disaster risks likely to affect the Project investments and their

operations are substantial. The sandbank, which provides natural shelter to the port, could experience extreme erosion

due to sea level rise and cyclones. The adaptation measures incorporated through the ongoing design and physical testing

of the breakwater reduce the climate adaptation risk to an acceptable level of residual risk for the achievement of the

PDO. These measures include stabilization of the sandbank, erosion protection, design against sea level rise and storm

surge, enlarged volume of armor layer units of breakwater, and widening of the capital dredged basin to reduce current

flows and related erosion. Risks will be further mitigated through the establishment of disaster risk management practices

in operating procedures, consideration of climate related risks in maintenance practices, and enhancement of institutional

capacity.



Economic Analysis



56.      The Project will deliver a broad spectrum of direct, indirect, and induced economic benefits to Bangladesh and

the South Asia Region. At the micro level, the Project will (a) produce beneficial economies of scale by allowing larger

vessels to berth directly at the new terminals rather than using small feeder vessels or lightering from large vessels to

smaller vessels at the anchorage area for accessing the river port; (b) shorten the turnaround time by about 50 percent

for bulk cargo and for containers by eliminating the necessity for waiting at the anchorage for availability of berths and

right tidal timing and by enhanced stevedoring efficiency; and (c) reduce the carbon footprint of shipping by economies

of scale, reduced lingering time, and improved energy efficiency. At the meso level, the Project and its associated facilities

will (a) contribute to creating job opportunities around the new port during construction and operation and in

Bangladesh’s leading export sector (especially the RMG industry that is currently employing approximately 4 million

people from low-income groups, about 80 percent of whom are female) by reducing logistics cost and enhancing export

competitiveness; (b) solidify Bangladesh’s integration into the global trade network by enabling direct shipping routes for

large vessels to major destination ports in the European Union and East Asia, gaining more business opportunities, and

generating an estimated value addition of 2 percent to the country’s supply chains (equivalent to US$1.2 billion in 2024);

and (c) facilitate technological progress and improvements in the management of Bangladesh’s logistics service sector. At

the macro level, the Project and its associated facilities will (a) unlock the potential for boosting the sustainable growth of

57.      The business-as-usual scenario without the Project. The ‘without the Project’ scenario assumes that the

efficiency, and high berth occupancy.



58.     The investment scenario with the Project. The ‘with the Project’ scenario assumes the development of a

greenfield deep seaport in the Bay of Bengal as enabled by the IDA-financed components for marine infrastructure.



59.      Results of the economic analysis. The economic analysis considers the economic costs that can be expected as

additional costs for developing a deep-sea port, namely the Bay Terminal, 16 and the additional benefits achieved over the

river terminals. Benefits such as reduction in ships’ queuing costs, reduction in ship times at berth, economies of vessel

size resulting from both capital and recurring dredging, and savings in inventory opportunity costs are considered in the

analysis. The economic internal rate of return (EIRR) and net present value (NPV, at a 9 percent discount rate) of the Bay

Terminal are 19.46 percent and US$2,114 million, respectively. A range of sensitivities from risks such as economic

lower-than-expected efficiencies of the private sector, were considered and their impact has been assessed by lowering

benefits by 20 percent and increasing costs by 20 percent. From the combined case where all benefits are reduced by 20

percent and costs increased by 20 percent, the lowest EIRR is 14.27 percent and the lowest NPV is US$1,104 million. In

addition, a scenario was considered where CT 2 (Phase 2) is not developed, after developing the full extent of the

breakwater and other public infrastructure. The EIRR, NPV, and benefit cost ratio, assuming a 5-year construction period

and a 30-year operation period are presented in Table 3. The results indicate that the project is economically viable, and

 VI.     KEY RISKS



82.    The overall risk to the achievement of the Project objectives is Substantial. Project-related risks are discussed

below.



83.      Sector Strategies and Policies risk is rated Substantial. There is a risk of backsliding on the adoption of the

landlord port model and concessioning of the development and operations of CT 1 to the ITO or the failure to meet grantor

obligations under the concession agreements (for example, berth allocation rights and timely tariff adjustments). This risk

is mitigated by (a) the CPA’s recent award of a long-term operations and maintenance concession for the PCT to an ITO,

(b) the progress made by the CPA in negotiating an agreement with a select ITO for CT 1 under a government-to-

government framework, and (c) the disbursement condition in the financing agreement for issuance of the RFP for the CT

1 concession.



84.      Technical Design risk is rated High. This is Bangladesh’s first large-scale maritime port project with unique

technical design risks. The boundary conditions of the planned Bay Terminal come with several technical challenges, which

are quite special and severe in view of the specific geographical location of the site. These conditions contribute to high

tidal ranges and related strong tidal currents in the Bay of Bengal, in addition to potential cyclones and tsunamis. As such,

proper master planning and design are required to ensure that the development of the Bay Terminal is technically sound,

economically viable, and socially and environmentally acceptable.



85.      The World Bank’s technical experts are supporting the CPA in reviewing inputs to project documentation to ensure

that the designs follow international design standards for safety and efficiency. Inputs are sought from private investors

to better inform the proposed design solutions. To mitigate the risks of poor construction quality and works not following

plans and specifications, the design firms currently hired for the marine and landside infrastructure design will extend

their services to supervise the works. The works contracts will be carefully structured to minimize interface risks between

the breakwater contractor, the dredging contractor, and the contractors for complementary investments. Climate-

resilient design solutions will be incorporated in the technical design in collaboration with the Global Center on

Adaptation.



86.       Institutional Capacity for Implementation and Sustainability risk is rated Substantial. An understaffed PIU and

slow decision-making in hiring the required consultants could delay the implementation of the Project. These risks will be

mitigated by ensuring that (a) PIU staff are in place and the POM is prepared and adopted before credit effectiveness; (b)

project management consultancy services or a civil works supervision engineer are in place before the commencement of

works; and (c) a capacity building program is implemented for landlord port management. The Global Infrastructure

Facility is being engaged for potential support to strengthen the CPA team in the RFP process for reaching an agreement

with the ITOs on the terms and conditions of the concessions.



87.     Fiduciary risk is rated Substantial. FM risk and procurement risk are both rated Substantial. The principal risks

and the corresponding mitigation measures are highlighted in paragraphs 67 and 72.



88.   Environment and Social risk is rated High. The E&S risks and impacts of the Project and the corresponding risk

management measures are highlighted in paragraphs 74–78.



89.      Stakeholders risk is rated Substantial. The Project involves a broad range of stakeholders at the international

level (ITOs) and at the national level (CPA, the MoS, Bangladesh PPP Authority, different contractors, and project-affected

persons [PAPs]). Financing sources are different for each investment and require good coordination to ensure timely

                               ANNEX 1: Implementation Arrangements and Support Plan



1.       Organization structure of the PIU. The PIU will be headed by a project director who will directly report to the

chairman of CPA and will comprise of two deputy project directors: one in charge of the implementation of the breakwater

and procurement activities and the other in charge of access channel and port basin dredging activities. The PIU will also

include (a) one hydrographer who will support the implementation of dredging activities and supply and installation of

aids to navigation; (b) one assistant engineer who will support the implementation of the breakwater; (c) one monitoring

and evaluation officer; (d) one account officer; (e) one programming and appraisal officer; (f) one sub-assistant engineer;

(g) one social expert; and (h) one environmental specialist. The social and environmental experts along with the E&S

consultant will ensure satisfactory implementation of project activities in line with World Bank E&S requirements. The

CPA engineering and hydrography team, headed by the Member Engineering and Member Hydrography will review

technical designs and provide technical inputs for the recruitment of contractors for public works.



2.       The ESMP will be an integral part of the bid and contract documents for each civil works contract, and the bids

will include a detailed E&S mitigation budget as part of the costs. The contractors will prepare site-specific ESMPs and

subplans identified in the ESCP and will be responsible for the implementation of, and compliance with, the ESMPs; the

contractors will hire environmental specialist(s) and Health and Safety Officer(s)/Specialist(s) for this purpose. The

supervision consultant/independent engineer will ensure implementation and monitoring of the ESMP by (a) reviewing

site-specific ESMPs and developing good practices to assist contractors in their implementation; (b) ensuring that

contractors comply with ESMP requirements and that construction activities are environmentally sound and sustainable;

and (c) preparing and submitting regular environmental progress reports, including details of noncompliance and

rectification by contractors, as well as the results of monitoring activities.



3.       Resettlement/compensation. The PD will be responsible for the implementation of the RAP and interagency

coordination, including grievance redress. He/she will be assisted by a social development/resettlement specialist. A land

acquisition officer and additional representatives, if deemed necessary, will acquire the land. An RAP Coordination

Committee, which will include the PD and the land acquisition officer, will assist in RAP implementation, and ensure that

all agencies involved are fully informed on the RAP. The social development/resettlement specialist will (a) review and

verify internal monitoring reports; (b) identify and select impact indicators; (c) conduct an impact assessment through

formal and informal surveys with PAPs; (d) consult with the PAPs and the CPA in preparing review reports; and (e) assess

resettlement efficiency, effectiveness, impact, and sustainability. Monitoring will focus on the status of project-affected

vulnerable groups. Indicators are expected to include (a) the socioeconomic conditions of PAPs; (b) reactions from PAPs

on entitlements and compensation; (c) changes in housing and business restoration; (d) grievance procedures, including

recording, processing time, and redress; (e) progress of community development schemes; (f) disbursement of

compensation; and (g) satisfaction of PAPs in the post-resettlement period. An independent agency (a nongovernmental

organization, an academic institute, or a consultant) will conduct periodic monitoring and evaluation and third-party

validation of RAP activities.



4.      An SEA/SH GRM will be established locally at the project site as a corresponding body to the overall project GRM

using the existing framework to allow for prompt, safe, and ethical handling of and response to GBV/SEA/SH cases in a

survivor-centric approach. A GBV focal point at the project level will be identified within the Grievance Redress Committee,

who will respond to all GBV/SEA/SH cases incurred by the project. The focal point will link with and refer cases to existing

local GBV services.

Financial Management



18.     Planning and budgeting. An FM specialist and an accounts officer will carry out day-to-day FM functions. The

relevant expenditures under the proposed Project will be segregated as specified in the Government’s Development

Project Proposal. Budget preparation and execution will take place electronically using the GoB’s integrated budgetary

and accounting system (iBAS++), and the budget must be released through the system in a timely manner for the PIU to

execute project activities according to the budget allocation. The counterpart funding contribution will flow to the Project

as parallel co-financing. The Project will use separate fund codes (Budget and Accounting Classification System) for each

source of financing, and expenditure to be financed under each source will be earmarked in the planning document to

avoid double-dipping while approving the payments for contractor’s invoices during implementation. The detailed

procedures will be included in the FM section of the Project Operations Manual.



19.      Eligibility of financing. IDA financing of US$650 million will flow to the Project at 100 percent in the beginning;

however, the financing gap funding, when materialized, will flow to the Project as joint co-financing at the ratio applicable

at the time of its commitment. The incurred costs will be adjusted at the agreed ratio in the amended financing agreement.

All incremental operating costs will be funded on parallel basis by the counterpart. World Bank financing will also not

cover expenditures related to vehicles, while taxes will be allowable up to 15 percent of the total IDA credit. All value

added taxes and customs duties will be financed by the counterpart. In view of two sources of financing, disbursements

would first commence under IDA 7604-BD.



20.     Funds flow and disbursement. The preferred method of disbursement will be the special commitment and direct

payment options. A Designated Account (DA), in the form of Convertible Taka Special Account, may be opened with a

national commercial bank to receive IDA funds for project implementation, if required after signing. IDA funds will flow

based on withdrawal applications submitted by the Government to the World Bank. The PIU will be responsible for

submitting disbursement applications to the World Bank, which will be based on Statements of Expenditure. Since there

are three sources of financing, disbursement would first commence under IDA 7604-BD, then under IDA 7605-BD and then

under IDA 7606-BD.



21.     Financial reporting. The PIU will prepare an IUFR on a quarterly basis in the format agreed with the World Bank

and submit it to the World Bank for its review and clearance within 45 days from the end of each quarter. The project will

use and mainstream Special Account Management Portal (that is, iBAS++ - SAMP) administered by the Finance Division

for processing all Project transactions, accounting, financial reporting, and monitoring of execution.



22.     External audit. The FAPAD will express an opinion on the project financial statements in accordance with

International Standards of Auditing and submit the report within six months of the end of each financial year. In addition,

the auditor is required to provide a management letter containing the auditor’s observations on internal controls and

compliance with the financial covenants as laid out in the Financing Agreement. There is no pending audit report for any

of the projects funded by IDA under the MoS, nor for the CPA.
